# Final Audit of the 1972 CIA Laos Narcotics Investigation

## File located

CIA FOIA document **CIA-RDP74B00415R000400020027-3**, dated September 29, 1972, is a one-page transmittal. It says the Inspector General report was attached, but the released digital object does not contain the attachment. No public annexes, interview list, field notes, interview memoranda, or exhibits were located.

The most detailed accessible official account remains the Church Committee's Air America chapter in *Foreign and Military Intelligence, Book I*, PDF pp. 12–17 / printed pp. 228–233. It quotes and summarizes the IG investigation.

## Method the accessible record describes

- Headquarters investigators interviewed roughly a score of officers.
- From August 24 to September 10, 1972, investigators visited Hong Kong and eleven Agency facilities.
- More than 100 representatives of CIA, State, AID, BNDD, Customs, the Army, Air America, and a cooperating carrier were consulted.
- The summary does not disclose names, selection rules, interview memoranda, questions, tested definitions, or the complete documentary corpus.

## What the IG ruled out

- No institutional CIA policy to traffic narcotics.
- No policy by senior Air America management to permit trafficking.
- No evidence then found that CIA staff or contract personnel participated in trafficking.
- No basis for claiming that every occasional opium load reflected knowing pilot participation.

These are important official findings, but they are narrower than “opium never moved through U.S.-supported systems” or “CIA never knowingly tolerated narcotics-connected allies.”

## What the IG conceded

- CIA had agents and liaison relationships involving known or suspected narcotics connections.
- The station did not try to suppress civilian opium use in areas under its influence when doing so could damage cooperation.
- It did not stop movement from hill regions toward southern cities.
- The war had overriding priority.
- A 1966–68 case officer recalled orders not to become deeply involved because narcotics enforcement could sour relations with indigenous military counterparts.
- Air America's rule prohibited narcotics, but chaotic loading, customers, indigenous assistants, baggage handlers, and incomplete control meant opium occasionally got aboard.
- Some employees were dismissed on suspicion.
- High-ranking Lao officials were connected to commercial carriers suspected of narcotics activity.
- Continued support to suspect agents could create the appearance that the Agency was unconcerned; the station was asked to reassess those relationships.

## What the public record does not let us test

- Who was interviewed and who was excluded.
- Whether witnesses included AID officers, individual pilots, kickers/loaders, Hmong or Lao sources, Vang Pao, Ouane Rattikone, or Thao Ma.
- Whether investigators reviewed flight manifests, cargo/loading logs, USAID contracts, XKAT records, Long Tieng financial records, or McCoy's underlying interviews.
- The operational definitions of “involvement,” “trafficking,” and “knowledge.”
- Whether absence of proof meant evidence was tested and rejected or records were unavailable.

## Audit verdict

**OFFICIAL POSITION plus DOCUMENTED CONCESSIONS.** The policy-level exoneration does not erase the report's findings of occasional carriage, incomplete control, narcotics-connected relationships, continued support, and deliberate non-suppression driven by war priorities. The complete investigation file remains a real source-quality gap, but not a break in the functional non-suppression chain.
