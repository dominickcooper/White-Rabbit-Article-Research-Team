# PART 3 BRAZIL / SOUTHERN CONE DRUG CHAIN

# CHAIN ESTABLISHED

The local record establishes a U.S.-supported regional security architecture, not a completed cocaine pipeline:

1. U.S. policy sought Brazilian political alignment and conducted covert action before the 1964 coup.
2. AID/OPS assistance modernized Brazilian police communications, records, mobility, laboratories, training, and riot/internal-security capacity.
3. Brazilian security services later participated in repression and regional intelligence cooperation; U.S. officials received contemporary reports of torture, death squads, and abuse.
4. Southern Cone anti-Communist security and exile networks overlapped historically with arms trafficking, clandestine finance, and—in some countries and episodes—cocaine actors.
5. *Cocaine Politics* documents regional mechanisms around Bolivia, Argentina, Paraguay, and Contra-era Central America, including the 1987 São Paulo arrest of Argentine operative Emilio/“Mingolla” with 357 kilograms of cocaine (name spelling requires source-image verification).

# CHAIN PARTIALLY ESTABLISHED

**U.S.-supported security/intelligence system → aligned actor:** DOCUMENTED in Brazil and regionally.  
**Aligned actor → narcotics network:** CORROBORATED for some Southern Cone/anti-Communist figures outside Brazil; POSSIBLE for Brazilian nodes.  
**Narcotics network → transport/finance:** CORROBORATED in Bolivian/Paraguayan/Argentine and later Contra-related networks.  
**Transport/finance → United States:** DOCUMENTED for several regional cocaine networks, but not continuously connected to a named CIA-backed Brazilian institution in the acquired records.  
**CIA knowledge/protection of the continuous chain:** INSUFFICIENT.

The Huanchaca complex near the Bolivia–Brazil border is a promising regional lead. Scott recounts claims that it financed Contra activity and that Roberto Suárez's son alleged DEA control. Those propositions rest on press/witness material and were not adopted by the Kerry report; they require the cited *Latin American Weekly*, *El País*, *Hoy*, Federico Aguiló, Paraguayan, DEA, and congressional records before escalation.

# BROKEN LINKS

- No acquired document identifies a CIA-backed Brazilian police/military official who protected a specified cocaine shipment to the United States.
- No local CIA station or DEA file supplies a dated allegation-and-response chain for a Brazilian asset.
- No manifest, port record, bank trail, or prosecution file connects OPS-supported infrastructure to a named U.S.-bound load.
- Huggins cannot presently fill the gap because the local book ends around printed p. 44, before the later Brazil/region chapters needed for this question.
- The Brazilian Truth Commission proves repression and institutional responsibility, not narcotics facilitation.
- Operation Condor's clandestine communications and cross-border protection establish capability and impunity, but capability is not proof of cocaine use.

# MOST PROMISING NAMES

- **Roberto Suárez / network:** Bolivian cocaine-finance hub; investigate Huanchaca, anti-Communist and security-service ties.
- **Emilio/“Mingolla” (spelling to verify):** Argentine anti-Communist operative arrested in São Paulo with a major cocaine load.
- **Leif Rasmussen:** Paraguay/Huanchaca lead identified through Scott's notes.
- **Luis Arce Gómez and Klaus Barbie:** Bolivia's cocaine-coup/security nexus; relevant to the regional model, not automatically to Brazil.
- **Sérgio Fleury and death-squad networks:** documented Brazilian repression/corruption context; a narcotics protection chain remains to be proved.
- **Paraguayan military/customs and Stroessner-era aviation/contraband figures:** plausible transit mechanism requiring primary records.

# MOST PROMISING DOCUMENTS

- DEA/Customs case files and Brazilian arrest/prosecution records for the 357-kilogram São Paulo seizure.
- Full Huggins chapters on Brazil and regional policing.
- Primary Huanchaca investigation files, Bolivian congressional/judicial records, and the cited contemporary press.
- Operation Condor case files that identify finance, aviation, customs, or contraband activity rather than only political repression.
- CIA/State station traffic and DEA country-office reporting on named Brazilian/Paraguayan assets.
- Bank, shell-company, aircraft-registration, and port/customs records linking a protected actor to a U.S. destination.

## Current verdict

Brazil belongs in Article 3 as a **contrast and an unresolved regional mechanism**, not as a forced replica of Laos or Nicaragua. U.S.-supported security capacity and documented repression form the first half of a potential chain. Regional cocaine/security overlaps form pieces of the second. The bridge between them is not yet established. Publication language should say the investigation found a platform capable of protection and cross-border coordination, but not a proved Brazilian U.S.-bound drug pipeline.
