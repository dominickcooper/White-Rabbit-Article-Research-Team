# USAID, the CIA, and the Pipelines That Met the Drug War

In 1972, a CIA inspector-general inquiry went looking for evidence that **Air America** was carrying opium out of Laos. Its answer sounded reassuring: investigators found no corporate narcotics policy, no order from senior management, and no proof that American pilots knowingly transported the drug.

Then came the admissions.

The inquiry found that opium could—and sometimes did—get aboard. Air America did not fully control what local passengers loaded. The CIA knew that allied figures and territory were entangled with narcotics, yet suppressing that trade ranked below the war. The denial ruled out the cleanest version of a conspiracy and exposed a messier question: *What happens when the people, aircraft and institutions needed for an American operation are also embedded in a narcotics economy?*

This article follows that question from mountain villages in Laos to police classrooms in Washington, from a disputed C-123 to the Contra supply network, and finally to the moments when law enforcement threatened relationships Washington still wanted.

## THE DENIAL THAT OPENED THE DOOR

The official inquiry deserves to be read in both directions. Its negative findings are substantial. It found no evidence that Air America adopted a trafficking policy, that senior officers ordered narcotics carriage, or that American pilots knowingly participated. Those findings weigh directly against the claim that the airline operated as a centrally managed CIA drug service.

But the same record described incomplete controls over locally loaded cargo and acknowledged occasional carriage. It also placed CIA officers amid allied commanders and territories implicated in the opium economy. The Agency's later official history of Laos supplied the priority decision: confronting those allies over narcotics risked the war effort, and the war came first.

[IMAGE: Split documentary panel from the Senate summary of the CIA inquiry—no corporate policy or senior order on one side; incomplete loading control, occasional carriage and wartime non-suppression on the other | ALT: Senate inquiry findings separating Air America policy denials from concessions about loading controls and opium carriage]

That leaves an important asymmetry. The record is strong against a headquarters order. It is also strong that CIA knew enough to recognize the narcotics problem and chose not to make suppression a condition of alliance. Policy, knowledge and tolerance are three different findings. The first was rejected; the latter two survived.

The denial did not close the case. It changed the case.

## FOLLOW THE PIPELINES

“Pipeline” can be a dangerous word. It suggests one conveyor belt, one command center and one cargo. The documents show something less tidy: people moved through training programs; biographies and intelligence moved through liaison channels; food, medicine, arms and money moved toward allies; aircraft and roads connected isolated territory; narcotics traveled through parts of the same geography.

These systems touched without becoming interchangeable. **USAID was not CIA.** A police academy was not an airline, and a road project was not a narcotics shipment. But institutions created access, logistics created capacity, and alliances determined which problems received enforcement pressure and which were tolerated. Across Laos and selected Contra cases, U.S. aid and covert operations built transport, access and security relationships around allies operating inside narcotics economies. When narcotics risks became known, strategic utility sometimes took priority over reporting or enforcement. That is a narrower charge than a CIA-owned cartel—and a better-supported one.

## BEFORE THE DRUGS CAME THE FILES

Part 2 established the architecture before narcotics control became central to this story. The **Office of Public Safety** and **International Police Academy** offered an overt route for foreign-police training. Behind it sat concealed CIA junctions, including International Police Services, where selected officers could receive follow-on training without knowing the facility was CIA-controlled.

A CIA Police Group memorandum described daily liaison with OPS and IPA, exchanges of participant information, placement of some CIA-sponsored officers, and additional briefings or training for selected visitors. The Pike investigation described instructors assessing pro-American orientation and sending names and biographies to CIA components for possible operational use.

The records stop at spotting, assessment and access. They do not identify the Lao narcotics trainees who appear later in this article as CIA recruits, nor do they show every academy student passing through a covert program. What they establish is an institutional network already capable of connecting foreign police, U.S. trainers and intelligence interests before drug enforcement entered the classroom.

## THE GOLDEN TRIANGLE WAS ALREADY THERE

Northern Laos did not wait for Americans to invent opium. Mountain communities cultivated a portable cash crop in terrain where roads, buyers and alternative markets were scarce. Chinese networks, merchants, soldiers and officials were part of a regional trade that predated the U.S. covert war.

The investigative question is therefore not whether America created the Golden Triangle. It did not. The question is what changed when American relief, aviation and military systems were built through the same valleys.

### Pop Buell Tries to Replace the Poppy

The most revealing account begins with **Edgar “Pop” Buell**, the aid worker who became a legendary figure among Hmong communities. In Don Schanche's telling, Buell first tried to replace poppy cultivation with sweet potatoes. The crop grew. The development theory did not.

Villagers lacked transport and dependable buyers. Sweet potatoes spoiled; opium did not. A small amount of opium could be carried across difficult terrain and exchanged for cash in a way bulky food crops could not. In Schanche's account, substitution failed because the surrounding economy offered no workable substitute market.

### “If You’re Gonna Grow It, Grow It Good”

Buell changed tactics. Schanche attributes to him the line: **“If you're gonna grow it, grow it good.”** It was not an institutional USAID directive or an archival transcript. It was Schanche's account of a relief worker who believed immediate eradication would impoverish the villages and who also tried to discourage local smoking.

Schanche added an uncomfortable economic turn. American medicines reduced some of the medicinal reasons villagers consumed opium, while production continued to rise. If less of the crop was used locally, more could be available for sale. That surplus is an inference from the sequence, not a measured USAID study, but the basic problem is plain: the crop remained, and the need for a buyer remained with it.

### The Buyer Changed

At Phou Vieng, Schanche recounts, a deteriorating crop presented an immediate crisis. **Blea Vu** and Buell discussed selling it to the Pathet Lao. Fourteen ponies carried the opium out. The villagers were paid in counterfeit kip.

Phou Vieng stopped dealing with the Communist buyers, but Schanche wrote that other communities continued. Later he observed that Communist buying disappeared in areas served by Air America. The crop had not vanished. The old market had.

What replaced it? Schanche believed CIA-controlled transport supplied the missing outlet, but he marked the boundary himself: **“one can only speculate about this.”** He had an observed economic sequence and an explanation, not a CIA transport ledger.

[IMAGE: Buell–Schanche economic-flow graphic separating observed events from inference: failed sweet-potato market, cultivation advice, reduced medicinal use, continued production, Communist buyers, reported buyer change, speculative replacement outlet | ALT: Flowchart distinguishing Schanche's documented narrative from his speculation about the replacement opium market]

That distinction gives the episode its force. Schanche also described Buell refusing to fly villagers' opium. The evidence does not turn Buell into a courier. It shows a relief program adapting to a crop economy it could not displace while a wartime transport system changed the market around it.

## HOW THE OPIUM WAS ACTUALLY COLLECTED

The mountain trade was not an abstraction. McCoy's interviews describe buyers, prices, packing methods and seasonal routines. The physical detail is what makes the later arrival of helicopters matter.

### Before the Helicopters

In the Long Pot area, McCoy reported that Hmong officers entered villages to test and purchase opium. He put their price at roughly **$60 per kilogram**, compared with **$40 to $50** offered by other merchants. The purchased opium went into bamboo containers, onto ponies and toward Long Tieng. Collection circuits could repeat two or three times during a season.

Those figures come through McCoy's published interviews, not an audited market ledger. Their value lies in the mechanics: village-to-village buying, consolidation and pack-animal movement linked producing communities to the military center.

### Then the Helicopters Arrived

**Ger Su Yang**, a village leader interviewed by McCoy in 1971, described a change in 1969 and 1970. The officers no longer needed to ride the whole route. They arrived by helicopter, spent days moving among villages, then radioed Long Tieng for pickup.

McCoy recorded him saying that **“Meo officers with three or four stripes ... came from Long Tieng to buy our opium. They came in American helicopters.”** A headman from Nam Ou gave McCoy a similar account. Both traced the buyers' origin and return toward Long Tieng.

Ger Su Yang went further: **“the helicopter pilots were always Americans.”** Nationality is not knowledge. His account identifies who he believed flew the aircraft; it does not establish that those pilots knew what every passenger carried. That is precisely where the official inquiry's pilot-knowledge limit remains important.

### The View From the Top

McCoy also interviewed senior Lao military figures. **General Ouane Rattikone** told him that opium had been dropped into the Gulf of Siam for pickup by fishing boats and described attempts to secure military C-47 capacity. Former Royal Lao Air Force commander **Thao Ma** separately recounted being offered wealth if he would allocate two C-47s; he said he refused.

These were interviews, not official adjudications. They do not amount to a senior-command confession that CIA directed a drug pipeline. They do show military aviation being used or sought for opium movement in the accounts of men positioned near the top of the system.

The village view and the command view meet at one place: access to transport.

## THE AIR SYSTEM

### Air America

Air America was critical to the CIA-backed war in Laos and materially sustained by AID contracting and assignments. Its aircraft carried food, medicine, weapons, personnel and other cargo through a country where roads were scarce and the war was officially concealed. That made the airline indispensable. It did not make every flight covert or every load narcotics-related.

The official inquiry found incomplete control over passenger cargo and conceded occasional opium carriage. McCoy and other published accounts went further, describing particular loads and practices. The official record remains stronger on the system-level problem than on any one flight: locally loaded cargo could escape full inspection, CIA knew the narcotics economy surrounded its allies, and the operation continued.

### Xieng Khouang Air Transport

Published accounts place **Xieng Khouang Air Transport**, or XKAT, inside the U.S.-backed wartime environment and describe a Long Tieng–Vientiane route. McCoy identified Lo Kham Thy as president and discussed the company's relationship to Vang Pao, but the acquired record does not include the corporate, ownership and aircraft files needed for a clean institutional history.

That gap is even wider for narcotics cargo. Allegations exist in McCoy's interviews, but the locked evidence package does not support presenting XKAT as a proved CIA or USAID narcotics airline. Its careful role in this story is logistical: another aviation channel appeared inside the same wartime geography, while its precise ownership and cargo record remain incomplete.

### Mae Salong: Roads Into an Opium Economy

The same discipline applies on the ground. U.S.-supported road construction increased mobility and commerce around Mae Salong, a KMT stronghold inside a regional narcotics economy. A road can change who reaches a market and what volume a region can move. It does not reveal the intention behind every construction decision or identify the cargo in every truck.

Regional KMT and opium-tonnage estimates describe the wider network. They cannot be assigned wholesale to one American-built road. Air America, XKAT, AID road construction and KMT commerce were related by geography and war, not one interchangeable transport organization.

## THE PIPELINE REACHES THE UNITED STATES

By the early 1970s, the downstream market was not theoretical. Official reporting connected Golden Triangle production to South Vietnam, the GI heroin crisis and wider international and American markets. The broad chain—production, regional movement, refining and overseas demand—is well established.

The named cases are less secure and should be read for what they are: McCoy's published accounts. He reported a **15.5-kilogram** Vientiane-to-New York case, a **7.7-kilogram** postal seizure at Fort Monmouth connected to arrivals from Laos, and the **60-kilogram** Sopsaisana seizure at Orly. For the Orly load, McCoy attributed the alleged Long Tieng origin to diplomatic sources; Sopsaisana denied ownership.

Those episodes show an American and international endpoint, but they do not trace one sack from a Hmong village through a specific aircraft, refinery, courier and U.S. dealer. The evidence is strongest at separate links. The missing continuous shipment is not a minor footnote—it is the line between a documented regional system and a story tidied into one seamless conspiracy.

That still leaves a policy problem. Washington was supporting a war inside the producing region while confronting heroin among American servicemen and at home. One answer was enforcement training. That route led back to the International Police Academy.

## THE POLICE ACADEMY REAPPEARS—THIS TIME IN THE DRUG WAR

The documentary hinge is an August 1974 Embassy program record from Vientiane. It describes an effort to create narcotics-control capacity inside the Royal Lao Army's military-police system—and says plainly that part of the problem was trafficking by Royal Lao Army officials using military transportation.

### 163 Lao Military Policemen

The Provost Marshal General's criminal and investigation division had been reorganized to include a narcotics-control section. According to the Embassy, **163 military policemen** had received initial training. USAID narcotics advisers conducted seminars, and formal classes were held at the Lao National Police Academy.

The program's objective stated that it **“will eliminate narcotics trafficking by Royal Lao Army officials, use of military transportation for such trafficking.”** That sentence captures the institutional contradiction without requiring embellishment: one part of the U.S.-supported Lao security system was being trained to suppress narcotics activity associated with other military personnel and transport.

The record does not identify the 163 men, and it does not say USAID knowingly trained traffickers. It describes an enforcement unit aimed at misconduct inside the military environment.

### Back to Washington

The Embassy reported that **seven Lao military police** had already received narcotics training in the United States. A special English-language program prepared participant trainees for possible U.S. courses. The listed destinations included the U.S. Army Military Police School, the International Police Academy and the DEA Training School.

The document does not allocate the seven by school. It cannot tell us that all seven attended IPA, much less that any entered the CIA assessment channels described in Part 2. It does show how local military-police training connected to U.S. institutions just as responsibility was scheduled to pass to DEA advisers at the end of fiscal 1975.

### Narcotics Orientation Course No. 8

A second original document brings Laos and Thailand into the academy itself. The January 1975 *Police Academy Review* covered a **December 20, 1974** graduation that included **PMNO No. 8**. The publication lists **27 classmates from nine countries**, including Laos and Thailand, and reports a cumulative academy total of **5,204 graduates from 77 countries**.

At commencement, Major **George Noujaim** declared: **“In the field of fighting crime and in the struggle against the illicit drug traffic, the United States plays a most important role.”** That was Noujaim's argument for international police cooperation, not an independent finding about where the drug trade originated.

The issue does not contain the previously attributed language about Southeast Asian opium, morphine base, heroin or transshipment to the United States. Nor does it print a complete student roster. What it proves is narrower and useful: a multinational narcotics-oriented cohort including Laos and Thailand passed through the Washington academy at the end of 1974.

## THE ACADEMY WAS BUILDING A NETWORK

### The Federal Bureau of Narcotics Was Already Inside

The relationship did not begin with that class. Treasury reports show the **Federal Bureau of Narcotics**, IPA and AID cooperating on narcotics instruction across three consecutive fiscal years.

In **FY1966**, FBN ran a two-week Spanish-language course for **19 Latin American officers**, covering cocaine, heroin and marijuana. The **FY1967** report documented another two-week narcotics-control course and continuing FBN lectures, but did not repeat the 19-officer count. In **FY1968**, bilingual agents again taught a two-week course and provided regular instruction. Three annual records establish recurrence without pretending every cohort had the same size or syllabus.

### Alumni Became the Contacts

A 1972 Justice Department report described the scale reached by then: **3,932 IPA graduates from 73 countries**, with graduates commanding **13 national police agencies**. It also described specialized narcotics-enforcement training and explained the operational value of the network.

IPA graduates and OPS advisers, the report said, could **“serve as valuable contacts overseas for Department of Justice representatives when needed.”** The last six words control the claim. DOJ described contacts for its representatives—not a roster of CIA assets.

Part 2 separately established that CIA's Police Group maintained daily liaison, exchanged participant information and sponsored selected officers. Those facts sit beside the DOJ network; they do not fuse with it. No record identifies the narcotics cohorts here as the people recruited or sponsored there.

### Thailand and PARU

The Thai record makes the agency boundary even sharper. CIA official history and Bill Lair's retrospective account describe **CIA's direct role in developing PARU**, whose personnel trained Hmong forces and provided communications support in Laos. Lair distinguished that channel from **USAID Public Safety advice to most other Thai police organizations**.

No named IPA graduate has been traced from the academy into PARU and onward to Long Tieng. The institutional systems operated near one another, but the person-level bridge remains missing.

## WHEN CONGRESS CLOSED ONE DOOR, NARCOTICS LEFT ANOTHER OPEN

Congress enacted **Section 660** on **December 30, 1974**, as part of Public Law 93-559. The restriction became effective on **July 1, 1975**. It broadly barred Foreign Assistance Act funds and related local currencies from supporting foreign police, prisons, other law-enforcement forces and internal-intelligence or surveillance programs. The exceptions were present at enactment: the statute preserved FAA Section 482 international narcotics-control assistance and specified activities relating to DEA and FBI authority over conduct unlawful in the United States. Other agencies could also operate under distinct appropriations and authorities.

[IMAGE: Section 660 legal-fork graphic using Public Law 93-559: broad police-assistance restriction effective July 1, 1975 on one branch; original narcotics-control and specified DEA/FBI exceptions on the other | ALT: Public Law 93-559 showing Section 660's police-assistance prohibition and original exceptions]

In **1985**, Public Law 99-83 added other exceptions and Administration of Justice authority that later supported ICITAP. That later history belongs mainly to Part 5. For this story, the point is concise: Congress closed much of the foreign-police route while leaving narcotics control and certain federal law-enforcement channels available. DEA did not become OPS under another name.

## AIR AMERICA ENDED. THE AVIATION NETWORK DIDN’T

After Air America ceased operations, men who said they had worked inside its Southeast Asian air world appeared in the covert supply operation supporting the Contras.

In contemporaneous accounts preserved in the 1986 Congressional Record, **Eugene Hasenfus** described about eight years with Air America as an air-freight specialist, or “kicker.” In October 1986, he was handling cargo aboard a C-123 resupply flight when it was shot down over Nicaragua.

Hasenfus also identified pilot **William J. Cooper** as a former Air America colleague. In an archival oral history, John McRainey recalled flying with Cooper beginning in Saigon in 1965 and serving with him as a C-123 instructor in Laos. He later described Cooper's role in the Central American supply program.

These are named firsthand and contemporaneous accounts, not acquired corporate personnel files. They support a careful sentence: **some veterans of the Southeast Asian covert-air world later appeared in Central American covert logistics.** They do not show that Air America survived as a company or became the Contra air network.

## THE C-123 CONNECTION

One aircraft makes that personnel continuity look almost physical.

**Barry Seal** was a convicted trafficker who began cooperating with DEA. In 1984, he participated in a Nicaragua operation that produced surveillance evidence involving a cocaine shipment and a Sandinista official. Political disclosure of the operation damaged the continuing investigation. Seal was a DEA cooperator in this episode; the reviewed record does not establish that he was a CIA employee.

The aircraft associated with Seal's operation was a C-123. Two years later, a C-123 carrying Contra supplies was shot down over Nicaragua. Hasenfus survived; Cooper and the other crewmen did not.

Compiled airframe histories present a striking identity chain: registration **N4410F**, USAF serial **54-0679**, manufacturer serial **20128**. A contemporary DEA spokesman was also reported as believing the downed aircraft was the one used in Seal's operation. Yet the certified FAA registration history, transfer documents and official serial-level crash-identification packet have not been located.

Available records and compiled airframe histories indicate that it was **probably the same C-123**, although the primary registration and transfer chain has not been verified.

[IMAGE: Two-event C-123 identity timeline—Seal/DEA operation in 1984 and Contra resupply loss on October 5, 1986—with N4410F, 54-0679 and 20128 shown as a consistent secondary chain and a visible PRIMARY TRANSFER RECORD MISSING break | ALT: Probable but unverified identity chain between the C-123 used in Seal's DEA operation and the aircraft lost during Contra resupply]

That is compelling continuity, not categorical proof. The aircraft should be understood the same way as the people around it: a strong bridge between two covert-air worlds whose exact documentary chain remains incomplete.

## THE CONTRA SUPPLY NETWORK

The larger network does not depend on solving the airframe question. In 1989, the Senate Foreign Relations subcommittee published *Drugs, Law Enforcement and Foreign Policy*. Its formal finding was that there was **“substantial evidence of drug smuggling through the war zones”** by individual Contras, suppliers, pilots, mercenaries and supporters. The committee found that trafficking organizations used Contra supply infrastructure and that elements of the movement accepted material or financial help from traffickers.

That finding did not say Contra leaders collectively directed a CIA cocaine operation. It did establish repeated overlap between the logistics of war and the logistics of narcotics.

The committee reported that the State Department paid four companies supporting the Contra effort **more than $806,000** despite drug ties, allegations or investigations connected to their principals and operations. The exact cents sometimes repeated in later accounts have not been independently reconstructed from vouchers and contracts, so the committee-attributed rounded figure is the honest one.

**George Morales**, a convicted trafficker, said he supplied aircraft, pilots, training and money to the Southern Front in an arrangement that gave him access. CIA's inspector general later documented that contacts with Morales-implicated individuals continued after the organization-level relationship with ARDE ended. The government record separates those continuing personal contacts from a claim that every earlier relationship simply carried on unchanged.

Then there was **John Hull**. Pilot Gary Betzner testified that weapons moved south and cocaine returned through facilities associated with Hull. A Costa Rican prosecutor told the committee he believed powerful interests interfered with the investigation. Those are attributed statements, not flight manifests or a committee finding that proved every alleged load.

The evidence is strongest when kept in its proper layers: official findings that trafficking organizations used the supply network; documented payments and relationships; testimony about particular routes; and gaps where the underlying transaction or flight records are absent.

## WHEN LAW ENFORCEMENT GOT TOO CLOSE

The revealing moments are not rumors about a hidden super-cartel. They are the points where an ordinary narcotics case threatened an intelligence relationship, an allied government or the survival of a covert program.

The structure was partly legal and bureaucratic. A **1982 CIA–Justice Department agreement** required reporting suspected federal crimes by employees. For nonemployees—agents, assets and independent contractors—the listed mandatory categories omitted narcotics. CIA lawyers retained discretion to report other serious offenses, and the Attorney General expected existing drug cooperation to continue, but narcotics allegations against people most likely to surround a deniable operation were not treated like allegations against staff.

Justice officials identified the gap in **1985**. Express coverage for narcotics crimes by nonemployees did not arrive until **1995**. No document shows that the agreement was designed to protect traffickers. The uneven reporting structure and its delayed repair are documented without inventing that motive.

The case of **Alan Hyde** shows how operational value affected decisions. CIA OIG recorded serious but unsubstantiated trafficking allegations around Hyde while his company remained a difficult-to-replace aviation-fuel supplier. The relationship continued under restrictions. A later cable warned that the Agency relationship could complicate a counternarcotics prosecution; the underlying cable is not local, so the OIG's scoped description—not an embellished quotation—controls.

Former CIA official **Alan Fiers** acknowledged significant evidence of cocaine trafficking to the United States by the Southern Front during the funding cutoff. In another case, the Kerry investigation documented advocacy for leniency toward Honduran general **José Bueso Rosa** tied to concerns that harsher treatment might damage Contra operations. Neither episode proves a universal protection policy. Both show national-security considerations entering law-enforcement decisions.

Seal's operation supplies a third mechanism. Intelligence from his 1984 sting was disclosed for Contra political purposes before prosecutors wanted it public, damaging the operation. The government did not need to employ Seal as a CIA operative for covert-war politics to collide with the narcotics case he was helping DEA build.

[IMAGE: Enforcement-conflict timeline comparing the 1982 reporting agreement, the 1985 warning, Seal's compromised sting, Bueso advocacy, Hyde retention and the 1995 reporting revision; label allegation, official finding and policy effect separately | ALT: Timeline of documented clashes between narcotics enforcement and Contra-era operational priorities]

CIA's inspector general also documented referrals, terminations and cases in which relationships ended. Practice varied. That variation is exactly why “CIA protection” is too blunt as a label. The record instead shows distinct mechanisms—reporting discretion, operational retention, political disclosure and case-specific advocacy—sometimes producing similar advantages for useful people.

## BRAZIL: WHERE THE DRUG TRAIL BREAKS BUT THE SECURITY PIPELINE DOESN’T

Brazil tests whether this article is really about narcotics or about a broader architecture of access and security.

Before the 1964 coup, U.S. records document CIA political action involving Brazilian labor, student and other organizations. Ambassador Lincoln Gordon sought contingency preparations for clandestine arms and other support, and **Operation Brother Sam** readied naval, fuel, ammunition and airlift assistance. Much of that support was not used because the takeover succeeded quickly.

Alongside that covert and diplomatic effort, USAID's police program expanded communications, records, laboratories, mobility, training, administration, riot-control and counter-subversion capacity. These were separate channels with distinct authorities. No acquired document places CIA political action and OPS police assistance under a single Brazilian command.

The institutions that received that capacity later operated inside a security state marked by detention, interrogation and torture. The Brazilian Truth Commission documented the roles of SNI, political-police units, OBAN and DOI-CODI. U.S. reporting addressed police-linked killings, widespread arrests and “psychophysical” interrogation. The documents show American awareness of abuse and continued institutional support; they do not show USAID ordering torture.

[IMAGE: Four-lane Brazil diagram for covert political action, OPS police capacity, repression and narcotics; carry the first three lanes through the documented record and terminate the narcotics lane at DOCUMENTARY GAP | ALT: Brazil evidence map showing documented security architecture and the missing narcotics commodity chain]

And the narcotics line stops. The reviewed record does not identify a protected Brazilian trafficker, a specific route, a financing mechanism and a U.S. endpoint comparable to the evidence assembled for Laos or selected Contra cases. Regional proximity and later criminal networks are leads, not a completed chain.

Brazil therefore strengthens one part of the thesis and rejects another. It shows how overt aid and covert political action could build compatible security relationships around an allied order. It does not complete a drug pipeline.

## WHAT THE PIPELINE ACTUALLY WAS

By now the word has to earn its place.

The pipeline was not one CIA-owned route carrying heroin from a Lao village and cocaine from Central America into the United States. It was a set of systems that carried different things: officers to schools, biographies to liaison desks, weapons and fuel to proxy forces, relief into isolated communities, and political backing toward allied institutions.

Those systems mattered when they ran through narcotics economies. Aircraft and roads expanded mobility. Police and intelligence relationships created access. Allied commanders controlled territory. Reporting agreements shaped what had to be disclosed. Operational value influenced which relationships survived scrutiny.

Across Laos and selected Contra cases, the record supports infrastructure, knowledge, tolerance and selective non-enforcement. It supports case-specific accommodation. It does not support a centrally directed CIA cartel or a USAID policy of promoting opium.

That is less cinematic than a master conspiracy. It is also harder to dismiss, because it rests on what institutions kept doing after the narcotics risk was visible.

## THE STRONGEST CASE AGAINST THIS

The strongest conventional explanation begins before any American arrived. The Golden Triangle already produced opium. Latin American traffickers and political actors pursued their own interests. Weak states, corruption, war and criminal opportunism can place narcotics around a covert operation without making narcotics the operation's purpose.

Air America's official inquiry found no corporate trafficking policy, no senior order and no established knowing participation by American pilots. Passenger cargo was difficult to police in wartime. Schanche's theory about CIA transport replacing Communist buyers was explicitly speculation. McCoy's village and military accounts remain interviews whose underlying notes and flight records are not local.

The downstream chain also breaks. No single shipment is traced from a Hmong village through Long Tieng, a refinery and an American dealer. McCoy's three named 1971 cases remain attributed accounts rather than acquired government case files. No named IPA student has been followed into PARU and Long Tieng. The C-123 connection is probable, not verified.

The Contra record carries its own limits. Witness testimony is uneven, CIA sometimes referred allegations or terminated relationships, and OIG found no CIA relationship with Rick Ross, Danilo Blandón or Norwin Meneses. It rejected the claim that CIA created the crack epidemic through those men. At the institutional level, OIG found: **“No information has been found to indicate that CIA as an organization or its employees conspired with, or assisted, Contra-related organizations or individuals in drug trafficking.”**

That conclusion cannot be brushed aside. Neither can it be stretched into a finding that there were no contacts, reporting failures, retained relationships or enforcement conflicts. OIG's case files documented all four.

Brazil completes the rebuttal. Its strong security and political record does not yield a narcotics commodity chain. Similar institutions and geography do not manufacture continuity.

After every one of those objections is applied, the narrower finding remains. CIA knew that important Lao allies and territory were implicated in narcotics and put the war first. Congressional and inspector-general records found traffickers, allegations and operationally useful relationships around Contra logistics. Reporting and enforcement sometimes bent around strategic value. Autonomous crime explains the origin of much of the activity; it does not erase what U.S. institutions did after they encountered it.

## THE FILES LEAD TO DAN MITRIONE

The paper trail now leaves narcotics and follows the police network itself.

OPS and IPA built transnational training and advisory relationships. Their graduates and advisers became overseas contacts. The same foreign-police system extended into Uruguay, where **Dan Mitrione** served as USAID's chief public-safety adviser after earlier work in Brazil and teaching at IPA.

Uruguayan torture predated Mitrione's arrival. OPS advisers later participated in police intelligence and search operations during his 1970 kidnapping. What remains disputed is what Mitrione personally taught, what Washington knew, and where institutional responsibility ends when an aid program strengthens a force already using torture.

There is no verified Moran-to-IPA-to-Special-Brigade bridge, and the famous Mitrione quotation does not belong here as settled fact. Part 4 begins with the harder question: **what can the files prove about one adviser—and what do they reveal about the system even if the legend around him cannot be proved?**

We followed the academy into the drug war. The next documents lead from the classroom into the interrogation room.

## FAQ

### Did the CIA directly traffic heroin?

The reviewed record does not establish a centrally directed CIA heroin operation. It documents allied narcotics activity, CIA knowledge, occasional carriage through an incompletely controlled air system and a decision to prioritize the Laos war over suppressing the trade. That supports knowledge and tolerance more strongly than direct institutional trafficking.

### Did Air America knowingly carry opium?

Official review found no corporate policy, senior order or established knowing participation by American pilots. It also conceded that opium could and occasionally did get aboard and that local loading controls were incomplete. Named interviews go further, but they do not establish what every pilot knew.

### Did USAID knowingly support drug trafficking?

No reviewed document establishes a USAID policy of supporting narcotics trafficking. USAID programs financed logistics, relief, roads and police capacity in places where allied officials and military systems intersected narcotics economies. The documented effects and institutional access matter, but effect is not proof of trafficking intent.

### What is actually documented versus inferred?

Documents establish the training programs, aircraft systems, statutory exceptions, reporting rules, contractor payments, official findings and selected relationship-retention decisions. Interviews and testimony establish attributed accounts of village collection, military aviation and particular Contra routes. The larger claim—that strategic utility sometimes preserved narcotics-connected people and infrastructure—is an inference drawn from those bounded cases, not a master-order document.

### Why does the International Police Academy matter to this story?

IPA connected foreign officers to U.S. training and created an alumni network that Justice described as useful to its representatives. Part 2 also documented CIA liaison, assessment and selected sponsorship around the broader police-training system. The academy matters as institutional infrastructure, not because every student or narcotics trainee was a CIA asset.
