# USAID, the CIA, and the Pipelines That Met the Drug War

In 1972, a [CIA inspector-general inquiry](https://www.intelligence.senate.gov/sites/default/files/94755_I.pdf) investigated reports that **Air America** was carrying opium out of Laos. It rejected the idea of a corporate trafficking policy or an order from senior management. Then, in the same inquiry, came the concessions: opium could—and sometimes did—get aboard, local passengers loaded cargo beyond the airline's full control, and the CIA knew that allied figures and territory were entangled with narcotics.

Suppressing that trade ranked below the war. The contradiction changed the question: *What happens when the people, aircraft and institutions needed for an American operation are also embedded in a narcotics economy?*

This article follows that question from mountain villages in Laos to police classrooms in Washington, from a disputed C-123 to the Contra supply network, and finally to the moments when law enforcement threatened relationships Washington still wanted.

## THE DENIAL THAT OPENED THE DOOR

The inquiry found no evidence that Air America adopted a trafficking policy, that senior officers ordered narcotics carriage, or that American pilots knowingly participated. It also described incomplete controls over locally loaded cargo and acknowledged occasional carriage. CIA officers were working with commanders and through territory already implicated in the opium economy.

The [Agency's later official history of Laos](https://www.cia.gov/resources/csi/static/CIA-Air-Ops-Laos.pdf) supplied the priority decision. Confronting those allies over narcotics risked the war effort, and the war came first.

[IMAGE: Split documentary panel from the Senate summary of the CIA inquiry—no corporate policy or senior order on one side; incomplete loading control, occasional carriage and wartime non-suppression on the other | ALT: Senate inquiry findings separating Air America policy denials from concessions about loading controls and opium carriage]

CIA had rejected an institutional trafficking policy while documenting knowledge, incomplete control and tolerance around an indispensable wartime airline. That was enough to keep the investigation alive.

## FOLLOW THE PIPELINES

The documents trace overlapping channels: officers through training programs; biographies through liaison offices; food, medicine, weapons and money toward allied forces; aircraft and roads through isolated territory; and narcotics through parts of the same geography. The connections appear in specific cables, courses, contracts, flights and reporting decisions. Follow them, and a larger structure comes into view.

## BEFORE THE DRUGS CAME THE FILES

Part 2 established the personnel route before narcotics control became central to this story. The **Office of Public Safety** and **International Police Academy** ran the overt foreign-police program. Behind it sat concealed CIA junctions, including International Police Services, where selected officers could receive follow-on training without knowing the facility was CIA-controlled.

A CIA Police Group memorandum described daily liaison with OPS and IPA, exchanges of participant information, placement of some CIA-sponsored officers, and additional briefings or training for selected visitors. The Pike investigation described instructors assessing pro-American orientation and sending names and biographies to CIA components for possible operational use.

Before drug enforcement entered the classroom, USAID/OPS/IPA had already built an international police-training route from which CIA obtained participant information, assessments, sponsorship opportunities and selected personnel connections.

## THE GOLDEN TRIANGLE WAS ALREADY THERE

Northern Laos already had an opium economy. Mountain communities cultivated a portable cash crop in terrain where roads, buyers and alternative markets were scarce; Chinese networks, merchants, soldiers and officials moved it through a regional trade. Then American relief, aviation, roads, military support and covert alliances entered the same valleys.

### Pop Buell Tries to Replace the Poppy

The most revealing account begins with **Edgar “Pop” Buell**, the aid worker who became a legendary figure among Hmong communities. In Don Schanche's telling, Buell first tried to replace poppy cultivation with sweet potatoes. The crop grew. The development theory did not.

Villagers lacked transport and dependable buyers. Sweet potatoes spoiled; opium did not. A small amount of opium could be carried across difficult terrain and exchanged for cash in a way bulky food crops could not. In Schanche's account, substitution failed because the surrounding economy offered no workable substitute market.

### “If You’re Gonna Grow It, Grow It Good”

Buell changed tactics. Schanche attributes to him the line: **“If you're gonna grow it, grow it good.”** In Schanche's account, Buell believed immediate eradication would impoverish the villages and also tried to discourage local smoking.

Schanche added an uncomfortable economic turn. American medicines reduced some of the medicinal reasons villagers consumed opium, while production continued to rise. Less local use meant more of the crop could reach the market. The crop remained, and so did the need for a buyer.

### The Buyer Changed

At Phou Vieng, Schanche recounts, a deteriorating crop presented an immediate crisis. **Blea Vu** and Buell discussed selling it to the Pathet Lao. Fourteen ponies carried the opium out. The villagers were paid in counterfeit kip.

Phou Vieng stopped dealing with the Communist buyers, but Schanche wrote that other communities continued. Later he observed that Communist buying disappeared in areas served by Air America. The crop had not vanished. The old market had.

What replaced it? Schanche believed CIA-controlled transport supplied the missing outlet, but he marked the boundary himself: **“one can only speculate about this.”**

[IMAGE: Buell–Schanche economic-flow graphic separating observed events from inference: failed sweet-potato market, cultivation advice, reduced medicinal use, continued production, Communist buyers, reported buyer change, speculative replacement outlet | ALT: Flowchart distinguishing Schanche's documented narrative from his speculation about the replacement opium market]

Schanche also described Buell refusing to fly villagers' opium. The aid program was adapting to a crop economy it could not displace while wartime transport changed the market around it.

## HOW THE OPIUM WAS ACTUALLY COLLECTED

The mountain trade was not an abstraction. [McCoy's interviews describe](https://archive.org/details/politicsofheroin0000mcco) buyers, prices, packing methods and seasonal routines. The physical detail is what makes the later arrival of helicopters matter.

### Before the Helicopters

In the Long Pot area, McCoy reported that Hmong officers entered villages to test and purchase opium. He put their price at roughly **$60 per kilogram**, compared with **$40 to $50** offered by other merchants. The purchased opium went into bamboo containers, onto ponies and toward Long Tieng. Collection circuits could repeat two or three times during a season.

Village-to-village buying, consolidation and pack-animal movement linked producing communities to the military center.

### Then the Helicopters Arrived

**Ger Su Yang**, a village leader interviewed by McCoy in 1971, described a change in 1969 and 1970. The officers no longer needed to ride the whole route. They arrived by helicopter, spent days moving among villages, then radioed Long Tieng for pickup.

McCoy recorded him saying that **“Meo officers with three or four stripes ... came from Long Tieng to buy our opium. They came in American helicopters.”** A headman from Nam Ou gave McCoy a similar account. Both traced the buyers' origin and return toward Long Tieng.

Ger Su Yang went further: **“the helicopter pilots were always Americans.”**

[IMAGE: Laos collection map showing village buyers, comparative prices, bamboo packing, pony movement toward Long Tieng, and the later helicopter insertion/radio-pickup sequence; label every aviation leg as named interview evidence | ALT: Map of the opium collection process described by Ger Su Yang and other villagers interviewed by McCoy]

### The View From the Top

McCoy also interviewed senior Lao military figures. **General Ouane Rattikone** told him that opium had been dropped into the Gulf of Siam for pickup by fishing boats and described attempts to secure military C-47 capacity. Former Royal Lao Air Force commander **Thao Ma** separately recounted being offered wealth if he would allocate two C-47s; he said he refused.

In the accounts of men positioned near the top of the Lao military, aircraft were being used—or actively sought—for opium movement.

The village view and the command view meet at one place: access to transport.

## THE AIR SYSTEM

### Air America

Air America was critical to the CIA-backed war in Laos and materially sustained by AID contracting and assignments. Its aircraft carried food, medicine, weapons, personnel and other cargo through a country where roads were scarce and the war was officially concealed.

The inquiry found incomplete control over passenger cargo and conceded occasional opium carriage. McCoy and other published accounts described particular loads and practices. Locally loaded cargo could escape full inspection, CIA knew the narcotics economy surrounded its allies, and the operation continued.

### Xieng Khouang Air Transport

Published accounts place **Xieng Khouang Air Transport**, or XKAT, on a Long Tieng–Vientiane route inside the U.S.-backed war. McCoy identified Lo Kham Thy as president, discussed the company's relationship to Vang Pao and recorded narcotics allegations. Corporate, ownership, aircraft and cargo records remain incomplete.

### Mae Salong: Roads Into an Opium Economy

On the ground, U.S.-supported road construction increased mobility and commerce [around Mae Salong](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76v20/d173), a KMT stronghold inside a regional narcotics economy. The road opened markets and increased carrying capacity, though regional KMT tonnage cannot be assigned to that single route.

## THE PIPELINE REACHES THE UNITED STATES

By the early 1970s, official reporting connected Golden Triangle production to South Vietnam, the GI heroin crisis and wider international and American markets: production, regional movement, refining and overseas demand.

McCoy gives three concrete downstream cases: a **15.5-kilogram** Vientiane-to-New York shipment, a **7.7-kilogram** postal seizure at Fort Monmouth connected to arrivals from Laos, and the **60-kilogram** Sopsaisana seizure at Orly. For the Orly load, McCoy attributed the alleged Long Tieng origin to diplomatic sources; Sopsaisana denied ownership.

No single shipment has yet been traced continuously from a Hmong village through Long Tieng to an American dealer.

That still leaves a policy problem. Washington was supporting a war inside the producing region while confronting heroin among American servicemen and at home. One answer was enforcement training. That route led back to the International Police Academy.

## THE POLICE ACADEMY REAPPEARS—THIS TIME IN THE DRUG WAR

An [August 1974 Embassy program record](https://foia.state.gov/DOCUMENTS/FOIA_Micro_Oct2024_10/F-1989-03959/DOC_0C09000035/C09000035.pdf) from Vientiane describes the contradiction plainly. Royal Lao Army officials were implicated in narcotics trafficking and the use of military transportation, while a USAID-backed program was building narcotics-control capacity inside the army's military police.

### 163 Lao Military Policemen

The Provost Marshal General's criminal and investigation division had been reorganized to include a narcotics-control section. According to the Embassy, **163 military policemen** had received initial training. USAID narcotics advisers conducted seminars, and formal classes were held at the Lao National Police Academy.

The program's objective stated that it **“will eliminate narcotics trafficking by Royal Lao Army officials, use of military transportation for such trafficking.”** One part of the U.S.-supported Lao security establishment was being trained to police narcotics activity associated with other military personnel and transport.

### Back to Washington

The Embassy reported that **seven Lao military police** had already received narcotics training in the United States. A special English-language program prepared participant trainees for possible U.S. courses. The listed destinations included the U.S. Army Military Police School, the International Police Academy and the DEA Training School.

The document does not allocate the seven by school. It does show local military-police training reaching U.S. institutions just as advisory responsibility was scheduled to pass to DEA at the end of fiscal 1975.

### Narcotics Orientation Course No. 8

A second original document brings Laos and Thailand into the academy itself. The January 1975 *[Police Academy Review](https://ojp.gov/pdffiles1/Digitization/19349NCJRS.pdf)* covered a **December 20, 1974** graduation that included **PMNO No. 8**. The publication lists **27 classmates from nine countries**, including Laos and Thailand, and reports a cumulative academy total of **5,204 graduates from 77 countries**.

At commencement, Major **George Noujaim** declared: **“In the field of fighting crime and in the struggle against the illicit drug traffic, the United States plays a most important role.”** A multinational narcotics-oriented cohort including Laos and Thailand had passed through the Washington academy.

[IMAGE: Side-by-side documentary spread from Vientiane A-102 page 16 and the PMNO No. 8 graduation pages, highlighting 163 military police, seven U.S.-trained officers, listed schools, cohort size and participating countries | ALT: 1974 Laos narcotics program record beside the International Police Academy PMNO No. 8 graduation report]

## THE ACADEMY WAS BUILDING A NETWORK

### The Federal Bureau of Narcotics Was Already Inside

The relationship did not begin with that class. Treasury reports show the **Federal Bureau of Narcotics**, IPA and AID cooperating on narcotics instruction across three consecutive fiscal years.

In **[FY1966](https://fraser.stlouisfed.org/title/annual-report-secretary-treasury-state-finances-194/annual-report-secretary-treasury-state-finances-fiscal-year-ended-june-30-1966-5600/fulltext)**, FBN ran a two-week Spanish-language course for **19 Latin American officers**, covering cocaine, heroin and marijuana. The **[FY1967](https://fraser.stlouisfed.org/title/annual-report-secretary-treasury-state-finances-194/annual-report-secretary-treasury-state-finances-fiscal-year-ended-june-30-1967-5601/fulltext)** report documented another two-week narcotics-control course and continuing FBN lectures. In **[FY1968](https://fraser.stlouisfed.org/title/annual-report-secretary-treasury-state-finances-194/annual-report-secretary-treasury-state-finances-fiscal-year-ended-june-30-1968-5602/fulltext)**, bilingual agents again taught a two-week course and provided regular instruction. Three consecutive Treasury reports show the relationship continuing.

### Alumni Became the Contacts

A [1972 Justice Department report](https://www.ojp.gov/ncjrs/virtual-library/abstracts/federal-law-enforcement-and-criminal-justice-activities-attorney) described the scale reached by then: **3,932 IPA graduates from 73 countries**, with graduates commanding **13 national police agencies**. It also described specialized narcotics-enforcement training and explained the operational value of the network.

IPA graduates and OPS advisers, the report said, could **“serve as valuable contacts overseas for Department of Justice representatives when needed.”** DOJ was explicit about whom those contacts served.

Part 2 documented a separate CIA channel: daily Police Group liaison, participant-information exchanges and sponsorship of selected officers. The overt academy was producing overseas contacts for Justice while also giving CIA biographies, assessments and access to selected personnel.

### Thailand and PARU

CIA official history and [Bill Lair's retrospective account](https://www.cia.gov/resources/csi/static/LairMemoir-May2022-Web.pdf) describe **CIA's direct role in developing PARU**, whose personnel trained Hmong forces and provided communications support in Laos. Lair distinguished that channel from **USAID Public Safety advice to most other Thai police organizations**. CIA built the PARU relationship directly; USAID advisers worked with the broader Thai police establishment.

## WHEN CONGRESS CLOSED ONE DOOR, NARCOTICS LEFT ANOTHER OPEN

Congress enacted **Section 660** on **December 30, 1974**, [as part of Public Law 93-559](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/STATUTE-88/pdf/STATUTE-88-Pg1795.pdf). The restriction became effective on **July 1, 1975**. It broadly barred Foreign Assistance Act funds and related local currencies from supporting foreign police, prisons, other law-enforcement forces and internal-intelligence or surveillance programs. The exceptions were present at enactment: the statute preserved FAA Section 482 international narcotics-control assistance and specified activities relating to DEA and FBI authority over conduct unlawful in the United States. Other agencies could also operate under distinct appropriations and authorities.

[IMAGE: Section 660 legal-fork graphic using Public Law 93-559: broad police-assistance restriction effective July 1, 1975 on one branch; original narcotics-control and specified DEA/FBI exceptions on the other | ALT: Public Law 93-559 showing Section 660's police-assistance prohibition and original exceptions]

In **1985**, [Public Law 99-83](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/STATUTE-99/pdf/STATUTE-99-Pg190.pdf) added other exceptions and Administration of Justice authority that later supported ICITAP. Congress had broadly restricted the foreign-police route while leaving international narcotics control and specified federal law-enforcement channels open.

## AIR AMERICA ENDED. THE AVIATION NETWORK DIDN’T

After Air America ceased operations, men who said they had worked inside its Southeast Asian air world appeared in the covert supply operation supporting the Contras.

In contemporaneous accounts preserved in the [1986 Congressional Record](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/GPO-CRECB-1986-pt21/pdf/GPO-CRECB-1986-pt21-2-3.pdf), **Eugene Hasenfus** described about eight years with Air America as an air-freight specialist, or “kicker.” In October 1986, he was handling cargo aboard a C-123 resupply flight when it was shot down over Nicaragua.

Hasenfus also identified pilot **William J. Cooper** as a former Air America colleague. In an [archival oral history](https://vva.vietnam.ttu.edu/images.php?from=website&img=%2FOH%2FOH0182%2FOH0182.pdf), John McRainey recalled flying with Cooper beginning in Saigon in 1965 and serving with him as a C-123 instructor in Laos. He later described Cooper's role in the Central American supply program.

Hasenfus and Cooper put veterans of the Southeast Asian covert-air world inside Central American covert logistics.

## THE C-123 CONNECTION

One aircraft makes that personnel continuity look almost physical.

**Barry Seal** was a convicted trafficker who began cooperating with DEA. In 1984, he participated in a Nicaragua operation that produced surveillance evidence involving a cocaine shipment and a Sandinista official. Political disclosure of the operation damaged the continuing investigation.

The aircraft associated with Seal's operation was a C-123. Two years later, a C-123 carrying Contra supplies was shot down over Nicaragua. Hasenfus survived; Cooper and the other crewmen did not.

[Compiled airframe histories](https://vva.vietnam.ttu.edu/images.php?img=%2Fimages%2F406%2F4060513001.pdf) present a striking identity chain: registration **N4410F**, USAF serial **54-0679**, manufacturer serial **20128**. A contemporary DEA spokesman was also reported as believing the downed aircraft was the one used in Seal's operation. Available records indicate that it was **probably the same C-123**, although the primary registration and transfer chain has not been verified.

## THE CONTRA SUPPLY NETWORK

The committee record widens the view beyond one airframe. In 1989, the Senate Foreign Relations subcommittee published *[Drugs, Law Enforcement and Foreign Policy](https://nsarchive2.gwu.edu/NSAEBB/NSAEBB113/north06.pdf)*. Its formal finding was that there was **“substantial evidence of drug smuggling through the war zones”** by individual Contras, suppliers, pilots, mercenaries and supporters. The committee found that trafficking organizations used Contra supply infrastructure and that elements of the movement accepted material or financial help from traffickers.

The committee reported that the State Department paid four companies supporting the Contra effort **more than $806,000** despite drug ties, allegations or investigations connected to their principals and operations.

**George Morales**, a convicted trafficker, said he supplied aircraft, pilots, training and money to the Southern Front in an arrangement that gave him access. [CIA's inspector general later documented](https://irp.fas.org/cia/product/cocaine2/index.html) that contacts with Morales-implicated individuals continued after the organization-level relationship with ARDE ended.

Then there was **John Hull**. Pilot Gary Betzner testified that weapons moved south and cocaine returned through facilities associated with Hull. A Costa Rican prosecutor told the committee he believed powerful interests interfered with the investigation. The committee's findings, the payments, Morales's account and Betzner's testimony placed narcotics activity repeatedly inside the logistics of the war.

## WHEN LAW ENFORCEMENT GOT TOO CLOSE

The most revealing records appear when narcotics investigations collided with covert operational priorities.

The structure was partly legal and bureaucratic. A **[1982 CIA–Justice Department agreement](https://oig.justice.gov/sites/default/files/legacy/special/9712/ch11p1.htm)** required reporting suspected federal crimes by employees. For nonemployees—agents, assets and independent contractors—the listed mandatory categories omitted narcotics. CIA lawyers retained discretion to report other serious offenses, and the Attorney General expected existing drug cooperation to continue, but narcotics allegations against people most likely to surround a deniable operation were not treated like allegations against staff.

Justice officials identified the gap in **1985**. Express coverage for narcotics crimes by nonemployees did not arrive until **1995**.

The case of **Alan Hyde** shows how operational value affected decisions. CIA OIG recorded serious but unsubstantiated trafficking allegations around Hyde while his company remained a difficult-to-replace aviation-fuel supplier. The relationship continued under restrictions. A later cable warned that the Agency relationship could complicate a counternarcotics prosecution.

Former CIA official **Alan Fiers** acknowledged significant evidence of cocaine trafficking to the United States by the Southern Front during the funding cutoff. In another case, the Kerry investigation documented advocacy for leniency toward Honduran general **José Bueso Rosa** because harsher treatment might damage Contra operations. National-security considerations had entered law-enforcement decisions.

Seal's operation supplies a third mechanism. Intelligence from his 1984 sting was disclosed for Contra political purposes before prosecutors wanted it public, damaging the narcotics case he was helping DEA build.

[IMAGE: Enforcement-conflict timeline comparing the 1982 reporting agreement, the 1985 warning, Seal's compromised sting, Bueso advocacy, Hyde retention and the 1995 reporting revision; label allegation, official finding and policy effect separately | ALT: Timeline of documented clashes between narcotics enforcement and Contra-era operational priorities]

CIA's inspector general also documented referrals, terminations and relationships that ended. Practice varied. But reporting discretion, operational retention, political disclosure and case-specific advocacy repeatedly gave strategically useful people advantages when narcotics enforcement came close.

## BRAZIL: WHERE THE DRUG TRAIL BREAKS BUT THE SECURITY PIPELINE DOESN’T

Brazil shows the security architecture without the narcotics chain.

Before the 1964 coup, [U.S. records document CIA political action](https://nsarchive2.gwu.edu/NSAEBB/NSAEBB118/) involving Brazilian labor, student and other organizations. Ambassador Lincoln Gordon sought contingency preparations for clandestine arms and other support, and **Operation Brother Sam** readied naval, fuel, ammunition and airlift assistance. Much of that support was not used because the takeover succeeded quickly.

Alongside that covert and diplomatic effort, USAID's police program expanded communications, records, laboratories, mobility, training, administration, riot-control and counter-subversion capacity.

The institutions that received that capacity later operated inside a security state marked by detention, interrogation and torture. The [Brazilian Truth Commission](https://www.gov.br/memoriasreveladas/pt-br/assuntos/comissoes-da-verdade/cnv) documented SNI, political-police units, OBAN and DOI-CODI. [U.S. reporting addressed police-linked killings](https://nsarchive2.gwu.edu/NSAEBB/NSAEBB478/), widespread arrests and “psychophysical” interrogation while American support continued.

[IMAGE: Four-lane Brazil diagram for covert political action, OPS police capacity, repression and narcotics; carry the first three lanes through the documented record and terminate the narcotics lane at DOCUMENTARY GAP | ALT: Brazil evidence map showing documented security architecture and the missing narcotics commodity chain]

Here the narcotics line stops. No protected Brazilian trafficker, route, financing mechanism or U.S. endpoint completes a chain comparable to Laos or the Contra cases. Brazil belongs in this story because overt aid and covert political action built compatible relationships around an allied security state—not because the documents establish a Brazilian drug route.

## WHAT THE PIPELINE ACTUALLY WAS

What recurs is an infrastructure of access. Aircraft and roads expanded mobility. Police schools and liaison offices opened personnel channels. Allied commanders controlled territory. Reporting rules determined which allegations had to reach Justice, and operational value influenced which relationships survived scrutiny.

In Laos, the war took priority after the narcotics risk was known. In the Contra cases, supply relationships repeatedly collided with enforcement. The recurring advantage was access to transportation, police contacts, military relationships and covert logistics that Washington still considered valuable.

## THE STRONGEST CASE AGAINST THIS

There is a serious case against reading too much into this record. The Golden Triangle produced opium before the American war. Latin American traffickers and political actors pursued their own interests. Weak states, corruption, war and criminal opportunism can put narcotics around a covert operation without making narcotics its purpose.

Air America's official inquiry found no corporate trafficking policy, no senior order and no established knowing participation by American pilots. Passenger cargo was difficult to police in wartime. Schanche's theory about CIA transport replacing Communist buyers was explicitly speculation. McCoy's village and military accounts remain interviews whose underlying notes and flight records are not local.

The downstream chain also breaks. No single shipment is traced from a Hmong village through Long Tieng, a refinery and an American dealer. McCoy's three named 1971 cases remain attributed accounts rather than acquired government case files. No named IPA student has been followed into PARU and Long Tieng. The C-123 connection is probable, not verified.

The Contra record carries its own limits. Witness testimony is uneven, CIA sometimes referred allegations or terminated relationships, and OIG found no CIA relationship with Rick Ross, Danilo Blandón or Norwin Meneses. It rejected the claim that CIA created the crack epidemic through those men. At the institutional level, OIG found: **“No information has been found to indicate that CIA as an organization or its employees conspired with, or assisted, Contra-related organizations or individuals in drug trafficking.”**

OIG's institutional conclusion is substantial. Its case files also document contacts, reporting failures, retained relationships and enforcement conflicts. Brazil offers another limit: a strong record of covert political action, police assistance and abuse, but no narcotics commodity chain.

Even with those limits, several facts remain difficult to explain away. CIA knew that important Lao allies and territory were implicated in narcotics and put the war first. U.S.-supported aircraft and roads moved through that territory. OPS and IPA created foreign-police access while CIA collected participant information, assessed candidates and sponsored selected officers. Narcotics training later traveled through the same broad police structure. Section 660 restricted foreign-police assistance but left narcotics-control channels open. In Central America, congressional and inspector-general records found traffickers, allegations and useful relationships around Contra logistics, while reporting and enforcement sometimes bent around strategic value.

## THE FILES LEAD TO DAN MITRIONE

From here, the narcotics trail fades, but the police network keeps going.

OPS and IPA built transnational training and advisory relationships whose graduates and advisers became overseas contacts. That foreign-police program extended into Uruguay, where **Dan Mitrione** served as USAID's chief public-safety adviser after earlier work in Brazil and teaching at IPA.

Uruguayan torture predated Mitrione's arrival. OPS advisers later participated in police intelligence and search operations during his 1970 kidnapping. What remains disputed is what Mitrione personally taught, what Washington knew, and where institutional responsibility ends when an aid program strengthens a force already using torture.

Part 4 asks what the files can prove about one adviser—and what they reveal about the program around him. The next documents lead from the classroom into the interrogation room.

## FAQ

### Did the CIA directly traffic heroin?

No corporate Air America policy or senior order emerged from the inquiry. Investigators did document CIA knowledge, occasional carriage, incomplete loading controls and a decision to put the Laos war ahead of narcotics suppression.

### Did Air America knowingly carry opium?

Investigators did not establish knowing participation by American pilots, but they conceded that opium occasionally got aboard and that local loading controls were incomplete. McCoy's witnesses described American-piloted helicopters in the collection districts.

### How did USAID become part of this story?

USAID financed relief, roads, aviation support and police capacity in places where allied military systems intersected narcotics economies. OPS and IPA also created an overt personnel network that CIA used for liaison, assessment and selected sponsorship.

### What is actually documented versus inferred?

Government records establish training programs, aircraft support, statutory exceptions, reporting rules, contractor payments and selected retention decisions. McCoy's interviews and Contra testimony supply attributed accounts of particular routes. Together they show strategic value repeatedly shaping what Washington tolerated or kept.

### Why does the International Police Academy matter to this story?

IPA connected foreign officers to U.S. training and created an alumni network useful to Justice representatives. Part 2 also documented CIA liaison, assessment and selected sponsorship around that broader police-training system.
