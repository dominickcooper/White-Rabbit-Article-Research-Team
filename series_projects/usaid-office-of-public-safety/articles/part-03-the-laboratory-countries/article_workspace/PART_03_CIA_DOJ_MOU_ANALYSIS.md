# PART 3 CIA–DOJ MOU ANALYSIS

## Two records must not be conflated

### The local 1978 packet

`DOJ-CIA Memorandum of Understanding Relating.pdf` is a 13-page 1978 packet, not the 1982 signed MOU. It includes:

- Attorney General Griffin B. Bell's letter of Jan. 9, 1978;
- CIA General Counsel Anthony A. Lapham's Jan. 26, 1978 memorandum to DCI Stansfield Turner;
- a draft/undated Turner response accepting the arrangement subject to two edits; and
- draft procedures implementing 28 U.S.C. § 535 and Executive Order requirements for crimes involving CIA officers/employees and other reportable conduct.

The packet does not clearly show a final instrument bearing both signatures and an unambiguous effective date. It is therefore evidence of the pre-1982 framework and negotiations, not proof of the 1982 terms.

### The signed 1982 MOU

The authoritative local copy is Exhibit 1 in CIA OIG Volume II, PDF pp. 409-418, titled **“Reporting and Use of Information Concerning Federal Crimes.”** Attorney General William French Smith signed the covering agreement on Feb. 11, 1982; DCI William J. Casey accepted/signed on Mar. 2, 1982.

## Legal context and coverage

Before March 2, 1982, the OIG understood the operative Attorney General guidelines under Executive Order 12036 to require reporting of federal-law violations involving the wider class of people “employed by, assigned to, or acting for” CIA—including agents, assets, and independent contractors. The 1982 agreement was adopted under Executive Order 12333 and superseded that arrangement.

The 1982 MOU treated a CIA **employee** principally as a staff employee or contract employee, with limited provisions for former employees. It treated agents, assets, and independent contractors as **nonemployees** for the reporting scheme.

Mandatory categories for nonemployees included, in substance:

- serious crimes of violence;
- offenses affecting national security, defense, or foreign relations;
- foreign interference with U.S. governmental or political institutions; and
- offenses involving a foreign power or international terrorism.

Narcotics offenses were not included in the mandatory nonemployee list. The General Counsel retained discretion to report other serious federal offenses.

Attorney General Smith's Feb. 11 covering letter explained that narcotics was omitted from the formal list because the Department expected existing cooperation on drug matters to continue. That expectation was not the same as a categorical, enforceable reporting duty for every asset/agent allegation.

## Did the agreement create a practical reporting gap?

**Yes.** The gap was practical and documented, though the OIG found no evidence it was intentionally designed to shield the Contras.

### Who fell inside the gap?

- foreign agents and operational assets;
- independent contractors who were not contract employees;
- liaison or proxy figures with whom CIA maintained contact but who were not staff/contract employees; and
- potentially aviation, logistics, and support personnel whose precise status did not meet the MOU's employee definition.

### How long did it exist?

From the effective 1982 agreement (Casey's Mar. 2 acceptance) until the Aug. 3, 1995 revision broadened coverage and made narcotics reporting explicit for the relevant nonemployee class—roughly thirteen years and five months.

### What did CIA and DOJ know about the problem?

- A Feb. 8, 1985 DOJ memorandum stated that the MOU did not require reporting of narcotics offenses by nonemployees and recommended revision.
- CIA did not issue an agencywide implementing regulation until Dec. 23, 1987.
- The Directorate of Operations did not formally issue its handbook until Jan. 1996.
- CIA could and sometimes did report information discretionarily, so the gap was neither a total ban nor proof that every omission was unlawful.

### Which documented cases may have been affected?

The OIG's cases involving Southern Front personnel, pilots, aviation companies, Alan Hyde, and other support figures illustrate the vulnerable category: people or companies with operational value and alleged trafficking ties who were not necessarily CIA employees. The report says information about some people was shared while no evidence of notification was found for eleven others. Individual causation must be proved case by case; the MOU cannot automatically explain every nonreport.

## Evidentiary significance

The 1982 MOU does not prove conspiracy or direct participation. It does prove that the institutional rule governing a covert service drew its strongest mandatory reporting line around formal employees while leaving narcotics allegations concerning the very class most useful to deniable operations—assets, agents, and independent contractors—to discretion. In the Contra environment, that was not an abstract defect. It intersected with documented continued contacts, retained service providers, inconsistent reporting, and operational dependence.

**Best current verdict:** DOCUMENTED reporting gap; STRONG INFERENCE that it enabled tolerance and uneven enforcement; INSUFFICIENT evidence that the gap was adopted with the intent to facilitate narcotics trafficking.
