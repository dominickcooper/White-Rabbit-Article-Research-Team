*AKA: Drug running? Not the CIA.*
But the same inquiry also found that opium could—and did—get aboard, that local passengers loaded cargo beyond the airline’s full control, and that the CIA knew allied figures and territory were entangled with narcotics.
Suppressing that trade ranked below the war.

## **1962: STAY IN THE BACKGROUND**

Before we get to the opium, we need to go back to **1962**.
On **July 23**, fourteen nations signed [**the Geneva agreements**](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Agreement_on_the_Neutrality_of_Laos) declaring Laos neutral. The accords required the withdrawal of foreign regular troops, irregular troops, paramilitary formations, and military personnel. Washington complied publicly by withdrawing its military advisory teams. But North Vietnam did not fully withdraw its forces, and the United States continued supporting **Lao and Hmong forces** after the agreement.
**That created an obvious problem.**
***How do you keep fighting a war*** 
*in a country where you’re not supposed to be fighting a war?*
A month later, on **August 24, 1962**, President **John F. Kennedy** approved [**NSAM 182**](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1961-63v08/d105), establishing a government-wide doctrine for what Washington called **“Overseas Internal Defense.”** 
This was national policy for **State, Defense, AID, USIA, and CIA**, distributed to American missions and military commands around the world.
The September policy paper that followed was remarkably candid about how this kind of war should work.
American involvement, it said, should be **“ancillary to the indigenous effort.”** The United States should **“remain in the background”** and, whenever possible, restrict its visible role to training, advice, and materiel. 
The objective was to build **local forces** capable of fighting the internal war themselves while **minimizing overt American intervention**.
Plain English?
***Keep our hands clean.***
And that doctrine wasn’t limited to soldiers.
The document identified **police as the first line of defense against subversion and insurgency**. 
*Remember part 2 of this series??*
Washington would provide **friendly foreign police** with equipment, training, and technical assistance—and the normal channel for doing so would be **AID**, although Defense or CIA could take the role depending on the circumstances. 
For indigenous paramilitary forces, 
the policy explicitly called for Defense to work 
**“in collaboration with AID and CIA.”**

## **THE GOLDEN TRIANGLE**

Now, Northern Laos already had an opium economy. The mountain communities cultivated a portable cash crop in terrain where roads, buyers and alternative markets were scarce; Chinese networks, merchants, soldiers and officials moved it through a regional trade. 
**Very agrarian.** Very simple.
Then roads, aviation, military support, USAID, and covert alliances entered the same valleys.
*What are the chances?*
One of the most discussed accounts from that region has to do with [**Edgar “Pop” Buell**](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pop_Buell).
He was an aid worker through the [International Voluntary Services](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Voluntary_Services) (a precursor to the **Peace Corps**) who became a legendary figure among Hmong communities. In [According to Don Schanche’s telling](https://archive.org/details/misterpop00scha), Buell first tried to replace poppy cultivation with sweet potatoes. 
The crop grew. The market did not.
Villagers lacked transport and dependable buyers. And then there was shelf life. Sweet potatoes spoiled; opium did not. 
**Plus opium was much more lucrative.** 
A small amount of opium could be carried across difficult terrain and exchanged for cash in a way bulky food crops could not. So the substitution failed because the surrounding economy offered no workable substitute market.

### **“If You’re Gonna Grow It, Grow It Good”**

Now Buell **wasn't a random white man** in a remote village. The  [International Voluntary Services](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Voluntary_Services) he was a part of received 70-90% of its funding from USAID.

**American medicines** reduced some of the medicinal reasons villagers consumed opium, while production continued to rise. **Less local use** then left **more of the crop** available for sale. 
The crop remained, and so did **the need for a buyer.**
***Enter CIA.***

## **BOOTS ON THE GROUND: ALFRED McCOY’S ACCOUNT**

Now Alfred McCoy, unlike Don Sanache, was not simply writing about the drug trade during the Vietnam war. 
**He was actually in it.** 
There are several observations and interviews (done **during the war**, mind you) and they were all put down in his book “[The Politics of Heroin in Southeast Asia](https://archive.org/details/politicsofheroin0000mcco).”  
McCoy’s interviews describe buyers, prices, packing methods and seasonal routines.

### **Before the Helicopters**

In the Long Pot area, McCoy reported that **Hmong officers** entered villages to test and purchase opium. 
**Hmong.** 
The side that the US was helping. (*for clarity*)
The Hmong officers were paying **$60 per kilogram**, compared with **$40 to $50** offered by other merchants. The purchased opium went into bamboo containers, onto ponies and toward [**Long Tieng**](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Long_Tieng) **in Laos**. Collection circuits could repeat two or three times during a season.
Village-to-village buying, consolidation and pack-animal movement linked producing communities to the military center.

### **Then the Helicopters Arrived**

**Ger Su Yang**, a village leader [interviewed by McCoy in 1971](https://archive.org/details/politicsofheroin0000mcco), described a change in 1969 and 1970. The officers no longer needed to ride the whole route. They arrived **by helicopter**, spent days moving among villages, then radioed Long Tieng for pickup.

> *“Meo officers with three or four stripes [captain or major) came from Long Tieng to buy our opium.* 
>
> ***They came in American helicopters,*** *perhaps two or three men at one time.* 
>
> *The helicopter leaves them here for a few days and they walk to villages over there (swinging his arm in a semicircle in the direction of Gier Goot, Long Makkhay and Nam Pac], then come back here and radioed Long Tieng to send another helicopter for them.* 
>
> ***They take the opium back to Long Tieng****.”*
>
> *—* Ger Su Yang Interview

The use of helicopters (specifically American ones) was further corrobated to McCoy by [**General Ouane Rattikone**](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ouane_Rattikone) and Former Royal Lao Air Force commander [**Thao Ma**](https://vi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thao_Ma)**.**

[A later CIA assessment](https://www.cia.gov/readingroom/docs/CIA-RDP86T01017R000808060002-7.pdf?utm_source=chatgpt.com) described the 3rd and 5th Armies as the region’s dominant opium traffickers—and noted that **intelligence networks they had already built in Burma were well suited to distributing opium throughout eastern Burma and northern Thailand.**

## **THE PIPELINE REACHES THE WORLD**

By the early 1970s, the question was no longer whether Golden Triangle heroin could reach Americans.

It was **who carried it after it left the mountains—and who controlled the places where it could have been stopped.**

That distinction matters. Poppies do not fly to New York. Opium does not stamp its own diplomatic passport. Heroin does not forge military cargo papers, pass an airport checkpoint, recruit a courier, board a merchant ship or decide that one investigation deserves help while another can wait.

People and institutions do those things.

And the files show that the export system did not depend on one route. It used **all of them**.

## **THE WAR HAD ALREADY BUILT THE SHIPPING LANES**

The clearest official map comes from [State Department testimony delivered in February 1972](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76ve01/d220). State described opium and morphine base moving out of Burma into Laos and Thailand for refining, then onward toward the large cities of Southeast Asia, American troops and—in growing quantities—the United States.

Some routes were almost embarrassingly simple.

### **Vientiane to Saigon**

State said Washington first focused on Laos after smugglers arriving from **Vientiane** were caught with heroin at **Tan Son Nhut Airport** in Saigon. In January 1972, Lao police acting on information developed by BNDD arrested a dealer who admitted supplying heroin seized there the year before. He had another **ten kilograms** when arrested.

That gives us a documented section of the chain:

**Vientiane supplier → commercial air route → Tan Son Nhut → American military market.**

### **Bangkok to U-Tapao to the United States**

The same testimony describes an even longer route. After months of work by Thai police and BNDD agents, authorities arrested ex-serviceman **Andrew Price** in Bangkok while **William Henry Jackson** and Sergeant **Jerald Ganius** were arrested in the United States. The case involved **17 pounds of high-purity No. 4 heroin** sent from Thailand through **U-Tapao Air Base** using forged military documentation.

The traffickers did not need to invent a cargo network.

**The war had supplied one.**

### **The APO Route**

Military mail offered another channel. On April 5, 1971, U.S. Customs officers at **Fort Monmouth, New Jersey**, seized roughly **7.7 kilograms** of Double U-O Globe heroin sent through the military postal system from Bangkok. The surviving local record is strongest in [McCoy's account](https://archive.org/details/politicsofheroin0000mcco) and the contemporary congressional discussion; the underlying Customs case file has not surfaced in this research set.

But the route itself is not mysterious:

**Bangkok → military mail → New Jersey.**

State's 1972 testimony ended its Asian survey by calling for tighter control of air and sea traffic and “adequate control” over the region's APOs.

Apparently somebody had noticed the mail problem.

### **Vientiane to New York**

McCoy reported another November 1971 case: a Filipino diplomat stationed in Vientiane and a Chinese merchant from Bangkok arrived in New York with **15.5 kilograms** of Double U-O heroin. U.S. narcotics agents had reportedly watched the movement and made the arrests in New York.

Again, the primary arrest and court files are missing from the acquired corpus. So the exact case remains attributed to McCoy.

But by then the broad system was visible in official records: commercial flights, military bases, military post, merchant vessels, fishing trawlers, returning servicemen and civilian couriers all linked Southeast Asia to overseas markets.

There was no single heroin superhighway.

There was a **transportation grid**.

## **THE WHOLESALERS WERE ALREADY WAITING**

The regional pipeline did not arrive in an empty world. It met criminal organizations that already knew how to move morphine base, refine heroin, bribe officials and distribute bulk shipments.

In Indochina, that meant Chinese trading networks and **Corsican syndicates** with connections running through Vientiane, Saigon and Marseille. In the United States, it meant organized crime figures looking for supply as Turkey came under pressure and the traditional French Connection became less secure.

Which brings us to **Santo Trafficante Jr.**

### **Trafficante: A Real CIA Relationship—and an Unproved Heroin Deal**

Trafficante's relationship with CIA is not rumor. The [Church Committee's assassination-plots report](https://www.intelligence.senate.gov/sites/default/files/94465.pdf) documented the Agency's use of Robert Maheu and Johnny Roselli to reach Sam Giancana and Trafficante during plots against Fidel Castro. CIA had dealt with a major American organized-crime figure when it believed he could be useful.

That relationship matters.

It also does **not** automatically turn every later Trafficante trip into a CIA narcotics operation.

McCoy documented that Trafficante traveled through **Hong Kong** in January 1968, visited Frank Furci—the son of a Tampa associate—and then continued to **Saigon**. McCoy wrote that a U.S. Embassy source believed Trafficante contacted Corsican syndicate leaders there. A later item placed in the [1973 Congressional Record](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/GPO-CRECB-1973-pt4/pdf/GPO-CRECB-1973-pt4-5-1.pdf) said federal narcotics agents had spotted Mafia figures in Saigon, Hong Kong, Singapore and Thailand and believed Asian connections were being developed to supplement French and Corsican suppliers.

That is the case **for** the trip as supply reconnaissance.

Now the part that complicates it.

Historian Jonathan Marshall traced the trip into the Senate military-club investigation and an FBI report dated February 26, 1969. His [review of those records](https://apjjf.org/2014/12/32/jonathan-marshall/4160/article) says federal investigators found no evidence that Trafficante and Dominick Furci were pursuing a narcotics conspiracy. They had around-the-world tickets; informants offered non-drug explanations; and Frank Furci reportedly did not want another visit because the attention harmed his reputation. The underlying FBI file is NARA record **124-10213-10207**.

So the honest finding is sharper than either slogan.

- Trafficante's CIA relationship is documented.
- His 1968 Hong Kong trip is documented.
- His movement onward to Saigon is well supported.
- Suspicion of a narcotics purpose was contemporaneous.
- A federal investigation reportedly found no evidence proving that purpose.
- No primary file found here shows Trafficante buying heroin from Vang Pao, meeting Vang Pao or concluding a CIA-assisted supply agreement.

**A connection is not the same thing as a transaction.**

And in this case, the transaction is the missing document.

## **THE CORSICAN BRIDGE**

The Corsican story is stronger as a route description, but it comes with its own evidentiary problem: much of the detail survives through interviews gathered by McCoy.

Former CIA officer **Lucien Conein** knew the Saigon underworld personally. In McCoy's account, Conein described Corsican family relationships connecting Saigon to Marseille. Corsican buyers in Saigon obtained morphine base through contacts in Vientiane, then shipped it on French merchant vessels to relatives and partners in Marseille for conversion into heroin.

Conein also alleged a 1969 meeting at Saigon's Continental Palace involving Corsican figures from **Marseille, Bangkok, Vientiane and Phnom Penh**. A U.S. diplomat reportedly told McCoy that the embassy had a Corsican informant and that similar meetings had occurred in other years.

Then there was **Edward Lansdale**.

Lansdale told McCoy that he had arranged “some kind of a truce” with Corsican leaders because he was not in Vietnam to run criminal investigations. He later learned they were deeply involved in narcotics and took no action because it was not his responsibility.

Read that again.

Not because it proves CIA ordered a heroin shipment. It doesn't.

Because it shows how mission priorities could work in practice. An intelligence officer could possess access to known criminal actors, decide criminal enforcement was outside the mission, and leave the information outside the narcotics bureau.

That is not central control of the trade.

It is **documented tolerance born of compartmentation and strategic priority**—precisely the environment in which traffickers thrive.

## **SIXTY KILOGRAMS AT ORLY**

The most dramatic attempt to connect Long Tieng to the international wholesale system remains the **Sopsaisana affair**.

On April 25, 1971, Prince **Sopsaisana**, Laos's ambassador-designate to France, arrived at Orly Airport. One suitcase did not reach the diplomatic reception. French Customs had intercepted it. Contemporary reporting and McCoy's account agree that it contained roughly **60 kilograms of high-grade heroin**. France refused to accept Sopsaisana's credentials, and he returned to Laos.

That much is solid enough.

The upstream chain is not.

McCoy wrote that diplomatic sources in Vientiane attributed the load to **Vang Pao** and a laboratory at **Long Tieng**. Sopsaisana denied knowing about it and publicly said political enemies had checked the suitcase in his name.

McCoy's [June 1972 congressional testimony](https://www.cia.gov/readingroom/docs/CIA-RDP74B00415R000400050035-1.pdf) adds a name absent from the old draft: **Michel Theodas**, manager of Vientiane's Lang Xang Hotel. McCoy said BNDD and diplomatic sources identified Theodas as the man who arranged the French connection.

That is a meaningful lead.

It is not yet a closed chain.

The surviving research has not produced the French Customs file, the French Foreign Ministry negotiations, a BNDD report naming Theodas, the Paris recipient, or a document proving that New York was the shipment's next destination. Nor has it produced a primary file proving the load came from Vang Pao or Long Tieng.

What the case proves is that a **bulk Lao heroin shipment reached Paris inside diplomatic luggage**.

What McCoy's sources allege is a longer chain:

**Long Tieng → Vang Pao → Sopsaisana → Theodas → Corsican receiver in France → American market.**

The middle and final links remain allegations, not acquired case-file findings.

Still, 60 kilograms is not a retail side hustle.

It is a wholesale shipment looking for a wholesale network.

## **THEN WASHINGTON BUILT THE CHOKEPOINTS**

By 1971, Washington understood that interdiction required more than catching a courier at the far end. Airports, customs posts, military bases, police intelligence units and checkpoints decided what moved and what stopped.

The [1972 State testimony](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76ve01/d220) describes the structure with unusual clarity. Every regional bureau created an interagency narcotics committee with representatives from **State, AID, BNDD, Customs, Defense, CIA and USIA**. More than fifty country teams were ordered to prepare action plans. The stated thrust was to increase local enforcement capacity and exchange intelligence with foreign governments.

In other words, the response to an international heroin pipeline was an international **police-and-intelligence pipeline**.

And in Laos, USAID sat inside it.

## **USAID'S NARCOTICS PROGRAM WAS NOT SMALL**

The August 1974 [Vientiane Airgram A-102](https://foia.state.gov/DOCUMENTS/FOIA_Micro_Oct2024_10/F-1989-03959/DOC_0C09000035/C09000035.pdf) is much more revealing than the few statistics previously pulled from it.

The mission said the Lao narcotics program had reached roughly **$2 million in 1973** and was projected at **$1.6 million in FY1974**. The American staff had once numbered about **thirty** and had been selectively reduced to roughly **ten**.

It also admitted something important: the program's goal had **never** been to eliminate all narcotics production or trafficking in Laos. The mission considered that objective too expensive for a country it then described as only remotely connected to the continuing U.S. narcotics problem.

That was not a confession of protection.

It was a written statement that enforcement was being calibrated to American priorities.

The same record counted **711 Lao personnel** trained in-country across the National Police, Military Police, Customs, the Groupe Spécial d'Investigation and the judiciary. Another **21 people** had received U.S. or third-country participant training.

USAID was not running every arrest. But it was financing advisers, participant training, vehicles, motorcycles, communications equipment, records work and institutional design.

Call that what the documents support:

**capacity, dependency and leverage.**

Not absolute control.

## **THE 163 MILITARY POLICE WERE POLICING THEIR OWN ARMY**

The sharpest police/trafficking overlap appears on page 16 of the Airgram.

The Royal Lao Army's Provost Marshal General had reorganized its criminal and investigation division to include a narcotics-control section. **One hundred sixty-three military policemen** had received initial training through USAID narcotics seminars and formal classes at the Lao National Police Academy. **Seven Lao military police** had received narcotics training in the United States.

Their objective was explicit: eliminate trafficking by **Royal Lao Army officials**, the use of **military transportation** for trafficking and narcotics consumption by army personnel.

The plan called for military anti-narcotics police to help operate irregular mobile checkpoints. It listed possible U.S. training at the Army Military Police School, the International Police Academy and DEA's training school.

So the contradiction was not hidden.

The same allied military system supplied accused traffickers, transport and the police assigned to stop them.

USAID helped train the police.

The seven U.S.-trained officers have not been identified. Neither have their individual schools or later postings. The document gives an aggregate, not a roster.

But the institution is clear enough.

## **THE INTERNATIONAL POLICE ACADEMY BECAME PART OF THE DRUG WAR**

FBN and USAID had been building this channel for years. Treasury's official reports for [1966](https://fraser.stlouisfed.org/files/docs/publications/treasar/AR_TREASURY_1966.pdf), [1967](https://fraser.stlouisfed.org/files/docs/publications/treasar/AR_TREASURY_1967.pdf) and [1968](https://fraser.stlouisfed.org/files/docs/publications/treasar/AR_TREASURY_1968.pdf) document repeated narcotics-control instruction at the AID International Police Academy. The 1966 course alone brought nineteen Latin American officers through two weeks on cocaine, heroin and marijuana.

By FY1971, the [Justice Department](https://ojp.gov/pdffiles1/Digitization/9474NCJRS.pdf) described OPS as a “unique U.S. asset” for building whole police institutions around narcotics enforcement. It reported:

- **3,932 IPA graduates** from **73 countries**;
- graduates commanding **13 national police agencies**; and
- academy graduates and OPS advisers serving as valuable overseas contacts for Justice Department representatives.

The thirteen agencies and chiefs are not listed. This investigation did not manufacture names to fill the gap.

Then, on December 20, 1974, **PMNO No. 8** graduated. The [*Police Academy Review*](https://ojp.gov/pdffiles1/Digitization/19349NCJRS.pdf) says the class included 27 officers from nine countries—including Laos, Thailand, Malaysia and the Philippines. The issue names the commencement speaker, Lebanese Major George Noujaim, but not all 27 participants.

By that graduation, IPA claimed **5,204 graduates from 77 countries**.

This did not prove every graduate became a U.S. asset.

It proved something more ordinary and more durable: Washington had built a transnational alumni and liaison network at the exact moment narcotics enforcement was becoming a global mission.

## **CIA HAD ITS OWN POLICE DOOR—AND THEN CAME TWO-FOLD**

Part 2 documented CIA's Police Group liaison with OPS, participant-information exchanges, biographies, assessments and selected sponsorship. That channel did not make every IPA graduate CIA-controlled. It gave CIA a way to see, assess and sometimes sponsor people moving through the overt police program.

The new downstream question was whether CIA also had access inside the American narcotics agency.

The answer is **yes**.

The document is [Project TWO-FOLD](https://www.cia.gov/readingroom/docs/DOC_0001451843.pdf).

In December 1970, BNDD director **Robert Ingersoll** went to CIA director Richard Helms with a problem. Ingersoll believed the old Federal Bureau of Narcotics had been infiltrated by dishonest or corrupt personnel connected to the narcotics-smuggling industry.

CIA's Office of Security responded by **spotting, assessing and recruiting personnel** for a BNDD internal-security unit. Once selected and trained, those people were turned over to BNDD's Chief Inspector for domestic assignments.

Then the program expanded.

CIA helped BNDD covertly acquire staff agents under nonofficial cover for work against principal international drug traffickers. They were genuine BNDD employees. CIA handled the administrative details. According to the memo, **the recruits did not know CIA was involved**.

In May 1973, William Colby recommended terminating the domestic internal-corruption portion while continuing the foreign branch for narcotics intelligence abroad.

This is the strongest evidence in the downstream record for **CIA access to narcotics enforcement**.

It is not evidence that CIA directed BNDD to protect Trafficante, Vang Pao, the Corsicans or Sopsaisana. The surviving memo does not name the recruits, countries, investigations or targets. It does not show whether CIA retained contact once BNDD began operational handling.

But it establishes the mechanism:

**CIA secretly helped select personnel inside the agency charged with investigating international narcotics.**

That is no longer a hypothesis.

## **SO WAS ENFORCEMENT SELECTIVE?**

At the structural level, yes.

All enforcement is selective when resources are finite. The real question is what drove the selection.

The record shows at least four pressures:

1. **War priorities.** Lansdale's own account placed Corsican criminal investigation outside his mission. McCoy's BNDD sources said State and CIA initially resisted a Vientiane narcotics office because pressure on important Lao figures could damage the war effort.
2. **Official corruption.** BNDD asked CIA for help because its director believed the predecessor bureau had been penetrated by people tied to smugglers. The Lao military-police program existed partly because Royal Lao Army officials and military transport were implicated.
3. **Institutional access.** OPS, IPA, BNDD, Customs, DEA and CIA each possessed liaison, training, personnel or intelligence channels touching the same enforcement systems.
4. **Declared limits.** The Vientiane mission wrote that total elimination was not the goal and calibrated the program to American priorities, budgets and Laos's perceived relevance to the U.S. market.

That is powerful evidence for **constrained and uneven enforcement**.

It is not the same as a documented order saying: protect this allied trafficker and arrest his competitor.

Official records also show real enforcement—seizures, arrests, BNDD-developed intelligence, checkpoint plans and refinery disruption. TWO-FOLD's foreign agents were directed against major international traffickers. CIA expanded collection on overseas heroin systems. Thai and Lao police sometimes made the cases.

The files therefore do not support a cartoon in which every seizure was theater and every narcotics officer worked for CIA.

They support a more unsettling system: officials fighting narcotics through institutions entangled with the military, political and intelligence relationships that helped the trade survive.

The guard dog was real.

**So were some of the people feeding it.**

## **TACHILEK: SUPPRESSION OR COMPETITION?**

The earlier CIA proposal to use Li and Tuan's opium-financed Chinese Irregular Forces against heroin refineries near Tachilek deserves a direct answer.

McCoy's account identifies the Tachilek zone as a cluster of **KKY and Shan militia refineries**. It names private refinery routes associated with Kokang trafficker **Law Sik Han—better known as Lo Hsing-han**—Wa leader Bo Loi Oo and militia commander Yang Sun. A 1971 CIA estimate counted fourteen laboratories in the area.

Those operators were not simply branches of Li and Tuan's CIF. They occupied a competing processing zone while the CIF remained deeply involved in moving raw opium toward Thailand and Laos.

Destroying those laboratories could therefore do two things at once:

- reduce heroin-processing capacity; and
- damage processors competing with the force selected to attack them.

The competitive benefit is not imaginary.

The motive remains unproved.

No located U.S. memo says market consolidation was the purpose, and the record does not show that Lo Hsing-han owned every target. The best finding is a **mixed-interest operation**: a genuine anti-narcotics objective executed through traffickers who could benefit economically from the result.

That is not a contradiction in the Golden Triangle.

It was practically a business model.

## **CONGRESS CLOSED THE PROGRAM AFTER IT WAS ALREADY CLOSING**

Section 660 did not strike a thriving OPS out of a clear blue sky.

Congress had already acted. The **Foreign Assistance Act of 1973** barred the classic AID police program after existing commitments, and AID began phasing out seventeen country programs and ninety-five overseas advisers, a chronology later tested in [GAO's contemporaneous implementation review](https://www.gao.gov/products/id-76-5). By August 1974, the Vientiane mission was already describing a smaller U.S. staff and planning to end OPS narcotics assistance at the close of FY1975.

Then Congress enacted [Public Law 93-559](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/STATUTE-88/pdf/STATUTE-88-Pg1795.pdf) on December 30, 1974. Section 660 became effective on **July 1, 1975**, broadly barring Foreign Assistance Act support to foreign police, prisons, other law-enforcement forces and internal-intelligence or surveillance programs.

But the original law preserved important doors:

- international narcotics-control assistance under Foreign Assistance Act section 482;
- assistance related to DEA and FBI authority over conduct unlawful in the United States; and
- completion of qualifying pre-enactment personnel contracts.

The narcotics exception was not added years later.

It was there from the beginning.

And the Lao planning record shows how the transition could work. OPS assistance to the National Police and Military Police would end; **DEA would assume narcotics advisory responsibility**. GSI, trained officers, records, radios, vehicles, checkpoints and personal relationships did not vanish because a statutory clock reached July 1.

The academy's December 1974 graduating issue claimed 5,204 alumni across 77 countries. DOJ had already described graduates and overseas advisers as useful contacts.

Congress ended an office and sharply restricted a funding route.

It did not erase the people.

## **WHAT THE DOCUMENTS ACTUALLY SUPPORT**

The strongest version of this story is not that CIA sat in a room directing the global heroin trade.

The files do not show that.

They show something institutional—and harder to dismiss.

Golden Triangle heroin entered world markets through commercial aviation, military bases, military mail, diplomatic luggage, merchant shipping and courier organizations. Existing Corsican and American criminal networks had the experience to absorb it. Some figures in that underworld, including Trafficante, had prior relationships with CIA for other operations. CIA officers possessed access to Corsican circles. U.S.-backed Lao and regional military actors were repeatedly implicated in production, transport or protection.

Then Washington built the enforcement response through the same political landscape.

USAID/OPS trained and equipped police. IPA created an international contact network. BNDD and Customs supplied advisers and intelligence. CIA maintained foreign-police channels and secretly helped recruit personnel into BNDD through Project TWO-FOLD. When broad police aid was shut down, narcotics authorities remained legally open and DEA was already scheduled to inherit specific advisory work.

That architecture created enormous capacity to influence chokepoints.

The record proves access, overlap, dependency and selective priorities. It documents cases in which strategic missions displaced criminal investigation. It also documents genuine enforcement.

What it does **not** yet provide is the missing person-level chain:

**named CIA-connected trafficker → named protected police officer → named CIA handler → named shipment allowed through.**

Until that file appears, the conclusion should not outrun the evidence.

But neither should the evidence be domesticated.

Washington fought a heroin emergency by building relationships with the people guarding airports, customs houses, police files and military checkpoints—while maintaining strategic relationships with actors accused of moving the product through those same systems.

That is not proof of one master conspiracy.

It is proof of a system in which **national-security value could decide what enforcement was allowed to see.**

## **FAQ**

### **Did CIA control the Golden Triangle heroin trade?**

The located documents do not establish central CIA control of production, wholesale distribution or a complete shipment into the United States. They do establish CIA relationships with regional actors implicated in narcotics, access to Corsican figures, a prior operational relationship with Trafficante, and covert CIA assistance recruiting personnel into BNDD.

### **What is the strongest evidence that enforcement could be selective?**

The strongest combination is the Vientiane mission's deliberately limited program, McCoy's participant accounts of criminal intelligence not reaching FBN, the Royal Lao military-police unit policing trafficking inside its own army, and Project TWO-FOLD's covert CIA role in selecting BNDD personnel. That proves capacity and uneven priorities; it does not identify a protected end-to-end shipment.

### **Did Santo Trafficante buy heroin from Vang Pao?**

No acquired primary document proves that. Trafficante's CIA relationship and Asian travel are documented, and contemporaries suspected supply-building. But an FBI investigation reportedly found no narcotics-conspiracy evidence behind his 1968 trip, and no file found here records a Trafficante–Vang Pao meeting or transaction.

### **What does the Sopsaisana case actually prove?**

It strongly supports that roughly 60 kilograms of heroin reached Orly in the Lao ambassador-designate's luggage and that France refused his credentials. McCoy's sources alleged a Vang Pao/Long Tieng origin and named Michel Theodas as the French-connection broker. Those upstream and onward links remain unverified by an acquired French, BNDD, State or CIA case file.

### **Did Section 660 end the foreign-police network?**

It ended and restricted important AID police-assistance authorities, following a phaseout already underway since 1973. It did not erase thousands of graduates, advisers, equipment or liaison relationships. The original law also preserved international narcotics assistance and specified DEA/FBI channels; in Laos, OPS had already planned a narcotics handoff to DEA.
