# PART 3 ALTERNATIVE EXPLANATIONS

## 1. “These were independent traffickers CIA merely happened to know.”

**WHAT WOULD HAVE TO BE TRUE:** Contacts would be incidental; relationships would end or enforcement would proceed once credible allegations emerged; transport and political support would not materially improve trafficking resilience; no reporting or prosecutorial anomaly would track intelligence value.

**TEST AGAINST THE RECORD:**

- Repeated relationships: present in Laos and Contra cases.
- Continued use after allegations: present in selected cases.
- Transport access: central in Laos; not shown as CIA-controlled in Contra cases.
- Intelligence reporting: knowledge documented.
- Law-enforcement anomalies: selected examples documented, general pattern disputed.
- Financial benefit: limited Contra contributions and proxy-economy benefit supported.

**VERDICT:** Explains autonomous profit motive and some proximity. It does not fully explain persistent strategic tolerance. Best synthesis: independent traffickers operated inside relationships U.S. agencies sometimes preserved.

## 2. “The drug trade predated U.S. intervention, so intervention did not facilitate it.”

**WHAT WOULD HAVE TO BE TRUE:** U.S. sponsorship, air access, territorial security, and political protection would have no material effect on volume, reliability, reach, or enforcement risk.

**TEST AGAINST THE RECORD:** Preexistence is unquestioned. Yet Air America transformed access to remote territory, CIA sponsorship concentrated military power, and officials deprioritized enforcement.

**VERDICT:** Strong against an “America created opium” claim; weak against facilitation, expansion, or resilience.

## 3. “Air America could not police every passenger, so any drugs aboard were accidental.”

**WHAT WOULD HAVE TO BE TRUE:** Controls were reasonably designed and applied; managers lacked specific notice; allegations were investigated; implicated personnel/allies faced consequences; carriage was rare and unauthorized.

**TEST AGAINST THE RECORD:** Official history supports practical inspection limits and denies knowing policy. McCoy offers multiple allegations; CIA knew the general problem and did little.

**VERDICT:** Plausible for individual loads and strongest direct-participation defense. It does not answer why the surrounding allied narcotics economy was tolerated.

## 4. “OPS was ordinary professional police assistance.”

**WHAT WOULD HAVE TO BE TRUE:** Curriculum, organizational design, equipment, and adviser work were predominantly common-crime functions; political intelligence and counter-subversion were marginal; CIA access was exceptional.

**TEST AGAINST THE RECORD:** Ordinary functions were substantial, but documents explicitly place police on the first line against communism, build internal-security information, and establish CIA liaison/referral routes.

**VERDICT:** Partly true but incomplete. Professionalization and political-security purpose coexisted.

## 5. “Brazilian repression was entirely indigenous; U.S. aid is irrelevant.”

**WHAT WOULD HAVE TO BE TRUE:** Assisted training/equipment did not reach repressive institutions or add useful capacity; Brazilian methods and scale were unchanged; U.S. political support had no effect on institutional confidence or resources.

**TEST AGAINST THE RECORD:** Brazil had a long coercive tradition and local agency. OPS nevertheless supplied the very communications, mobility, records, laboratories, and coordination that modernization sought to expand.

**VERDICT:** Strong against claims that the United States invented Brazilian repression. It fails to negate material capacity contribution.

## 6. “U.S. concern about torture proves the United States did not tolerate repression.”

**WHAT WOULD HAVE TO BE TRUE:** Concern produced timely conditionality, relationship termination, or effective constraints; abusive institutions lost access and support.

**TEST AGAINST THE RECORD:** Diplomats criticized abuse and reduced/ended OPS staffing, but stronger action was avoided and strategic relations continued amid documented systematic torture.

**VERDICT:** Shows genuine disagreement and reputational concern, not absence of tolerance. Policy was conflicted and limited.

## 7. “Webb was wrong, therefore Contra-drug allegations were baseless.”

**WHAT WOULD HAVE TO BE TRUE:** Blandón/Meneses were not major traffickers, made no Contra contributions, government lacked knowledge, and reported anomalies did not occur.

**TEST AGAINST THE RECORD:** DOJ’s own report confirms the first two and documents Zavala intervention and other failures, while rejecting epidemic causation and general protection.

**VERDICT:** False dichotomy. Webb’s broad framing overreached; the narrow institutional pattern remains.

## 8. “Bureaucratic incompetence explains every law-enforcement anomaly.”

**WHAT WOULD HAVE TO BE TRUE:** Anomalies would not systematically favor politically useful relationships; intelligence agencies would not affirmatively intervene; reporting gaps would be neutral in effect; comparable ordinary cases would show similar outcomes.

**TEST AGAINST THE RECORD:** Disorganization and jurisdiction explain many episodes. The Zavala intervention and discretionary reporting structure show that intelligence equities also mattered.

**VERDICT:** Major competing explanation, probably correct for many individual failures, insufficient as a universal account.

## 9. “Because no order says ‘traffic drugs,’ there was no intentional facilitation.”

**WHAT WOULD HAVE TO BE TRUE:** Intent can only be proved by confession rather than repeated decisions, known consequences, preserved relationships, and designed reporting rules.

**TEST AGAINST THE RECORD:** Covert systems rarely state illicit purpose plainly. Still, inference requires a continuous pattern and identifiable benefit, not just absence of a memo.

**VERDICT:** Too narrow as a method. But the present record still does not reach intentional U.S.-bound trafficking because crucial links are absent and contrary findings are substantial.

## 10. “The CIA ran the entire drug trade through proxies.”

**WHAT WOULD HAVE TO BE TRUE:** Major producers, transporters, financiers, and distributors would be controlled or knowingly coordinated; drug proceeds would be tracked into operations; enforcement anomalies would be systematic; official contrary evidence would be false or incomplete across the board.

**TEST AGAINST THE RECORD:** Local actors display autonomous motives; markets predate and outlast covert programs; official reviews find no institutional trafficking policy; Brazil chain is missing.

**VERDICT:** Does not fit the total record. The evidence supports enabling, tolerance, and episodic protection more strongly than centralized control.

## 11. “Brazil belongs only as a Cold War repression case, not in a drug-pipeline article.”

**WHAT WOULD HAVE TO BE TRUE:** The article’s frame must concern only commodity movement, and security/information/political pipelines are irrelevant to protection mechanisms.

**TEST AGAINST THE RECORD:** Brazil directly tests how overt aid and covert political action construct an aligned security state—the proposed mechanism that could protect routes. But the narcotics-specific chain remains incomplete.

**VERDICT:** Brazil belongs as the laboratory of access, alignment, and coercive capacity, with the cocaine gap stated openly. It should not carry claims it cannot prove.

## 12. “All overlapping programs were one coordinated operation.”

**WHAT WOULD HAVE TO BE TRUE:** shared command, objectives, targeting, reporting, personnel, and resources would connect CIA political action, OPS, military contingency, and drug networks.

**TEST AGAINST THE RECORD:** Overall U.S. policy coordination and compatible outcomes are documented; operational integration is not.

**VERDICT:** Overstated. “Parallel and mutually reinforcing” best fits Brazil; “functionally integrated” best fits parts of the Laos war economy.

## AE-09 — Allegations were retained because they were unsubstantiated, not because actors were protected

**TEST AGAINST THE RECORD:** This explains some OIG decisions and is supported by referrals, terminations, and conflicting intelligence. It does not fully explain why allegation resolution was deprioritized, why essential providers remained in use for extended periods, why Congress was incompletely informed, or why a later prosecution concern focused on exposing a CIA connection.

**VERDICT:** Material partial explanation; insufficient as a complete cross-case account.

## AE-10 — Weak states and preexisting trade, not U.S. intervention, caused the trafficking

**TEST AGAINST THE RECORD:** Opium/cocaine systems predated the covert programs and local actors retained agency. Yet U.S. sponsorship changed territorial survival, aviation access, political protection, contractor demand, and enforcement incentives. Causation is contributory rather than originary.

**VERDICT:** Strong alternative to “CIA created the trade”; compatible with “U.S. systems materially enabled or preserved it.”

## AE-11 — Classified-information protection explains enforcement anomalies

**TEST AGAINST THE RECORD:** Source/method protection and prosecutorial viability legitimately explain caution in Hyde, Hull, Noriega, and Bueso-related matters. But when secrecy consistently protects operational relationships at the expense of drug enforcement, the distinction between legitimate compartmentation and functional protection narrows.

**VERDICT:** Strong case-level explanation; does not erase the protection effect.

## AE-12 — The pattern is fragmented bureaucracy, not intentional architecture

**TEST AGAINST THE RECORD:** Different agencies did conflict, some referrals occurred, and there is no single command document. The repeated hierarchy of priorities—covert utility first, narcotics resolution second—nevertheless appears across different systems.

**VERDICT:** Best argument against a centrally designed drug system; weaker against the inference of a knowingly preserved covert architecture.
