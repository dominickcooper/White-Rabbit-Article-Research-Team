*AKA: Drug running? Not the CIA.*
But the same inquiry also found that opium could—and did—get aboard, that local passengers loaded cargo beyond the airline’s full control, and that the CIA knew allied figures and territory were entangled with narcotics.
Suppressing that trade ranked below the war.

## **1962: STAY IN THE BACKGROUND**

Before we get to the opium, we need to go back to **1962**.
On **July 23**, fourteen nations signed [**the Geneva agreements**](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Agreement_on_the_Neutrality_of_Laos) declaring Laos neutral. The accords required the withdrawal of foreign regular troops, irregular troops, paramilitary formations, and military personnel. Washington complied publicly by withdrawing its military advisory teams. But North Vietnam did not fully withdraw its forces, and the United States continued supporting **Lao and Hmong forces** after the agreement.
**That created an obvious problem.**
***How do you keep fighting a war*** 
*in a country where you’re not supposed to be fighting a war?*
A month later, on **August 24, 1962**, President **John F. Kennedy** approved [**NSAM 182**](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1961-63v08/d105), establishing a government-wide doctrine for what Washington called **“Overseas Internal Defense.”** 
This was national policy for **State, Defense, AID, USIA, and CIA**, distributed to American missions and military commands around the world.
The September policy paper that followed was remarkably candid about how this kind of war should work.
American involvement, it said, should be **“ancillary to the indigenous effort.”** The United States should **“remain in the background”** and, whenever possible, restrict its visible role to training, advice, and materiel. 
The objective was to build **local forces** capable of fighting the internal war themselves while **minimizing overt American intervention**.
Plain English?
***Keep our hands clean.***
And that doctrine wasn’t limited to soldiers.
The document identified **police as the first line of defense against subversion and insurgency**. 
*Remember part 2 of this series??*
Washington would provide **friendly foreign police** with equipment, training, and technical assistance—and the normal channel for doing so would be **AID**, although Defense or CIA could take the role depending on the circumstances. 
For indigenous paramilitary forces, 
the policy explicitly called for Defense to work 
**“in collaboration with AID and CIA.”**

## **THE GOLDEN TRIANGLE**

Now, Northern Laos already had an opium economy. The mountain communities cultivated a portable cash crop in terrain where roads, buyers and alternative markets were scarce; Chinese networks, merchants, soldiers and officials moved it through a regional trade. 
**Very agrarian.** Very simple.
Then roads, aviation, military support, USAID, and covert alliances entered the same valleys.
*What are the chances?*
One of the most discussed accounts from that region has to do with [**Edgar “Pop” Buell**](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pop_Buell).
He was an aid worker through the [International Voluntary Services](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Voluntary_Services) (a precursor to the **Peace Corps**) who became a legendary figure among Hmong communities. In [According to Don Schanche’s telling](https://archive.org/details/misterpop00scha), Buell first tried to replace poppy cultivation with sweet potatoes. 
The crop grew. The market did not.
Villagers lacked transport and dependable buyers. And then there was shelf life. Sweet potatoes spoiled; opium did not. 
**Plus opium was much more lucrative.** 
A small amount of opium could be carried across difficult terrain and exchanged for cash in a way bulky food crops could not. So the substitution failed because the surrounding economy offered no workable substitute market.

### **“If You’re Gonna Grow It, Grow It Good”**

Now Buell **wasn't a random white man** in a remote village. The  [International Voluntary Services](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Voluntary_Services) he was a part of received 70-90% of its funding from USAID.

**American medicines** reduced some of the medicinal reasons villagers consumed opium, while production continued to rise. **Less local use** then left **more of the crop** available for sale. 
The crop remained, and so did **the need for a buyer.**
***Enter CIA.***

## **BOOTS ON THE GROUND: ALFRED McCOY’S ACCOUNT**

Now Alfred McCoy, unlike Don Sanache, was not simply writing about the drug trade during the Vietnam war. 
**He was actually in it.** 
There are several observations and interviews (done **during the war**, mind you) and they were all put down in his book “[The Politics of Heroin in Southeast Asia](https://archive.org/details/politicsofheroin0000mcco).”  
McCoy’s interviews describe buyers, prices, packing methods and seasonal routines.

### **Before the Helicopters**

In the Long Pot area, McCoy reported that **Hmong officers** entered villages to test and purchase opium. 
**Hmong.** 
The side that the US was helping. (*for clarity*)
The Hmong officers were paying **$60 per kilogram**, compared with **$40 to $50** offered by other merchants. The purchased opium went into bamboo containers, onto ponies and toward [**Long Tieng**](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Long_Tieng) **in Laos**. Collection circuits could repeat two or three times during a season.
Village-to-village buying, consolidation and pack-animal movement linked producing communities to the military center.

### **Then the Helicopters Arrived**

**Ger Su Yang**, a village leader [interviewed by McCoy in 1971](https://archive.org/details/politicsofheroin0000mcco), described a change in 1969 and 1970. The officers no longer needed to ride the whole route. They arrived **by helicopter**, spent days moving among villages, then radioed Long Tieng for pickup.

> *“Meo officers with three or four stripes [captain or major) came from Long Tieng to buy our opium.* 
>
> ***They came in American helicopters,*** *perhaps two or three men at one time.* 
>
> *The helicopter leaves them here for a few days and they walk to villages over there (swinging his arm in a semicircle in the direction of Gier Goot, Long Makkhay and Nam Pac], then come back here and radioed Long Tieng to send another helicopter for them.* 
>
> ***They take the opium back to Long Tieng****.”*
>
> *—* Ger Su Yang Interview

The use of helicopters (specifically American ones) was further corrobated to McCoy by [**General Ouane Rattikone**](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ouane_Rattikone) and Former Royal Lao Air Force commander [**Thao Ma**](https://vi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thao_Ma)**.**

[A later CIA assessment](https://www.cia.gov/readingroom/docs/CIA-RDP86T01017R000808060002-7.pdf?utm_source=chatgpt.com) described the 3rd and 5th Armies as the region’s dominant opium traffickers—and noted that **intelligence networks they had already built in Burma were well suited to distributing opium throughout eastern Burma and northern Thailand.**

## **THE PIPELINE REACHES THE WORLD**

By the early 1970s, the question was no longer whether Golden Triangle heroin could reach Americans.

It was **how it was getting there**.

The answer was: almost any way it could.

[State Department testimony from 1972](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76ve01/d220) described heroin moving from **Vientiane to Tan Son Nhut Airport** in Saigon, where it reached the enormous market created by the American war. The same testimony documented **17 pounds of high-purity No. 4 heroin** routed from Thailand through **U-Tapao Air Base** toward the United States using forged military paperwork.

Then there was the mail.

On April 5, 1971, Customs officers at **Fort Monmouth, New Jersey**, seized roughly **7.7 kilograms** of Double U-O Globe heroin sent from Bangkok through the military postal system. McCoy also reported a **15.5-kilogram Vientiane-to-New York shipment** later that year involving a Filipino diplomat and a Chinese merchant from Bangkok. [His account](https://archive.org/details/politicsofheroin0000mcco) traced the couriers all the way to their arrests in New York.

Commercial flights worked. Military bases worked. Military mail worked. Fishing trawlers and merchant vessels worked. Returning servicemen and civilian couriers worked.

[CIA analysis of the regional traffic](https://www.cia.gov/readingroom/docs/CIA-RDP80-01601R000300170027-5.pdf) described opium and morphine base moving south toward **Bangkok**, then leaving for **Hong Kong** aboard fishing trawlers. Other loads moved through **Vientiane and Luang Prabang** toward Saigon and international markets. Once the product reached those cities, it entered ports, airports and commercial systems designed to move people and cargo across the world.

And diplomatic baggage worked too.

On April 25, 1971, **Prince Sopsaisana**, Laos's ambassador-designate to France, arrived at Orly Airport with a suitcase containing roughly **60 kilograms of high-grade heroin**. France refused his credentials and sent him home. McCoy's sources alleged that the shipment came from **Vang Pao and Long Tieng**, and his [congressional testimony](https://www.cia.gov/readingroom/docs/CIA-RDP74B00415R000400050035-1.pdf) identified **Michel Theodas**, manager of Vientiane's Lang Xang Hotel, as the alleged French-connection intermediary. Sopsaisana denied knowing the heroin was there.

Whatever its precise origin, the suitcase makes one point beautifully clear:

**A bulk Lao heroin shipment had reached Europe.**

By then the regional pipeline was feeding transit points in **Saigon, Bangkok, Hong Kong, Paris, Marseille, and New York**. The product no longer needed one perfect route. It had entered a global transportation system built for war, commerce, diplomacy and ordinary travel.

## **THE DISTRIBUTORS WERE ALREADY THERE**

Nobody had to invent an international criminal market from scratch.

Corsican syndicates already connected **Vientiane and Saigon to Marseille**. Chinese trafficking networks already moved narcotics through Hong Kong. European organizations knew how to refine morphine base, arrange shipping and wholesale heroin. American organized crime already had distributors waiting at the other end.

This is an important distinction. The covert-war system did not need to run street sales in New York or build a criminal syndicate in Marseille. It only had to deliver narcotics into places where professional traffickers already had brokers, chemists, ships, couriers, corrupt contacts and customers.

Former CIA officer **Lucien Conein** told McCoy that Corsican contacts in Vientiane supplied buyers in Saigon, who used French merchant vessels and family connections to move morphine base toward Marseille. Conein knew those circles personally. **Edward Lansdale** separately told McCoy that he had arranged “some kind of a truce” with Corsican leaders because criminal investigation was not his mission.

*Well, that was convenient.*

Then there was **Santo Trafficante Jr.** The [Church Committee](https://www.intelligence.senate.gov/sites/default/files/94465.pdf) documented his earlier relationship with CIA through the plots against Fidel Castro. In 1968, Trafficante traveled through Hong Kong and onward to Saigon while U.S. officials were worried that organized crime was developing Asian supply connections. A later [congressional account](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/GPO-CRECB-1973-pt4/pdf/GPO-CRECB-1973-pt4-5-1.pdf) described federal narcotics agents reaching the same suspicion. The FBI investigated the trip and established no narcotics conspiracy.

So no, the file does not give us a CIA-directed Mafia heroin deal.

It gives us something simpler: established criminal wholesalers, intelligence officers with access to their world, and a major Mafia figure with a prior CIA relationship traveling through the cities where Asian supply connections were being built.

The growers existed.

The buyers existed.

The aircraft, roads and refineries existed.

The international wholesalers existed.

But there was still one problem.

***What about the police?***

## **THE POLICE ACADEMY REAPPEARS—THIS TIME IN THE DRUG WAR**

Remember all those foreign police officers from Part 2?

**They're back.**

By FY1971, the [Justice Department](https://ojp.gov/pdffiles1/Digitization/9474NCJRS.pdf) reported that the International Police Academy had trained **3,932 officers from 73 countries**. Graduates commanded **13 national police agencies**. And DOJ openly described IPA graduates and overseas OPS advisers as valuable contacts for Justice Department representatives.

Three years later, the academy's own [*Police Academy Review*](https://ojp.gov/pdffiles1/Digitization/19349NCJRS.pdf) claimed **5,204 graduates from 77 countries**.

Thousands of officers had passed through the school in Washington and returned to police institutions around the world.

Narcotics instruction was already part of that machinery. Treasury reports for [1966](https://fraser.stlouisfed.org/files/docs/publications/treasar/AR_TREASURY_1966.pdf), [1967](https://fraser.stlouisfed.org/files/docs/publications/treasar/AR_TREASURY_1967.pdf) and [1968](https://fraser.stlouisfed.org/files/docs/publications/treasar/AR_TREASURY_1968.pdf) show the Federal Bureau of Narcotics repeatedly teaching at the AID academy. Police training and drug enforcement were not two separate stories that happened to collide in Laos. Washington had been joining them for years.

That was the overt system.

CIA maintained its own door into the same world.

Part 2 documented the Agency's Police Group, its liaison with OPS and IPA, the exchange of participant information, biographies and assessments, and the sponsorship of selected foreign officers. CIA-sponsored participants could enter the overt training stream. Selected officers could receive additional CIA instruction or be assessed for possible operational use.

Not every IPA graduate worked for CIA.

CIA did not need every graduate.

It needed access to the system, information about the people moving through it, and a way to reach the ones it wanted.

That is why the police story was never a tangent.

It was setup.

## **LAOS BUILDS A NARCOTICS POLICE SYSTEM**

Heroin arriving from Vientiane helped force Washington to pay attention to Laos. With encouragement from the U.S. Mission, the Royal Lao Government created a narcotics-control program. **USAID/OPS, BNDD and Customs** helped build it.

The August 1974 [Vientiane program record](https://foia.state.gov/DOCUMENTS/FOIA_Micro_Oct2024_10/F-1989-03959/DOC_0C09000035/C09000035.pdf) counted **711 Lao personnel** trained across the National Police, Military Police, Customs, the Groupe Spécial d'Investigation and the judiciary.

The program had reached roughly **$2 million in 1973**. USAID supplied more than classroom instruction: the plan included advisers, participant training, vehicles, motorcycles, communications equipment and records work. That did not give Washington command of every Lao officer. It did give Washington a material relationship with the agencies expected to control narcotics routes.

Inside the Royal Lao Army, the Provost Marshal General created a narcotics-control section. **One hundred sixty-three military police** received initial narcotics training. **Seven** received narcotics training in the United States, with possible destinations including the U.S. Army Military Police School, the International Police Academy and DEA's training school.

And what were these military police supposed to investigate?

The document says it plainly: trafficking by **Royal Lao Army officials** and the use of **military transportation** to move narcotics.

Just sit with that for a moment.

The same U.S.-supported security establishment surrounding the war—and many of the trafficking allegations—was now being trained by the United States to police the drug trade inside itself.

## **WHO GUARDS THE GATES?**

A global heroin pipeline needs more than farmers, pilots and gangsters.

It needs **gatekeepers**.

Customs officers decide which suitcase gets opened. Airport police decide which passenger gets stopped. Military police decide which vehicle gets searched. Narcotics investigators decide which allegation becomes a case. Intelligence officers decide which information reaches law enforcement—and which problem remains inside an intelligence file.

That is the hidden power of the chokepoint. A trafficking organization does not need every officer on the payroll. It needs warning at the right airport, a friendly commander at the right checkpoint, or an allegation to land on the desk of someone who considers another mission more important.

Informants can be useful and still commit crimes. Allied commanders can be strategically indispensable and still have narcotics allegations hanging over them. A police unit can seize one shipment while never getting close to the political network behind another.

Washington had spent years building relationships with the institutions standing at those gates.

USAID funded advisers, training, vehicles, communications equipment and records systems. IPA trained thousands of officers. DOJ viewed graduates as useful overseas contacts. CIA maintained separate liaison, assessment and sponsorship channels. BNDD, Customs and later DEA connected those institutions to the international drug war.

None of those relationships automatically produced protection. But together they shaped who exchanged intelligence, who trained whom, and which Americans could pick up a telephone overseas and reach a familiar police official. That is influence in its most practical form—not ownership, but access.

Then the CIA files add one more detail.

Under [Project TWO-FOLD](https://www.cia.gov/readingroom/docs/DOC_0001451843.pdf), CIA secretly helped spot, assess and recruit personnel who became BNDD employees. Some were intended for internal-security work; others operated under nonofficial cover against international traffickers. The memo says the recruits did not know CIA was handling part of the process.

So CIA did not merely have doors into foreign police systems.

It had a concealed personnel role touching America's own narcotics bureau.

Does that mean CIA controlled every checkpoint, customs office or drug case?

Of course not.

It means the physical pipeline sat inside a police and intelligence environment that Washington had spent years funding, training, advising and cultivating.

The United States did not need to own every gate.

**It had relationships with the people standing around them.**

That is how a pipeline becomes insulated.

## **THE PROGRAM ENDED. THE CONTACTS DIDN'T.**

By 1974, broad USAID police assistance was already winding down. Congress then enacted [Section 660](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/STATUTE-88/pdf/STATUTE-88-Pg1795.pdf), formally restricting Foreign Assistance Act support for foreign police, prisons, law-enforcement forces and internal-intelligence programs beginning July 1, 1975.

The closure was real.

But it did not send thousands of graduates back through time. It did not erase the advisers, alumni, equipment, files or personal relationships already created. And it did not close the international narcotics channel. Section 660 preserved narcotics-control assistance and specified DEA/FBI activities from the beginning.

Laos shows the handoff in advance. The 1974 mission plan called for OPS narcotics assistance to end at the close of FY1975 while **DEA assumed advisory responsibility** for Lao narcotics institutions.

The office could close.

The academy could close.

The network did not have to disappear.

## **FAQ**

### **Did CIA create the Golden Triangle drug trade?**

No. The regional opium economy and criminal networks existed independently. What the documents show is that U.S. covert-war infrastructure strengthened or intersected with actors, routes and institutions already involved in that economy.

### **How did Golden Triangle heroin reach international markets?**

Through multiple overlapping channels: commercial aircraft, military bases, military mail, diplomatic baggage, merchant shipping, fishing vessels, servicemen and civilian couriers. Saigon, Bangkok, Hong Kong, Paris, Marseille and New York all appear in the documented routes.

### **What role did organized crime play?**

Corsican, Chinese and American criminal networks already knew how to move, refine and wholesale narcotics. They supplied the international distribution system once the product left the regional pipeline.

### **Why does the International Police Academy matter?**

IPA trained thousands of foreign officers and sent them back into police institutions worldwide. DOJ treated graduates as useful contacts, while CIA maintained separate channels for information, assessment, sponsorship and additional training involving selected officers.

### **Did closing OPS and IPA eliminate the network?**

No. The programs genuinely wound down, but their graduates, advisers, equipment and relationships remained. International narcotics authorities also stayed open, and DEA was positioned to inherit specific advisory responsibilities.
