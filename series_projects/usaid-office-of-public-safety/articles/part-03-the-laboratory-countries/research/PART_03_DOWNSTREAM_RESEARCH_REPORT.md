## Executive finding

The downstream record supports three conclusions at different strengths.

1. **International distribution is documented as a bundle of routes, not a single chain.** Official U.S. testimony identifies Vientiane–Tan Son Nhut, Thailand–U-Tapao–United States, APO/military-mail, commercial-air and maritime routes. McCoy adds named Vientiane–New York, Corsican and Sopsaisana cases, but several of those shipment-level accounts still depend on interviews rather than acquired case files.
2. **U.S. institutions obtained substantial access to the enforcement side of the same geography.** USAID/OPS helped build narcotics capacity in Lao police and military police; BNDD, Customs and later DEA advised and exchanged intelligence; IPA trained multinational cohorts; CIA maintained separate police-participant channels; and Project TWO-FOLD covertly recruited personnel who became BNDD employees. This is demonstrated access and leverage, not demonstrated command of every police action.
3. **Uneven enforcement is supported; a centrally directed protection system is not.** The record shows enforcement constrained by war priorities, official corruption, limited goals and institutional compartmentation. McCoy's BNDD interviews allege State/CIA resistance in Vientiane and Conein/Lansdale tolerance of Corsican actors. Official records also document seizures, arrests, intelligence sharing and a CIA/BNDD program directed at major international traffickers. No acquired primary document orders protection of Trafficante, the Corsican network, Sopsaisana, Vang Pao or a named shipment.

The strongest defensible conclusion is therefore that the national-security apparatus occupied relationships on both sides of the chokepoints and created the capacity for selective enforcement, while the surviving files do not establish a single centrally controlled heroin system.

## Major discoveries

### 1. Project TWO-FOLD is direct evidence of CIA access inside BNDD

The strongest new institutional record is the CIA's 25 May 1973 Project TWO-FOLD memorandum, preserved in *Family Jewels* and as an individual CIA Reading Room document.

- Robert Ingersoll approached Richard Helms in December 1970 because he believed the old Federal Bureau of Narcotics had been infiltrated by dishonest or corrupt personnel tied to the narcotics-smuggling industry.
- The DCI approved assistance in February 1971.
- CIA's Office of Security spotted, assessed and recruited personnel for a BNDD internal-security unit. After recruitment and training, BNDD's Chief Inspector directed their domestic assignments.
- CIA then supported covert acquisition of staff agents under nonofficial cover, directed against principal international traffickers. The recruits were BNDD employees, but CIA handled administrative details and the recruits did not know CIA was involved.
- In May 1973, William Colby recommended ending the domestic internal-corruption portion while continuing foreign assignments collecting narcotics intelligence abroad.

This proves covert CIA personnel access to BNDD and a concealed role in staffing narcotics enforcement. It does **not** identify the agents, operations, countries or targets; show that CIA retained operational control after BNDD assignment; or connect TWO-FOLD to OPS/IPA graduates, Laos, Trafficante or a protected shipment.

### 2. The 1974 Lao program was larger and more explicit than the old draft showed

Airgram A-102 reports:

- A program budget that reached roughly **$2 million in 1973**, projected at **$1.6 million in FY1974**.
- U.S. narcotics-program personnel reduced selectively from roughly **30 to 10**.
- The program's goal had **never been total elimination** of narcotics production or trafficking because the mission considered the required investment disproportionate to Laos's then-current connection to the U.S. narcotics problem.
- **711 Lao personnel** from National Police, Military Police, Customs, GSI and the judiciary had received in-country narcotics-enforcement training.
- **21 people** had received U.S. or third-country participant training, including civil- and military-police narcotics categories.
- The Royal Lao Army Provost Marshal General's criminal/investigation division contained a narcotics-control section; **163 military police** had initial training and **seven** had U.S. narcotics training.
- The military-police objective explicitly addressed trafficking by Royal Lao Army officials and use of military transportation.
- Military narcotics police were to supervise and assist irregular mobile checkpoints.
- OPS narcotics assistance was scheduled to end at FY1975, with DEA assuming advisory responsibility for the National Police, Military Police and GSI.

This is simultaneously evidence of police/trafficking overlap, U.S. leverage, measurable enforcement activity and a planned institutional handoff.

### 3. Section 660 was the second cutoff, not the first

The Foreign Assistance Act of 1973 had already prohibited the classic AID police program after existing commitments, leading AID to phase out seventeen programs and ninety-five overseas advisers. Section 660 was enacted on 30 December 1974 and became effective 1 July 1975. Its original text preserved FAA section 482 international narcotics-control assistance and specified DEA/FBI authorities.

The 1974 Vientiane plan independently shows personnel reduction and an OPS-to-DEA narcotics handoff before Section 660 took effect. The best description is **wind-down plus jurisdictional migration**, not an overnight shutdown and not a simple renaming of OPS.

### 4. Trafficante's CIA relationship is proved; the Asian heroin deal is not

The Church Committee documented CIA's use of intermediaries Robert Maheu and Johnny Roselli to reach Sam Giancana and Santo Trafficante in plots against Fidel Castro. That is a real prior CIA relationship with Trafficante.

McCoy traced Trafficante's 1968 Hong Kong visit to the Senate military-club investigation and reported a subsequent Saigon visit and believed Corsican contacts from embassy sources. A later congressional insertion said federal narcotics agents had spotted major Mafia figures in Asian cities and suspected new connections.

The strongest counterevidence is a 26 February 1969 FBI report identified as NARA record 124-10213-10207. Historian Jonathan Marshall reports that the investigation found no evidence supporting a narcotics-conspiracy interpretation of Trafficante's trip; the men had around-the-world tickets and the visit to Frank Furci had non-narcotics explanations. The original NARA scan requires human review before relying on that conclusion as a direct primary finding.

No primary record found in this pass proves that Trafficante bought heroin from Vang Pao, met Vang Pao, or concluded a supply agreement with CIA assistance.

### 5. The Corsican route is best supported as a participant account

McCoy's most detailed account comes from CIA officer Lucien Conein and Edward Lansdale:

- Conein described family and syndicate links connecting Saigon, Vientiane and Marseille, and said morphine base moved by French merchant vessels.
- Lansdale described a “truce” with Corsican leaders because criminal investigation was not his mission.
- Conein alleged a 1969 Continental Palace meeting involving Corsican representatives from Marseille, Bangkok, Vientiane and Phnom Penh.
- A U.S. diplomat reportedly believed Trafficante contacted Corsican leaders in Saigon.

These are identifiable participant/diplomatic accounts, but the underlying interview notes and independent intelligence reports were not acquired. They support attributed narrative, not an unqualified CIA–Corsican trafficking partnership.

### 6. The Sopsaisana case has one new name but still lacks a case file

McCoy's 1972 Senate testimony identifies **Michel Theodas**, manager of Vientiane's Lang Xang Hotel, as the alleged organizer of Sopsaisana's French connection, attributing this to BNDD and diplomatic sources. McCoy's book says two Western/Third World diplomatic officials, a Lao political observer and a BNDD agent corroborated the alleged Long Tieng origin. It records Sopsaisana's denial and accusation that rivals framed him.

Contemporary press corroborates the Orly interception, the large heroin quantity and France's refusal to accept Sopsaisana's credentials. No acquired French Customs, Foreign Ministry, court, State cable, BNDD report or CIA operational file independently establishes Vang Pao as supplier, Long Tieng as refinery, Theodas as broker, the intended Corsican recipient or New York as the shipment's actual next destination.

### 7. Tachilek ownership makes mixed motives plausible

McCoy identifies private Tachilek refineries associated with Kokang militia leader **Law Sik Han** (Lo Hsing-han), Wa leader Bo Loi Oo and militia commander Yang Sun. A 1971 CIA estimate placed fourteen laboratories around Tachilek. The CIF/KMT controlled much of the raw-opium carriage into Thailand and Laos.

Using Li and Tuan's CIF against Tachilek laboratories could therefore have served a genuine interdiction objective and damaged competing processors at the same time. The competitive benefit is real as an inference. No located U.S. document states that market consolidation was the motive, and the files do not establish that Lo Hsing-han controlled every proposed target.

## Selective-enforcement test

### Evidence for

- McCoy's named participant interviews say Lansdale and Conein did not refer Corsican intelligence to FBN and that BNDD met State/CIA resistance when seeking a Vientiane office.
- U.S. officials continued strategic relationships with Lao and CIF actors after their narcotics involvement was known.
- The Lao program itself targeted trafficking by Royal Lao Army officials and misuse of military transport, establishing overlap inside an allied force.
- OPS/IPA relationships, CIA police liaison and TWO-FOLD created several channels through which information, personnel and priorities could be shaped.
- The Vientiane mission expressly chose limited rather than total suppression and calibrated effort to U.S. interests and available resources.

### Evidence against or narrowing the claim

- Official records document arrests, seizures, refinery disruption, BNDD-developed intelligence and joint work with Lao and Thai police.
- TWO-FOLD's foreign staff-agent branch was formally directed against principal international drug traffickers.
- CIA reporting acknowledged multiple Golden Triangle routes and expanded foreign narcotics collection.
- The strongest located counteraccount of Trafficante's 1968 trip says federal investigation found no narcotics conspiracy.
- No primary order directs a police unit to spare a named CIA ally, target a rival for commercial advantage, suppress evidence or derail a prosecution.

### Verdict

**Supported at the structural and priority-setting level; unresolved at the named-case protection level.** The evidence shows constrained and uneven enforcement in a system where war priorities, official corruption and intelligence access shaped what could be investigated. It does not yet prove a centrally directed guard-dog model protecting one end-to-end trafficking chain.

## Recommended narrative

1. Open with verified shipment routes and show that war infrastructure supplied ready-made channels.
2. Introduce the preexisting wholesalers—Corsicans, Hong Kong networks and American organized crime—without collapsing suspicion into proof.
3. Use Trafficante as a test case: a proven CIA relationship plus proven Asian travel, counterbalanced by the FBI-derived finding of no narcotics conspiracy.
4. Reconstruct Sopsaisana as the most suggestive but incomplete bulk shipment, naming Theodas only with attribution.
5. Turn from commodity flow to chokepoints: State's interagency architecture, OPS/USAID capacity, GSI, military police, IPA and DEA.
6. Reveal TWO-FOLD as the strongest documentary proof of CIA access inside narcotics enforcement.
7. Test selective enforcement openly and deliver a split verdict: structural capacity and uneven priorities proved; a named protected chain not proved.
8. Close with the 1973–75 wind-down and migration into preserved narcotics authorities, then stop with a brief Central America bridge.
