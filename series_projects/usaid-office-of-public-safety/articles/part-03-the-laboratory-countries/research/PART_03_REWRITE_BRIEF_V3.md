## CENTRAL MYSTERY

How did overt aid, covert logistics, police training and law-enforcement relationships repeatedly intersect with narcotics economies—and what did U.S. institutions do when enforcement threatened strategically useful people or infrastructure?

## DOCUMENTED CORE

1. Official review rejected an Air America corporate trafficking policy or senior order but conceded incomplete loading control, occasional carriage risk, narcotics-connected allies and wartime non-suppression.
2. Named Laos interviews describe village collection, military buyers, helicopters and Long Tieng; they remain interview evidence rather than CIA confirmation.
3. A 1974 Embassy record documents USAID-supported Lao military-police narcotics training, U.S. training routes and concern about RLA officials using military transport for trafficking.
4. Original IPA, Treasury/FBN and DOJ reports document recurring narcotics instruction and an international alumni/contact network, with strict limits on what “contact” means.
5. Kerry and CIA OIG records establish Contra-drug overlap, case-specific operational retention, reporting gaps and enforcement conflicts while rejecting an institutional CIA trafficking conspiracy.
6. Brazil documents security access, police capacity, covert political action and abuse knowledge, but not a comparable narcotics route.

## RECOMMENDED THESIS

> Across Laos and selected Contra cases, U.S. aid and covert systems built transport, access, and security relationships around allies operating inside narcotics economies. When narcotics risks became known, strategic utility sometimes took priority over reporting or enforcement. The record is strongest for infrastructure, knowledge, tolerance, and selective non-enforcement—not for a centrally directed CIA drug pipeline into the United States.

This is the narrowest thesis that survives all four component tests and the Brazil, C-123, IPA-PARU and Moran evidence boundaries.
