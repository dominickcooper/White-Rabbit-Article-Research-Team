# Part 6 Research Dossier

## Governing question

What happened to the functions of U.S. foreign-police assistance after Congress closed USAID's Office of Public Safety—and does the record show a hidden institutional successor, or a distributed ecosystem?

## Answer in brief

Section 660 dismantled the visible AID operating center but did not create a universal federal ban. It attached to Foreign Assistance Act funds and preserved specified DEA/FBI and narcotics channels. Congress then added exceptions for administration of justice, antiterrorism, maritime enforcement, selected countries and post-conflict civilian-police rebuilding. In 1986 AID transferred money to DOJ, which established ICITAP under State policy oversight. Other agencies continued or expanded their own programs. By FY1990 GAO found at least $117 million in police assistance across 125 countries—and could not determine the full amount.

The most revealing continuity is neither a secret name change nor a single hidden command. It is the migration of functions through legal exceptions, interagency funding, personnel, missions and contracts. The system that emerged was more specialized, more dispersed and often harder to audit.

## Finding 1: The ban had a jurisdiction

The current Foreign Assistance Act retains Section 660's core prohibition: FAA funds cannot support foreign police, prisons, internal intelligence or surveillance. The same section contains exceptions. DEA/FBI assistance concerning U.S.-type crimes and assistance under international narcotics authority appear in subsection (b)(1); later paragraphs cover maritime enforcement, Eastern Caribbean security, sanctions and post-conflict police reconstitution.

The wording matters. Congress closed a funding lane. It did not prevent Justice, Defense or other agencies from using separate appropriations and authorities, and it did not prevent Congress from cutting new channels through the prohibition.

## Finding 2: ICITAP was built as a deliberately awkward hybrid

GAO describes the key 1986 transaction directly: AID transferred money to DOJ to improve investigative capacity in Latin America and the Caribbean, and DOJ established ICITAP. NDU's institutional history supplies the organizational anatomy. ICITAP reported inside Justice, received State policy guidance, and initially received AID money channeled through State.

That design reflected aversion to OPS as much as continuity with it. AID reportedly resisted renewed association with police aid; the FBI declined to house the program for fear of another OPS; State lacked the operating capacity; DOD faced restrictions. The compromise was a small Justice program with foreign-policy direction and foreign-assistance money from elsewhere.

ICITAP also began narrower than OPS. Its authority focused on criminal investigation under judicial or prosecutorial control. Congress later allowed management and academy-curriculum assistance, then wider post-conflict police development country by country.

## Finding 3: One man crossed the seams

David J. Kriskovich makes the paper architecture visible. In 1981, as an FBI inspector, he developed the Caribbean Police School. In 1985 he trained El Salvador's Special Investigative Unit; DOD funded the early training and AID funded Kriskovich. On January 6, 1986, he became ICITAP's founding director on loan from the FBI.

He remained until 1994 while ICITAP expanded from regional investigations into police management, academies and post-conflict missions. In April 1997 he became the Bosnia IPTF's deputy commissioner for development, the senior American post. He died in a helicopter crash that September with other international police-development officials.

This is a powerful personnel bridge, but precision is essential. The record does not place Kriskovich in OPS or CIA. He links AID-funded pre-ICITAP work, FBI expertise, DOJ's new institution and a U.N. post-conflict mission.

## Finding 4: The ecosystem became larger than any one successor

GAO's 1992 accounting found at least 125 countries receiving U.S. police training or assistance in FY1990 at a cost of at least $117 million. State narcotics accounted for roughly $45 million; DOD police support for about $42 million; ICITAP for $20 million; State antiterrorism for $10 million. Two or more programs operated in 46 countries.

GAO could not calculate a complete total because agencies kept inconsistent data and could double-count trainees. It found no clear overall policy, no central decision point, limited headquarters coordination and possible duplication. Embassies sometimes coordinated programs in country, but authority depended on ambassadors and separate agency channels.

This is the ecosystem's defining feature: the absence of a single successor did not mean the absence of scale. It meant aggregate activity became difficult to see.

## Finding 5: Narcotics was a durable route

The narcotics exception survived from the original law and grew through later legislation. It joined law-enforcement training to a U.S. crime-control objective that Congress continued to authorize. State's narcotics bureau, DEA, DOD and ICITAP all developed relationships with foreign police or specialized units, sometimes in the same countries.

The record supports a major legal and funding pathway. It does not support treating every narcotics program as a covert police program or every shared recipient as a single command.

## Finding 6: Post-conflict policing widened the mission

Panama, El Salvador, Haiti, Somalia and Bosnia forced U.S. policy beyond classroom training. The relevant problem was who would patrol streets, investigate crimes, screen officers and rebuild institutions after authoritarian rule or war. Congress's post-conflict exception allowed the United States to “reconstitute civilian police authority and capability.”

ICITAP's portfolio expanded accordingly. It helped build academies and management systems, deployed advisers and contractors, and worked alongside military and U.N. missions. DOJ's own history later carried the arc through Kosovo, Afghanistan and Iraq.

The conventional rationale is substantial: collapsed or abusive police institutions can wreck a peace settlement. But the widening mission also returned many capabilities—training, equipment, organization and embedded advising—that reformers believed they had removed from foreign aid.

## Finding 7: Contractors created another seam

By the mid-1990s contractors and subcontractors performed much of ICITAP's overseas training and advising. Bosnia shows the accountability consequences. State contracted DynCorp to supply the American contingent to the U.N. police mission, while DOD also used DynCorp support personnel with SFOR.

Human Rights Watch's 2002 investigation cites Army CID records, sworn statements and internal reports concerning DynCorp employees who purchased women or were accused of trafficking-related misconduct. HRW also records that a British tribunal unanimously found DynCorp unfairly dismissed Kathryn Bolkovac, an IPTF human-rights officer who reported trafficking allegations.

The precise conclusion is an oversight failure: contracts, immunity, employer discipline, repatriation and host-country jurisdiction did not align. The source does not prove DynCorp corporate policy was trafficking, and a DOD review found negligible evidence of widespread U.S. service-member involvement.

## Finding 8: CIA continuity remains unproved

CIA records show an active post-OPS counternarcotics mission and interagency interest in foreign law enforcement. The countries and problems overlap. But the collected primary sources do not show CIA control of ICITAP, ILEA or later USAID justice programs, nor do they reproduce the IPA intelligence-screening pipeline documented in Part 2.

That negative finding matters. The stronger Part 6 story does not require a hidden command. Fragmentation itself explains how functions survived and why accountability weakened.

## Negative findings

1. No proven post-OPS USAID–CIA command structure.
2. No verified OPS/IPA personnel migration roster.
3. No basis to equate funding with operational control.
4. No basis to equate shared country with institutional handoff.
5. No proof the Naruvu scenario occurred.
6. No proof USAID intended Afghan poppy support.
7. No authenticated proof that IRI plotted to destabilize Bangladesh.

## Publication recommendation

Publish Part 6 around the ecosystem thesis. Use the Kriskovich bridge and GAO's incomplete $117 million map as the dual reveal. Keep ZunZuneo, Afghanistan, Bangladesh and the hypothetical port as a short “tests of the theory” section and Part 7 queue, not as competing centers of gravity.

## WHAT THIS PART ADDS

It identifies the post-OPS successor as a distributed architecture rather than a renamed office and documents the legal, financial, personnel and contractor paths by which that architecture formed.

## EARLIER FINDINGS REQUIRED FOR CONTEXT

Parts 1–3 established the overt OPS system, its IPA/CIA channel and country-level institutional effects. Part 4 established functional migration as a method. Part 5 established that Section 660 ended the AID-centered model while preserving bounded exceptions.

## NEW FINDINGS

The new findings are the AID-to-DOJ founding transfer, ICITAP's Justice/State/AID split, Kriskovich's cross-agency career, GAO's uncountable aggregate system, post-conflict functional expansion and contract-mediated accountability failures.

## NEW SOURCES

Thirty-two valid sources were added, led by the current FAA compilation, P.L. 93-559, P.L. 99-83, DOJ ICITAP records, USAID OIG's ZunZuneo review, the Senate ZunZuneo hearing, HRW's Bosnia investigation and the JSOU civil-military volume.

## NEW PEOPLE / ORGANIZATIONS

## EVIDENCE-WEIGHTED CONCLUSIONS

High confidence: legal/financial fragmentation, ICITAP's founding structure, Kriskovich bridge and FY1990 scale. Medium-high confidence: contractor-accountability case. Unsupported: post-OPS CIA command, Bangladesh coup causation, Naruvu execution and intentional poppy support.

## RESPONSIBILITY ASSESSMENT

Congress defined exceptions; State often supplied policy and money; DOJ operated ICITAP; AID financed or partnered in selected programs; DOD and federal law-enforcement agencies used distinct authorities; contractors implemented specific scopes. Responsibility must be assigned by program, not collapsed into “the U.S. government.”

## CONTRARY EVIDENCE

ICITAP was designed partly to avoid OPS's reputation, had a narrower original mandate and emphasized professional investigations, human rights and civilian policing. GAO documented genuine coordination problems rather than a smoothly unified hidden system.

## COMPETING EXPLANATIONS

Fragmentation can be explained by federal jurisdiction, congressional compromise, genuine transnational threats and post-conflict needs. These explanations coexist with the critical finding that fragmentation reduced whole-system visibility.

## CONNECTIONS TO MASTER DOSSIER

The findings extend the series' legal timeline, agency network, personnel map and handoff model. They refine “continuity” into function-specific funding, legal, personnel and contract relationships.

## QUESTIONS RESOLVED

OPS did not have a single successor; AID did directly finance DOJ's early ICITAP work; narcotics and post-conflict authorities became major routes; Kriskovich is a provable personnel bridge; aggregate oversight remained weak.

## QUESTIONS CARRIED FORWARD

The full ICITAP funding ledger, authenticated IRI files, Afghanistan police contracting, CIA interagency records and OPS/IPA alumni rosters remain unresolved.

# Revision Addendum — Benz Ecosystem Test

The ground-up revision changes the organizing question from “what office replaced OPS?” to “what division of labor replaced the need for one office?” The strongest documented formula is: **AID funded; State guided policy; Justice operated; host governments received.** Contractors, grantees, federal law-enforcement agencies, DOD and intelligence-community participants occupied additional nodes depending on mission.

Targeted official-source review adds two bounded intelligence findings. A 1976 CIA/CCPC study describes foreign law-enforcement and intelligence cooperation as vital to narcotics work, an interagency source registry and DEA notification to CIA station chiefs. 1983 Bush remarks describe regional narcotics information centers staffed across FBI, DEA, Customs, Coast Guard, military, immigration and the intelligence community. These records establish intelligence interest and formal coordination without establishing CIA command over ICITAP or USAID.

The revision promotes ZunZuneo and Afghanistan into core tests. ZunZuneo documents contractor implementation, concealed sponsorship, offshore structures and attempted political segmentation, with OIG’s limiting findings retained. GAO-10-291 and SIGAR-18-52-LL document a collision among counternarcotics, counterinsurgency, stabilization and development priorities in Afghanistan; they do not establish intentional support for narcotics.
