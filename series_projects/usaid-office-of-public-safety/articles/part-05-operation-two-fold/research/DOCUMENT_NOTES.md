# Part 5 document notes

Document-level notes will be appended in stable source-ID order. Each entry records
provenance, what the record directly says, what it does not say, OCR limits, relationship
to other records and any primary-source escalation needed.

## P5-0001 — Seven John Does closing brief

- **Provenance/type:** Appellants' closing brief before the U.S. Merit Systems Protection
  Board, docket numbers DA08318110337 and DC08318110596, submitted after a December 1981
  evidentiary hearing. It is advocacy, not a judicial finding.
- **Direct record:** Seven former CIA officers transferred to BNDD/DEA in 1973–74 for a
  Narcotics Intelligence Officer program: Robert A. Simon, Terry T. Baldwin, Elias P.
  Chavez, Louis J. Davis, Hugh E. Murray, Wesley C. Dyckman and one anonymized Appellant
  A. The brief says CIA and DEA encouraged the moves and DEA officials promised that CIA
  service would count toward law-enforcement retirement. It cites hearing testimony from
  the transferees, William Colby, Lucien Conein, John Warner and agency witnesses.
- **Operational details alleged under oath:** the transferees described CIA narcotics,
  police-liaison and terrorism assignments; CIA/DEA information sharing; joint overseas
  work; similar informant recruitment, surveillance, interrogation and reporting; Murray's
  first DEA assignment transferring CIA foreign narcotics informants to DEA; and Baldwin's
  continuation in the same country with some of the same CIA officers and informants.
- **What it establishes now:** a concrete, named CIA-to-BNDD/DEA personnel and function
  transfer is supported by an adversarial administrative record. This is more specific
  than the earlier series formulation of an unidentified TWO-FOLD recruitment mechanism.
- **Limits:** the source is the appellants' brief and selectively summarizes testimony.
  The OCR corpus stops around printed page 17 although the contents list runs to page 36;
  the estoppel argument and conclusion are absent. Classified assignments remain sanitized.
- **Escalation:** obtain the complete MSPB record, hearing transcript, exhibits and any
  precedential/final Board or appellate disposition. Resolve whether these seven are the
  complete or only a related subset of TWO-FOLD recruits.

## P5-0002 — Seven John Does initial decision

- **Provenance/type:** MSPB Administrative Law Judge Edward J. Reidy, initial decision,
  May 20, 1982, same consolidated dockets. This is adjudicative rather than party advocacy,
  but the local OCR copy is incomplete.
- **Direct record:** Reidy states that all appellants had been CIA special agents, were
  recruited into DEA/BNDD in 1973–74 because of specialized narcotics experience, and
  entered the NIO program while Nixon directed DEA to create its own narcotics-intelligence
  capability and relieve CIA of that obligation. The decision identifies the seven and
  summarizes their testimony, including CIA/DEA overseas overlap, foreign-police liaison,
  informant work, and oral retirement assurances.
- **Additional person/mechanism:** Thomas C. Tripodi is described as FBN (1960), CIA
  (roughly 1963–68), then BNDD/DEA, where he returned to address internal corruption. His
  path predates the seven and may be a separate continuity bridge.
- **Organizational evidence:** DEA witnesses said the agency was launching a clandestine
  intelligence-collection effort and recruited experienced CIA agents as cadre; CIA fully
  supported recruitment. William Colby supported the NIO transfer. The decision separately
  notes that DEA and CIA exchanged narcotics intelligence.
- **Limits:** the OCR terminates mid-discussion of OPM witness Andrew Ruddock, before legal
  findings and disposition. It cannot support a claim that the appellants won, lost, or
  received retirement credit. Some statements remain the judge's summaries of contested
  testimony rather than independently corroborated operational files.
- **Escalation:** locate the complete decision and later review history; compare the names,
  transfer dates and NIO positions against Project TWO-FOLD memoranda and DEA personnel
  rosters.

- **Provenance/type:** Undated prosecution-style evidence packet concerning the November

## P5-0007 — Seymour Bolten White House exit interview

- **Provenance/type:** White House exit interview with Seymour Bolten, February 5, 1981,
  plus career appendix; retrospective participant account.
- **Direct record:** Bolten says he headed the CIA Narcotics Control Group, located under the
  Operations Directorate while also reporting to the DCI because of the DCI's Cabinet
  Committee for International Narcotics Control role. Bolten headed the committee's
  Intelligence Subcommittee. He describes CIA/DEA/Customs/State rivalries and says DEA could
  circulate CIA reports, but DEA Intelligence retained them rather than placing them in
  operational files because court use created legal problems.
- **Relevance:** strong lead on information architecture, compartmentation and the White
  House/Cabinet coordination layer. It supports CIA participation in narcotics intelligence,
  not a conclusion that CIA commanded DEA.
- **Limits:** retrospective interview; precise dates, directives and classification rules
  need contemporaneous committee and agency records.

## P5-0014 — Cusack report on Sung Hon Yung, July 1960

- **Provenance/type:** Secret FBN report from Rome District Supervisor John T. Cusack to
  Commissioner Harry Anslinger, July 29, 1960.
- **Direct record:** Cusack and Agent Anthony Mangiaracina worked with Spanish Interpol and
  Barcelona criminal police, approached Sung Hon Yung under cover seeking heroin, and
  documented the Chinese tailoring/restaurant network and its knowledge of U.S. naval

- **Agent judgment:** Cusack concluded the group was probably a Chinese Communist espionage

- **Relevance/limit:** primary evidence of FBN overseas undercover work, foreign-police
  liaison and the agency's fusion of narcotics and security suspicion. It is not evidence
  of TWO-FOLD and should not be used to label the subjects traffickers or spies.

## P5-0087–P5-0100 — Habib/FBN Southeast Asia operational memoranda, 1965–66

- **Coverage:** FBN Agent Albert Habib's Bangkok-, Laos- and Saigon-based reporting, plus
  related seizure, identity and liaison memoranda. The series records day-to-day contact
  with Thai narcotics officers, Vietnamese Military Security, Vietnamese police/customs,
  U.S. Army Intelligence, OSI, CIA, Embassy political/security officers and USAID staff.
- **Laos inquiry (P5-0095/P5-0098):** Ambassador William Sullivan asked Habib to investigate
  heroin/morphine laboratories and possible American involvement. Habib consulted Embassy
  officers, USAID operations/refugee personnel and Public Safety chief Paul Skuse. His
  report found a serious narcotics problem and official Lao involvement, but the interviewed
  American officials said they knew of no American personnel involved. This is a scoped
  investigation and attributed negative finding, not proof that no involvement existed.
- **Thailand–Vietnam operation (especially P5-0090, 0092, 0093, 0096, 0099):** a Vietnamese
  intelligence source sought American military transport for hundreds of pounds of opium
  said to have penetrated a Viet Cong network. FBN and U.S. military intelligence explored
  a controlled delivery, demanded to inspect the drug and briefed Embassy leadership. The
  deputy ambassador refused approval because of distrust, setup/blackmail risk and the
  prospect of opium being found aboard a USAF aircraft. A CIA officer later said the
  ambassador would not approve without formal Vietnamese contact. Habib ultimately refused
  to furnish an aircraft; Vietnamese officers claimed they moved and seized the first load
  by boat. The records do **not** show that an American aircraft carried the opium.
- **Other files:** P5-0087–89, 0091, 0094, 0097 and 0100 supply case, seizure, fingerprint,
  informant and undercover-negotiation details. They reinforce the overseas liaison picture
  but do not independently establish CIA sponsorship or TWO-FOLD.
- **Central limit:** these records document real boundary-crossing among diplomacy,
  intelligence, military security and narcotics enforcement. Proposed controlled transport,
  however, is not evidence of completed trafficking; source claims about Viet Cong or
  American participants remain allegations unless independently corroborated.

## P5-0103–P5-0125 — CIA–FBN/BNDD behavioral-drug and safehouse records

- **Direct institutional record:** P5-0110/0111 state that CIA regarded unwitting human
  testing as operationally necessary and called its Bureau of Narcotics arrangement the
  most practical and secure method. P5-0108 records CIA funding of FBN safe apartments,
  receipt of information about informant behavior, audio-equipment transfers and a 1967
  effort to keep the Agency relationship out of a Senate investigation.
- **FBN participation:** the 1977 interview of Charles Siragusa (P5-0117) says Anslinger made
  him CIA liaison; FBN rented/equipped New York and San Francisco observation apartments,
  CIA held access, and FBN also prepared credentials for CIA officers abroad. His memory was
  uneven, and he said CIA did not tell him precisely how it used the New York apartment.
- **Scope and reconstruction:** DEA Internal Security's 1977–79 inquiry (P5-0119/0120/0121)
  found FBN cooperated in establishing and maintaining safehouses from May 1953 to April
  1966; it identified George White but no other predecessor-agency employee as participating
  in drug testing, found no record that FBN supplied CIA drugs, and found no current DEA
  safehouse cooperation. P5-0120 exactly duplicates P5-0119.
- **Victim search/result:** CIA and DEA jointly reconstructed the program in 1979, interviewed
  former personnel and examined George White's diary (P5-0115, 0121–24). Turner's July 1979
  letter reports seven, possibly eight, unwitting LSD administrations; two people were found
  and notified, and investigators could not be confident those were the only cases.
- **BNDD continuation of liaison, not testing:** P5-0103 records a March 1969 CIA meeting
  with BNDD science/intelligence officials, disclosure of CIA interest in behavioral agents,
  agreement on continuing liaison and periodic BNDD briefings. It does not show a revived
  unwitting-testing program. P5-0104/0106/0109/0112/0114/0118/0125 document the later legal,
  medical and notification process.
- **Side records:** P5-0105 is a CIA cover-file response involving several HSCA names, not
  evidence about Conein's TWO-FOLD role; P5-0113 is a web-era Lansdale/assassination
  commentary rather than a primary MKULTRA record; P5-0116 is a 1997 private Siragusa note
  about Cuba and is contextual only.
- **Central significance/limit:** this cluster proves a concealed, long-running CIA–FBN
  domestic operational partnership and subsequent resistance to disclosure. It predates
  TWO-FOLD and cannot, without bridging records, prove that the later recruitment program
  shared its purpose, personnel or chain of command.

## P5-0127–P5-0134 — Iran/Pahlevi and Garland Williams policy cluster

- **Garland Williams report (P5-0127):** a lengthy 1959 official-use-only survey of Iran and
  the Asian opium system. Its recommendations explicitly call for narcotics advisers in the
  U.S. missions in Thailand and Burma; new operational FBN districts in South Asia and the
  Near East; narcotics instruction for personnel from ICA, CIA, State and other agencies;
  and a State Department desk to guide overseas narcotics-intelligence collection. P5-0155
  is its exact normalized duplicate.
- **Pahlevi/case files (P5-0128–134):** FBN reports and correspondence from 1953–55 combine
  a real Paris morphine-base seizure/laboratory case with approaches to or allegations about
  Prince Mahmoud Pahlevi and other well-connected Iranians. The investigative reports show
  undercover work with French police and an effort to exploit a cooperating defendant.
- **Evidence limit:** arrest/seizure and sentencing records have firmer footing than source
  claims about royal or political participants. Do not treat the prince allegations as
  adjudicated merely because they appear in an official file. The Williams report is policy
  advocacy and observation, not proof that all recommendations were adopted.
- **Significance:** P5-0127 supplies a concrete pre-1960 blueprint for embedding narcotics
  advisers, intelligence collection and training across foreign-assistance, diplomatic and
  intelligence agencies—a stronger continuity lead than the celebrity allegation material.

## P5-0141/P5-0142 — DEA objections to CIA operational narcotics collection, 1976–77

- **Provenance/type:** internal DEA enforcement memoranda from Daniel P. Casey and Jerry
  Strickler to senior DEA management.
- **Direct record:** enforcement chiefs unanimously warned that unilateral CIA electronic
  surveillance and source recruitment compromised investigations, triggered discovery
  problems, and could confer practical immunity on traffickers. DEA had supplied target
  phone numbers; the memoranda recommended ending that support and confining CIA to
  strategic intelligence.
- **Concrete examples:** P5-0142 lists a fugitive not accepted for deportation, an indicted
  trafficker and contacts effectively immunized, a cooperator/case against roughly twenty
  targets lost, a multinational network case halted, CIA competition for sources, false
  travel documents, blackmail recruitment and protection commitments.
- **Limit:** targets are redacted/unnamed and the memoranda present enforcement's side of an
  interagency dispute. Case files and prosecutorial dispositions are needed before describing
  each example as finally adjudicated. They nevertheless directly document institutional
  consequences, not merely a later author's conclusion.

## External primary recovery E-001 — CIA Family Jewels, MORI 1451843, pp. 56–62

## External primary recovery E-003 — CIA damage assessment, NARA 104-10428-10019

- **Record:** “Probable Compromise of Information Furnished to the House Select Committee

## External primary recovery E-004 — CIA Office of Training FY1972 annual report

- **Record:** CIA-RDP78-06207A000100060002-9, dated 25 July 1972, especially the section on
  training for non-Agency personnel and its appended institutional chronology.
- **Recovered detail:** BNDD's director requested CIA guidance in November 1969, primarily
  for clandestine agent and informant operations abroad. A CIA training officer surveyed BNDD
  for three weeks in March 1970 and concluded that the Bureau needed assistance across the
  range of tradecraft to sustain long-term clandestine operations. CIA initially contemplated
  only ad hoc support.
- **Implemented training:** after a withdrawn November 1971 request from BNDD's Strategic
  Intelligence Office and a renewed December request, CIA's OTR, DDI and CI Liaison briefed
  18 BNDD Regional Intelligence Officers on 24–25 April 1972. The report says coverage was
  introductory, with basic clandestine concepts and overseas narcotics responsibilities.
- **Prospective language:** CIA anticipated that BNDD and Customs might seek something closer
  to purely covert training for selected officers, but the report does not establish that such
  later instruction occurred.
- **Evidence limit:** no passage names TWO-FOLD or the 18 officers. This is a separate foreign-
  operations training channel unless project or personnel records establish a bridge.
