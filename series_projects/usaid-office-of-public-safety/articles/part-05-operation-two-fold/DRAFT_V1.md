# USAID & THE CIA, PART 5: OPERATION TWO-FOLD

## 1. THE ORDER TO END TWO-FOLD—AND KEEP PART OF IT

On **May 25, 1973**, the CIA’s director of security, Howard J. Osborn, sent a memorandum upstairs about a project most Americans had never heard of. The memo was only two pages long. Its subject was **TWO-FOLD**, and its closing instruction contained a small bureaucratic mystery: end one part of the operation, but keep the other.

The part marked for termination had placed secretly recruited personnel inside the Bureau of Narcotics and Dangerous Drugs, or **BNDD**, to detect corruption. The part to be preserved involved nonofficial-cover agents assigned overseas against major drug traffickers. William Colby’s recommendation, approved by the director of central intelligence, was to [“terminate paragraph 2 activity and continue paragraph 3”](https://www.cia.gov/readingroom/docs/DOC_0001451843.pdf)—but only for foreign narcotics collection.

That is a strange way for a project to end.

[IMAGE: Highlighted excerpt from the May 25, 1973 CIA memorandum showing the two TWO-FOLD components and the split disposition.]

The memo said Director Richard Helms had approved the arrangement on **February 12, 1971**. Its origin lay in a request from BNDD director John Ingersoll for help creating an internal-security capability. CIA Security would spot, assess and recruit candidates. Once hired, the recruits would pass to BNDD’s chief inspector for direction and domestic assignments.

Then the project widened. CIA began arranging a second category of personnel: people who would appear to be ordinary BNDD employees while serving abroad under commercial or other nonofficial cover. BNDD paid the cost, but CIA handled concealed administrative details. The memo said the agents themselves were “unaware of any Agency involvement.”

Two years later, the [Rockefeller Commission’s official account](https://www.fordlibrarymuseum.gov/sites/default/files/pdf_documents/library/document/0005/7288238.pdf) supplied the numbers. CIA had recruited **19 people** for BNDD between December 1970 and July 1973. It also placed two CIA agents under commercial cover for BNDD’s overseas work from January 1972 to July 1973. The Commission said BNDD directed them operationally and reimbursed CIA for their salaries and expenses.

The Commission also supplied the judgment that the original memo avoided: “These activities violated the 1947 Act which prohibits the CIA’s participation in law enforcement activities.” CIA was not merely sharing foreign intelligence with a federal police agency. It had helped manufacture a personnel capability inside one.

Yet the paperwork records both termination and survival. Rockefeller reported that the recruiting project ended in July 1973. Osborn’s memo shows that Colby simultaneously preserved the foreign collection function. The contradiction disappears once the project is opened into its two halves: **domestic internal security was stopped; foreign narcotics intelligence remained useful**.

This was not an isolated oddity in the history of drug enforcement. It sat at the junction of two policing systems to which CIA had acquired covert access. One ran through the Agency for International Development’s **Office of Public Safety**. The other ran through federal narcotics enforcement—first the Federal Bureau of Narcotics, then BNDD, and soon the Drug Enforcement Administration.

As the first system came under political attack, the second was expanding.

## 2. TWO PARALLEL DOORS INTO POLICE POWER

Readers of this series already know the first door. AID’s Office of Public Safety, or **OPS**, trained, equipped and advised foreign police forces across the Cold War world. Its programs were presented as technical assistance: communications, records, patrol, criminal investigation, riot control and police management. In practice, that work offered access to the institutions that watched political movements, developed informants and enforced the security priorities of allied governments.

The man at the center of that apparatus was **Byron Engle**, OPS director for more than a decade. His institutional reach extended through the International Police Academy in Washington and into overseas public-safety missions. The academy brought foreign officers to the United States for instruction, while American advisers worked within police organizations abroad. Narcotics enforcement was already part of that ecosystem, including instruction supplied by federal narcotics personnel.

Engle’s position becomes more consequential when read beside a CIA damage-assessment record prepared after his retirement. The document did not merely say he cooperated with the Agency. It warned of the consequences if it became public that Engle, the longtime OPS director, was [“in actual fact a CIA employee”](https://www.archives.gov/files/research/jfk/releases/2023/104-10428-10019.pdf). The same record said he had “retired under cover.”

**CIA had one of its own officers at the top of the Office of Public Safety, serving under cover.**

[IMAGE: Facsimile of the 1976 CIA damage-assessment language identifying Byron Engle as a CIA employee who retired under cover.]

That conclusion does not require an unrecovered appointment order or a speculative reporting chart. It comes from CIA’s own description of the man who ran OPS. It changes how the office must be understood: not as a CIA department, but as an AID program whose chief secretly belonged to the intelligence service that benefited from foreign-police access.

The second door was older and less visible to the public. The Federal Bureau of Narcotics had long relied on overseas relationships, informants and liaison arrangements. When BNDD replaced it in 1968, the new bureau inherited an international problem that ordinary domestic policing could not solve. Heroin supply chains crossed borders; foreign services controlled access; and intelligence gathered overseas did not arrive in courtroom-ready form.

In **November 1969**, Ingersoll asked CIA for guidance, initially focused on the clandestine development and handling of agents and informants abroad. A CIA training survey followed in March 1970. The Agency concluded that BNDD personnel needed broader clandestine tradecraft, not a single narrow course. By April 1972, according to the CIA Office of Training’s [fiscal-year report](https://www.cia.gov/readingroom/document/cia-rdp78-06207a000100060002-9), CIA instructors had briefed 18 BNDD Regional Intelligence Officers.

This chronology matters. **TWO-FOLD did not open the CIA–BNDD relationship. It entered through a door that was already open.** The project formalized a more intrusive personnel arrangement within a relationship that had already moved from consultation to clandestine instruction.

There was a conventional reason for some of this cooperation. The narcotics traffic was real, transnational and capable of corrupting the officials assigned to fight it. BNDD had reason to worry about its own integrity and to want personnel who understood clandestine work. CIA possessed overseas access and tradecraft that BNDD lacked.

But the conventional explanation does not account for the chosen machinery. Advice does not require a proprietary company to conceal recruitment. Foreign liaison does not require recruits to be kept unaware of CIA’s role in their own employment. And protecting an agency from corruption does not erase the statutory problem created when an intelligence service secretly helps build a domestic law-enforcement unit.

[IMAGE: Split diagram showing CIA access into AID/OPS on one side and FBN/BNDD on the other, converging around foreign police, liaison, training and narcotics intelligence.]

The two doors did not make OPS and BNDD the same institution. Their budgets, authorities and missions remained distinct. What connected them was a set of portable functions: foreign liaison, police training, informant development, covert communications, intelligence collection and access to security services abroad. Those functions could move even when an office could not.

That is the transformation TWO-FOLD helps us see.

## 3. WHAT TWO-FOLD ACTUALLY BUILT

### THE PROPRIETARY AND THE 19

The institutional record describes a concealed recruiting system. CIA used a proprietary corporation, placed an officer behind the cover of a research consultant and had candidates interviewed without being told the Agency was behind the process. Selected recruits received a short course in clandestine methods before entering BNDD. According to Rockefeller, CIA then relinquished contact and control over the domestic recruits.

Patrick Fuller later gave Douglas Valentine the view from inside the mechanism. [Fuller told Valentine](https://www.counterpunch.org/2008/01/25/operation-two-fold/): “We recruited the CIA officers for BNDD through a proprietary company.” He described private-business cover being used to make contact and conduct interviews.

That testimony matters because it converges with the official account on the project’s most unusual feature. The CIA memo establishes recruitment for BNDD. Rockefeller identifies the proprietary and counts 19 recruits. Fuller describes how the concealment worked in practice.

The recruits’ stated mission was internal security. BNDD had a genuine corruption problem, and the bureau wanted a cadre capable of investigating its own people. Terry Burke later told Valentine that after his December 1970 hiring he was assigned to examine allegations involving senior narcotics agents. His account is useful precisely because it is less cinematic than the mythology surrounding TWO-FOLD: he described building cases and credibility inside a troubled agency, not conducting a free-floating CIA operation.

The official record supports that distinction. BNDD, not CIA, was supposed to direct the recruits after they entered the bureau. CIA had created the channel and selected the personnel; operational control of the domestic assignments then passed to law enforcement. That division of labor did not satisfy Rockefeller, which still found CIA’s participation unlawful.

### A MAN INSIDE THE MACHINE

Charles Gutensohn’s account gives the arrangement a human scale. Gutensohn later told Valentine that he joined CIA in 1968 and spent roughly two years at Pakse, Laos. When that tour ended, he said, the Agency offered him a choice: return to Laos as CIA liaison to BNDD or enter TWO-FOLD in the United States. His brother Joel had already joined the program.

Gutensohn recalled meetings with Fuller and Jerry Soul, CIA’s security official involved in the project. He also described code names—“Leo Adams” and “Walter DeCarlo”—and cover thin enough that some participants could see through it. That recollection cannot substitute for the missing roster, but it does place a named participant inside a mechanism that official records independently prove existed.

His later career is not dependent on recollection. A federal personnel record identifies [Charles J. Gutensohn as a senior DEA official](https://www.govinfo.gov/app/details/CRI-1991/CRI-1991-GUTENSOHN-CHARLES-J), serving by 1991 as acting deputy assistant administrator for operations. A man who described entering BNDD through TWO-FOLD went on to occupy a high position in its successor.

[IMAGE: Personnel timeline for Charles Gutensohn from CIA service in Laos, through his account of TWO-FOLD, to documented senior DEA service.]

John Ingersoll later confirmed to Valentine that the foreign covered component was tightly held. Regional directors could be unaware of the arrangement. The people involved appeared to belong to BNDD, while administrative support remained concealed. This matches the May 1973 memo’s description of agents presented as bona fide bureau employees while CIA handled details behind the curtain.

The secrecy produced a peculiar chain of responsibility. CIA could say the agents worked for BNDD. BNDD could use personnel whose path into the bureau had been secretly engineered by CIA. The agents themselves could be kept unaware of CIA’s administrative role.

Joseph DiGennaro’s account sits at the outer edge of this evidence. He told Valentine that he had been recruited into the covered foreign side and participated in an overseas seizure and forcible return of a trafficker; he also alleged that lethal force had been authorized if the man resisted. Official records establish that a two-agent, nonofficial-cover foreign component existed, but no recovered operational order confirms DiGennaro’s rendition story or the claimed lethal authority. Other witnesses offered observations consistent with unusual cover arrangements, while the official account said the two agents were directed by BNDD and did not conduct operations in the United States.

The unresolved details should not obscure the established architecture. TWO-FOLD was two related personnel projects: a larger domestic internal-security recruitment effort and a much smaller foreign covered capability. The first supplied human beings to police the narcotics bureau from within. The second hid the administrative hand of CIA behind apparently normal BNDD employment abroad.

That was the “two-fold” design.

## 4. THE PROJECT ENDS. THE RELATIONSHIP DOESN’T.

Formal termination is the easy part of the story. The May 1973 disposition ended the domestic recruiting activity and limited the surviving work to foreign narcotics collection. Rockefeller placed the final termination of CIA’s direct participation in July 1973. On paper, TWO-FOLD was over.

The practical wind-down was less tidy. Former officials later described cases and personnel arrangements that did not obey the clean boundary implied by the closing date. A 1974 inspection memorandum recorded agent Anthony Triponi’s allegation that he and another recruit had been selected in a covert process and were expected to report through Patrick Fuller. Inspectors also recorded that Triponi was acutely ill, having difficulty determining reality and at times becoming incoherent. The memorandum proves the allegation was made contemporaneously; it does not establish that the channel worked exactly as he described it.

That distinction is more useful than either credulity or dismissal. Bureaucratic projects rarely vanish the moment a termination memo is signed. Training, assignments, personal loyalties and cover arrangements leave residue. Triponi’s statement is evidence of a contested wind-down, not a license to redefine every later anomaly as secret TWO-FOLD activity.

[IMAGE: Layered 1973–74 timeline separating formal termination, preserved foreign collection, practical wind-down allegations and later personnel transfers.]

A separate personnel bridge is better documented. In 1973 and 1974, seven former CIA special agents moved into BNDD and DEA as the new agency built a more sophisticated narcotics-intelligence capability. Administrative litigation over their retirement credit later preserved testimony about the arrangement. CIA supported the recruitment, and William Colby backed it as a way for DEA to develop its own capacity and relieve CIA of an intelligence obligation.

**Those seven transferees are not established as the 19 TWO-FOLD recruits.** They belong to a later, separate arrangement. Their significance is not that they prove the project secretly continued; it is that personnel and expertise continued to move from CIA into federal narcotics intelligence immediately after TWO-FOLD’s formal end.

Thomas C. Tripodi offers an earlier version of the same personnel traffic. The administrative record traces his path from the Federal Bureau of Narcotics to roughly five and a half years at CIA, then back to BNDD to help address internal corruption. He described strong similarities between his intelligence and narcotics duties: surveillance, interviews, recruitment, interrogation, reporting and liaison with local police.

Tripodi’s career does not make him a TWO-FOLD recruit. It demonstrates that the institutional boundary had long been permeable. Skills, retirement promises and people crossed it in both directions.

The result was continuity without the need for a single immortal project. The project name ended. A foreign collection function had been expressly preserved. CIA-trained and CIA-experienced personnel remained inside narcotics enforcement, while a new group arrived to build DEA intelligence. Liaison between the agencies continued because each still possessed something the other wanted.

This is where the story becomes larger than TWO-FOLD. A covert operation can terminate while the capability it assembled becomes ordinary personnel, practice and institutional memory. The more durable legacy is often not the code name. It is the workforce.

## 5. THE NARCOTICS EXCEPTION BECOMES A BRIDGE

At the same moment, Congress was closing the first door. Revelations about overseas police programs, political repression and the techniques taught under American sponsorship had made OPS politically toxic. Congress enacted **Section 660** of the Foreign Assistance Act in December 1974, generally barring U.S. assistance to foreign police and prisons after July 1, 1975.

But the prohibition was not absolute. The [statutory text preserved international narcotics control](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/STATUTE-88/pdf/STATUTE-88-Pg1795.pdf), including certain activities of the Drug Enforcement Administration and Federal Bureau of Investigation. The law that shut down the broad public-safety program left a channel open for drug enforcement.

That exception did not spring from nowhere. OPS and the International Police Academy already treated narcotics work as a police-assistance function. Federal narcotics agents had instructed foreign officers. BNDD had built overseas liaison and intelligence capacity, and CIA had trained its regional intelligence officers in clandestine methods. TWO-FOLD’s foreign component was one more piece of that preexisting structure.

Laos supplies the concrete handoff. An August 1974 AID document from Vientiane said OPS narcotics assistance was scheduled to end with fiscal year 1975. It also said DEA would assume advisory responsibility involving the Lao National Police, Military Police and General Staff Intelligence. One agency’s public-safety program was closing; the narcotics agency would carry a defined advisory function forward.

[IMAGE: Annotated excerpt from the August 28, 1974 Vientiane A-102 program document showing the scheduled end of OPS narcotics assistance and DEA’s assumption of advisory responsibility.]

The Laos record does not establish a worldwide transfer of OPS into DEA. It establishes something more precise and more defensible: **an OPS narcotics-advisory function was actually handed to DEA**. The recipient already had foreign offices, intelligence officers, clandestine training and a continuing relationship with CIA.

Now put the pieces in chronological order. CIA had covert access at the top of OPS through Engle. It had a longstanding working relationship with federal narcotics enforcement. BNDD asked for clandestine instruction in 1969; TWO-FOLD recruited personnel beginning in 1970; its foreign component was preserved in 1973; CIA-experienced officers moved into DEA intelligence in 1973–74; and Section 660 preserved narcotics work as the wider police-assistance system was dismantled.

No institutional merger is needed to explain the continuity. The better model is **functional migration across distinct agencies**. Foreign-police liaison, training and intelligence work could survive under the politically protected heading of narcotics control. People who knew the old terrain could continue through a different legal and bureaucratic doorway.

This also explains why the series has arrived at narcotics. OPS did not simply disappear from history when Congress cut off police assistance. Some of its most valuable relationships sat in fields with independent statutory life. Drug enforcement was one of them, and it linked foreign security services to an American agency whose overseas mandate was growing.

The conventional case for this system was formidable. Heroin moved through countries where U.S. law-enforcement officers could not act alone. Cooperation with intelligence and local police could locate laboratories, identify routes and penetrate organizations. Section 660’s exception recognized that a blanket ban could cripple a legitimate response to international trafficking.

The danger appeared when intelligence collection and criminal prosecution aimed at the same people under different rules. The CIA could collect information it would never expose. DEA had to build a case it could eventually defend in court. By the middle of the decade, that conflict was no longer theoretical.

DEA’s own officials were writing warnings about it.

## 6. WHEN INTELLIGENCE MADE PROSECUTION IMPOSSIBLE

The memoranda are blunt because the mechanism was simple. CIA wanted to protect foreign surveillance methods, liaison partners and sensitive operations. DEA wanted to arrest traffickers and prosecute them in the United States. A defendant, however, had a right to seek information relevant to the case. If CIA refused to answer discovery, a prosecution contaminated by secret intelligence could collapse.

In a memorandum to DEA administrator Peter Bensinger, enforcement chief **Daniel P. Casey** warned that the problem had been documented by DEA and BNDD for years. Even a case built independently could be endangered if the defendant had appeared in CIA electronic surveillance. Casey predicted dismissals and refusals to indict major traffickers if the programs continued.

His conclusion was extraordinary. Once traffickers appeared in CIA-promoted surveillance, he wrote, they could become [“virtually immune from prosecution in the United States”](https://www.counterpunch.org/2008/01/25/operation-two-fold/) because the Agency could not respond positively to discovery. The resulting **“de facto immunity”** would let them operate more openly and effectively.

[IMAGE: Highlighted excerpt from Daniel P. Casey’s DEA memorandum containing “virtually immune from prosecution” and “de facto immunity.”]

Casey then described the feedback loop. CIA had asked DEA for telephone numbers to target overseas, and DEA had provided them. In his words, that placed DEA in the position of determining which violators would receive practical immunity. Cooperation intended to improve collection could poison the prosecution it was supposed to serve.

Jerry Strickler, a DEA operations official responsible for Europe and the Middle East, supplied cases rather than abstractions in a **February 11, 1977** memorandum. In one, foreign officials offered to deport a major fugitive to the United States. CIA then disclosed that the man had been subject to a CIA-coordinated electronic surveillance likely to trigger dismissal after discovery. The offer was not accepted, and the trafficker was released.

In another example, a prosecutor was prepared to indict the head of a trafficking organization and a major domestic distributor. After learning of the CIA collection effort, he declined. Strickler wrote that a separate CIA surveillance “immunized” not only the principal but numerous contacts surfaced through the operation. He also described a DEA target recruited by CIA with the help of confidential DEA information; CIA commitments then precluded a successful prosecution.

These memoranda do not show CIA deliberately granting immunity as a favor to traffickers. They show something structurally more disturbing. Intelligence operations pursued for collection value could create immunity as a consequence of secrecy. A target useful enough to watch—or recruit—could become impossible to prosecute without exposing how the watching was done.

That conflict completes the arc opened by the May 1973 TWO-FOLD memo. CIA’s value to narcotics enforcement was access: covert personnel, overseas services, clandestine methods and collection unavailable to ordinary police. The same secrecy that made that access valuable could make its product unusable in court.

TWO-FOLD crossed the line identified by Rockefeller when CIA secretly helped build personnel capacity inside a law-enforcement agency. Its domestic component ended, but foreign narcotics work was preserved. Separate transfers then carried CIA experience into DEA, while the narcotics exception kept foreign advisory work alive as OPS contracted.

The story is not that CIA “became” DEA, or that every narcotics officer answered to Langley. It is that American policing and intelligence had become interdependent in one of the few foreign-police channels Congress left open. That interdependence could solve problems neither agency could solve alone. It could also leave each agency unable to perform its basic public function without compromising the other.

For CIA, disclosure could destroy an intelligence operation.

For DEA, secrecy could destroy a criminal case.

And for a major trafficker caught between them, the collision could look very much like immunity.

## FAQ

### What was Operation TWO-FOLD?

TWO-FOLD was a CIA-supported personnel project for BNDD approved in 1971. CIA secretly recruited people for a domestic internal-security unit and supported a smaller nonofficial-cover component for foreign narcotics work.

### Did CIA control DEA?

The evidence does not show institutional CIA control of DEA. It shows covert CIA help building BNDD personnel capacity, continued foreign intelligence cooperation and later movement of CIA-experienced officers into DEA intelligence.

### Was Byron Engle really CIA while running OPS?

Yes, according to CIA’s own 1976 damage assessment. It identified the longtime OPS director as “in actual fact a CIA employee” and said he retired under cover.

### Did TWO-FOLD end in 1973?

Its domestic recruitment activity was ordered terminated, and Rockefeller dated CIA participation’s end to July 1973. The May 1973 disposition expressly preserved foreign narcotics collection, while later personnel transfers and liaison were separate arrangements rather than proven continuation of the project itself.

### What does TWO-FOLD have to do with USAID?

TWO-FOLD reveals the parallel narcotics channel operating as AID’s foreign-police program contracted. In Laos, an OPS narcotics-advisory function was handed to DEA, while Congress’s Section 660 ban preserved an exception for international narcotics control.
