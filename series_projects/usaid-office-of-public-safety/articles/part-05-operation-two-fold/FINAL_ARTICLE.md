# USAID & THE CIA, PART 5: OPERATION TWO-FOLD

## 1. THE ORDER TO END TWO-FOLD—AND KEEP PART OF IT

On **May 25, 1973**, the CIA’s director of security, Howard J. Osborn, sent a memorandum upstairs about a project most Americans had never heard of. The memo was only two pages long. Its subject was **TWO-FOLD**, and its closing instruction contained a small bureaucratic mystery: end one part of the operation, but keep the other.

The part marked for termination had placed secretly recruited personnel inside the Bureau of Narcotics and Dangerous Drugs, or **BNDD**, to detect corruption. The part to be preserved involved nonofficial-cover agents assigned overseas against major drug traffickers. William Colby’s recommendation, approved by the director of central intelligence, was to [“terminate paragraph 2 activity and continue paragraph 3”](https://www.cia.gov/readingroom/docs/DOC_0001451843.pdf)—but only for foreign narcotics collection.

That is a strange way for a project to end.

[IMAGE: Highlighted excerpt from the May 25, 1973 CIA memorandum showing the two TWO-FOLD components and the split disposition | ALT: CIA memorandum dated May 25, 1973, with the domestic termination and foreign continuation language highlighted.]

The memo said Director Richard Helms had approved the arrangement on **February 12, 1971**. It traced the project to a request from BNDD director John Ingersoll for help creating an internal-security capability. CIA Security would spot, assess and recruit candidates. Once hired, the recruits would pass to BNDD’s chief inspector for direction and domestic assignments.

Then the project widened. CIA began arranging a second category of personnel: people who would appear to be ordinary BNDD employees while serving abroad under commercial or other nonofficial cover. BNDD paid the cost, but CIA handled concealed administrative details. The memo said the agents themselves were “unaware of any Agency involvement.”

Two years later, the [Rockefeller Commission’s official account](https://www.fordlibrarymuseum.gov/sites/default/files/pdf_documents/library/document/0005/7288238.pdf) supplied the numbers. CIA had recruited **19 people** for BNDD between December 1970 and July 1973. It also placed two CIA agents under commercial cover for BNDD’s overseas work from January 1972 to July 1973. The Commission said BNDD directed them operationally and reimbursed CIA for their salaries and expenses.

The Commission also supplied the judgment that the original memo avoided: “These activities violated the 1947 Act which prohibits the CIA’s participation in law enforcement activities.” CIA had gone beyond passing foreign intelligence to federal agents. It had secretly helped BNDD build an internal personnel capability.

The paperwork records both termination and survival. Rockefeller reported that CIA’s recruiting work and the two covered-agent arrangements ended in July 1973. Osborn’s memo, written two months earlier, records approval to preserve foreign narcotics collection. The implementation records needed to reconcile those two endpoints have not surfaced: the foreign work may have ended in July, continued with different personnel, or passed into another channel.

What the record does establish is more precise. **The domestic internal-security activity was ordered stopped, while foreign narcotics intelligence was selected for continuation.**

This project sat between two policing systems to which CIA had secured covert access. One ran through the Agency for International Development’s **Office of Public Safety**. The other ran through federal narcotics enforcement—first the Federal Bureau of Narcotics, then BNDD, and soon the Drug Enforcement Administration.

As political pressure closed in on the first system, the second was expanding.

## 2. TWO PARALLEL DOORS INTO POLICE POWER

[Readers of this series already know the first door](https://thewhiterabbitreport.substack.com/p/usaid-and-the-cia-series-part-1). AID’s Office of Public Safety, or **OPS**, trained, equipped and advised foreign police forces across the Cold War world. Its programs covered communications, records, patrol, criminal investigation, riot control and police management. That work also brought American advisers close to the institutions that tracked political movements, developed informants and enforced the security priorities of allied governments.

The man at the center of that apparatus was **Byron Engle**, OPS director for more than a decade. His reach extended through the International Police Academy in Washington and into overseas public-safety missions. The academy brought foreign officers to the United States for instruction, while American advisers worked inside police organizations abroad. Federal narcotics personnel also taught in that training system.

Engle’s position becomes more consequential when read beside a CIA damage assessment prepared after his retirement. The document went much further than cooperation. It warned of the consequences if it became public that Engle, the longtime OPS director, was [“in actual fact a CIA employee”](https://www.archives.gov/files/research/jfk/releases/2023/104-10428-10019.pdf). The same record said he had “retired under cover.”

**CIA had one of its own officers at the top of the Office of Public Safety, serving under cover.**

[IMAGE: Facsimile of the 1976 CIA damage-assessment language identifying Byron Engle as a CIA employee who retired under cover | ALT: September 29, 1976, CIA document stating that Byron Engle was in actual fact a CIA employee and retired under cover.]

CIA’s own description supports that conclusion without supplying the missing command chart. OPS remained an AID office, not a CIA department. But the official who directed it secretly belonged to the intelligence service that benefited from access to foreign police.

The second door was older and less visible to the public. The [FBN had long maintained an overseas intelligence-and-liaison apparatus](https://museum.dea.gov/exhibits/online-exhibits/anslinger/cold-war-narcotics-control), working with foreign police and developing sources beyond American jurisdiction. When BNDD replaced it in 1968, the new bureau inherited a problem ordinary domestic policing could not solve. Heroin supply chains crossed borders, foreign services controlled access, and intelligence gathered overseas did not necessarily arrive in courtroom-ready form.

In **November 1969**, Ingersoll asked CIA for guidance, initially focused on the clandestine development and handling of agents and informants abroad. A CIA training survey followed in March 1970. The Agency concluded that BNDD personnel needed broader clandestine tradecraft, not a single narrow course. By April 1972, according to the CIA Office of Training’s fiscal-year report, CIA instructors had briefed [18 BNDD Regional Intelligence Officers](https://www.cia.gov/readingroom/document/cia-rdp78-06207a000100060002-9).

**TWO-FOLD did not open the CIA–BNDD relationship. It entered through a door that was already open.** By then the relationship had moved from consultation into clandestine instruction; TWO-FOLD added covert recruitment and covered personnel.

There was a conventional reason for cooperation. Narcotics trafficking was transnational, and corruption inside BNDD was not imaginary. The bureau wanted investigators who understood clandestine work. CIA had overseas access and tradecraft BNDD lacked.

But the machinery went beyond ordinary advice. CIA used a proprietary company to hide its recruiting role. Some foreign staff agents could be kept unaware of CIA’s administrative involvement. And an intelligence service was helping build a domestic law-enforcement unit despite a statutory bar Rockefeller would later say the arrangement violated.

[IMAGE: Split diagram showing documented CIA access into AID/OPS on one side and FBN/BNDD on the other, converging around foreign police, liaison, training and narcotics intelligence | ALT: Explanatory diagram of the separate AID public-safety and federal narcotics systems, with documented CIA relationships to each.]

OPS and BNDD remained distinct institutions with different budgets and authorities. The overlap lay in functions both systems needed: foreign liaison, police training, informant development, clandestine communications and access to security services abroad. The records that follow show some of those functions surviving on the narcotics side, while intelligence personnel continued to move across the CIA–narcotics boundary as OPS contracted.

That is the transformation TWO-FOLD helps us see.

## 3. WHAT TWO-FOLD ACTUALLY BUILT

### THE PROPRIETARY AND THE 19

The official record describes a concealed recruiting system. CIA used a proprietary corporation, placed an officer behind the cover of a research consultant and interviewed candidates without telling them the Agency was involved. Selected recruits received a short course in clandestine methods before entering BNDD. According to Rockefeller, CIA then relinquished contact and control over the domestic recruits.

Patrick Fuller later gave Douglas Valentine the view from inside the mechanism. [Fuller told Valentine](https://www.counterpunch.org/2008/01/25/operation-two-fold/): “We recruited the CIA officers for BNDD through a proprietary company.” He described private-business cover being used to make contact and conduct interviews.

Here the testimony and the official record meet. The CIA memo establishes recruitment for BNDD. Rockefeller identifies the proprietary and counts 19 recruits. Fuller describes how the concealed approach worked.

The recruits’ stated mission was internal security. BNDD had a real corruption problem and wanted a cadre capable of investigating its own personnel. Terry Burke later told Valentine that after his December 1970 hiring he was assigned to examine allegations involving senior narcotics agents. Burke described building cases inside a troubled bureau, not taking orders on a free-floating CIA operation.

Rockefeller drew the same operational boundary: BNDD, not CIA, directed the recruits after they entered the bureau. CIA nevertheless created the channel, screened the candidates and provided clandestine training. That was enough for the Commission to find that CIA had crossed the law-enforcement line.

### A MAN INSIDE THE MACHINE

Charles Gutensohn’s account gives the arrangement a human scale. Gutensohn later told Valentine that he joined CIA in 1968 and spent roughly two years at Pakse, Laos. When that tour ended, he said, the Agency offered him a choice: return to Laos as CIA liaison to BNDD or enter TWO-FOLD in the United States. His brother Joel had already joined the program.

Gutensohn recalled meetings with Fuller and Jerry Soul, the CIA security official associated with the project. He also described code names—“Leo Adams” and “Walter DeCarlo”—and cover thin enough for some participants to see through it. His account remains firsthand testimony rather than an official roster entry. The program and its recruiting mechanism are independently documented; the missing roster has not confirmed his membership or CIA biography.

His later career is documentary fact. A federal personnel record identifies [Charles J. Gutensohn as a senior DEA official](https://www.govinfo.gov/app/details/CRI-1991/CRI-1991-GUTENSOHN-CHARLES-J), serving by 1991 as acting deputy assistant administrator for operations. A man who described entering BNDD through TWO-FOLD later occupied a high position in its successor.

[IMAGE: Personnel timeline for Charles Gutensohn from his attributed CIA service in Laos, through his TWO-FOLD account, to documented senior DEA service | ALT: Timeline distinguishing Charles Gutensohn’s reported CIA and TWO-FOLD history from his documented senior DEA position in 1991.]

John Ingersoll later told Valentine that the foreign covered component was tightly held and that regional directors could be kept outside the circle. The people involved appeared to belong to BNDD, while administrative support remained hidden. That account matches the May 1973 memo on compartmentation, although the surviving official records do not identify the two covered agents.

The arrangement created a peculiar chain of responsibility. CIA could say the agents worked for BNDD. BNDD could use personnel whose route into the bureau CIA had secretly engineered. The agents themselves could be kept unaware of CIA’s administrative role.

Joseph DiGennaro’s account sits at the disputed edge of this record. He told Valentine that Fuller recruited him into a deeper foreign program. In one alleged Colombia mission, DiGennaro said a team located a cocaine laboratory, seized the men operating it, drugged and handcuffed them, and returned them to the United States. Valentine’s account, attributed to DiGennaro, describes the assignment more broadly as kidnapping traffickers and killing them if they resisted.

Official records confirm a two-agent, commercially covered foreign component targeting international traffickers. They do not identify DiGennaro, confirm the Colombia operation, or document lethal authority. Ingersoll’s account supports tight compartmentation but says the agents were not operational inside the United States; other witnesses described unusual absences and safe-house arrivals without establishing who controlled the alleged missions. The operational claims remain unresolved.

The documentary architecture stands on its own. TWO-FOLD had a larger domestic internal-security recruitment effort and a much smaller foreign covered component. The first supplied covertly recruited people to police BNDD from within. The second concealed CIA administration behind apparently normal BNDD employment abroad.

**The surviving record gives us two distinct halves of the operation. It does not tell us why the cryptonym TWO-FOLD was chosen.**

## 4. THE PROJECT ENDS. THE PEOPLE KEEP MOVING.

Formal termination is the easy part of the story. The May 1973 disposition ended the domestic activity and authorized only foreign narcotics collection to continue. Rockefeller placed the end of CIA recruiting and the two covered-agent arrangements in July 1973. On paper, TWO-FOLD was over.

On paper, the project was dead. The 1974 files are messier. A 1974 inspection memorandum recorded agent Anthony Triponi alleging that he and another recruit had been selected through a covert process and were expected to report through Patrick Fuller. The inspectors also recorded that Triponi was acutely ill, had difficulty determining reality and at times became incoherent. The record establishes that he made the allegation; it does not establish the channel’s existence as he described it.

That leaves the episode where it belongs: evidence of a disputed residue, not proof that the intact domestic project continued after July 1973. Assignments, training, personal relationships and cover arrangements do not necessarily vanish when headquarters closes a project. Whether Triponi had encountered one of those remnants remains unanswered.

[IMAGE: Layered 1973–74 timeline separating formal domestic termination, authorized foreign collection, the disputed Triponi allegation and the separate CIA-to-DEA transfers | ALT: Timeline showing four distinct strands after May 1973 rather than one continuous program.]

A separate personnel bridge is better documented. In 1973 and 1974, seven former CIA special agents moved into BNDD and DEA while the new agency built its own sophisticated narcotics-intelligence capability. Administrative litigation over their retirement credit preserved testimony about the arrangement. CIA supported their recruitment, and William Colby backed it as a way for DEA to develop its own capability and relieve CIA of that obligation.

**Those seven transferees are not established as the 19 TWO-FOLD recruits.** They entered through a later, separately documented arrangement. What moved across the boundary was still consequential: officers with CIA experience, knowledge of foreign operations and—in several accounts—existing liaison or informant relationships.

Thomas C. Tripodi offers an earlier personnel bridge. The administrative record traces his path from the Federal Bureau of Narcotics to roughly five and a half years at CIA, then back to BNDD to help address internal corruption. He described strong similarities between his intelligence and narcotics duties: surveillance, interviews, recruitment, interrogation, reporting and liaison with local police.

Tripodi’s career does not place him on the TWO-FOLD roster. It shows that the personnel boundary between intelligence and narcotics enforcement was already permeable. The seven later transfers then moved another group across it for a new DEA intelligence mission.

The code name ended; the agencies still had reasons to work together. Foreign narcotics collection had been selected for continuation, CIA-experienced personnel were serving inside narcotics enforcement, and another group arrived to build DEA’s intelligence capability. None of that proves the original project survived unchanged. It shows how a capability can outlast the file that first assembled it—through people, training and working relationships rather than a single unbroken program.

The more durable legacy was not necessarily the cryptonym.

It was the workforce.

## 5. THE NARCOTICS EXCEPTION BECOMES A BRIDGE

At the same moment, Congress was closing the first door. Revelations about overseas police programs, political repression and the techniques taught under American sponsorship had made OPS politically toxic. Congress enacted **Section 660** of the Foreign Assistance Act in December 1974, generally barring U.S. assistance to foreign police and prisons after July 1, 1975.

But the prohibition was not absolute. The [statutory text preserved international narcotics control](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/STATUTE-88/pdf/STATUTE-88-Pg1795.pdf), including certain activities of the Drug Enforcement Administration and Federal Bureau of Investigation. Congress shut down the broad public-safety program while keeping a legal channel open for drug enforcement.

The exception fit machinery already in place. OPS and the International Police Academy had treated narcotics as a police-assistance subject. Federal narcotics agents taught foreign officers. BNDD had overseas offices and a police-liaison and training post, while CIA had briefed its Regional Intelligence Officers in clandestine methods. TWO-FOLD’s foreign component added covered personnel to that larger narcotics structure.

Laos supplies the concrete handoff. The State Department’s public copy of the August 1974 [Vientiane A-102 program submission](https://foia.state.gov/DOCUMENTS/FOIA_Micro_Oct2024_10/F-1989-03959/DOC_0C09000035/C09000035.pdf) said OPS narcotics assistance was scheduled to end with fiscal year 1975. It also assigned DEA advisory responsibility involving the Lao National Police, Military Police and General Staff Intelligence. OPS was leaving the narcotics-advisory role; DEA was scheduled to take it over.

[IMAGE: Annotated excerpt from Vientiane A-102 showing the scheduled end of OPS narcotics assistance and DEA’s advisory responsibility | ALT: August 1974 AID program document identifying OPS and DEA roles in the Laos narcotics-control program.]

The Laos record gives us one documented transfer: an OPS narcotics function assigned to DEA. DEA already had foreign offices, intelligence personnel, clandestine training and a continuing liaison relationship with CIA when it received the role. The handoff is specific to Laos, not a worldwide transfer order.

Now put the chronology together. CIA had covert access at the top of OPS through Engle and a longstanding working relationship with federal narcotics enforcement. BNDD requested clandestine instruction in 1969; CIA recruited for TWO-FOLD beginning in late 1970; foreign collection was selected for continuation in 1973; former CIA officers entered DEA intelligence in 1973–74; and Section 660 preserved narcotics work as the broader police-assistance program closed.

**The office disappeared. Some of the work found another home.** In Laos, the transfer is explicit. More broadly, the law left foreign narcotics work standing while DEA already performed foreign liaison, training and intelligence functions. This is what functional migration looks like across distinct agencies.

This is why a story about BNDD belongs in a series about USAID. OPS did not simply evaporate when Congress cut off police assistance. Some of the work Washington still valued occupied fields with separate legal authority. International narcotics control was one of them.

The conventional case was serious. Heroin moved through countries where American agents could not operate alone. Intelligence and police cooperation could locate laboratories, identify routes and penetrate organizations. Section 660’s exception reflected the judgment that a blanket ban could cripple a legitimate response to cross-border trafficking.

The danger appeared when intelligence collectors and criminal investigators targeted the same people under different rules. CIA could gather information it would never expose. DEA had to build a case it could defend in court. By the middle of the decade, DEA’s own officials were warning that those goals could collide.

## 6. WHEN INTELLIGENCE MADE PROSECUTION IMPOSSIBLE

The memoranda are blunt because the mechanism was simple. CIA wanted to protect foreign surveillance methods, liaison partners and sensitive operations. DEA wanted to arrest traffickers and prosecute them in the United States. A defendant had a right to seek information relevant to the case. If CIA refused discovery, a prosecution touched by secret intelligence could collapse.

In a memorandum to DEA administrator Peter Bensinger, enforcement chief **Daniel P. Casey** wrote that DEA and BNDD had documented the problem for more than four years. Even a case built independently could be endangered if the defendant had appeared in CIA electronic surveillance. Casey expected more dismissals and refusals to indict high-level traffickers if the programs continued.

His warning was extraordinary. Once traffickers surfaced in CIA-promoted surveillance, he wrote, they could become [“virtually immune from prosecution in the United States”](https://www.counterpunch.org/2008/01/25/operation-two-fold/) because the Agency would not respond positively to discovery. He called the result **“de facto immunity.”** The link provides a public reproduction; the factual basis here is Casey’s underlying DEA memorandum in the recovered corpus.

[IMAGE: Highlighted excerpt from Daniel P. Casey’s DEA memorandum containing “virtually immune from prosecution” and “de facto immunity” | ALT: DEA memorandum warning that CIA surveillance could give traffickers de facto immunity from United States prosecution.]

Casey also described the feedback loop. CIA had asked DEA for telephone numbers to target overseas, and DEA had complied. That put DEA, he wrote, in the position of deciding which violators would receive practical immunity. Cooperation intended to improve intelligence collection could poison the prosecution it was meant to support.

Jerry Strickler, then a DEA operations official responsible for Europe and the Middle East, answered with case examples in a **February 11, 1977** memorandum. These are DEA’s descriptions of redacted events; the underlying case files have not been recovered.

In one example, foreign officials offered to deport a major DEA fugitive to the United States. Before DEA accepted, CIA notified the agency that the man had been subject to CIA-coordinated electronic surveillance likely to produce dismissal after discovery. Strickler wrote that the offer was not accepted and the trafficker was released.

In another, a federal prosecutor was prepared to indict the head of a trafficking organization and a major domestic distributor. After learning of CIA’s intelligence collection, he declined. Strickler characterized a separate CIA surveillance as immunizing not only its principal target but the numerous traffickers who had contacted him. He also described a DEA target recruited by CIA using confidential DEA information; in DEA’s assessment, CIA commitments then prevented a successful prosecution.

These memoranda do not say CIA deliberately granted immunity as a favor to traffickers. They document DEA leaders’ conclusion that immunity could arise from the relationship itself. A target valuable enough to surveil—or recruit—could become impossible to prosecute without exposing the intelligence operation around him.

That conflict completes the arc opened by the May 1973 TWO-FOLD memo. CIA’s value to narcotics enforcement was access: covert personnel, overseas services, clandestine methods and collection unavailable to ordinary police. The secrecy that made that access useful could make its product unusable in court.

TWO-FOLD crossed the line Rockefeller identified when CIA secretly helped BNDD build personnel capacity. Its domestic component ended, foreign collection was selected for preservation, and separate transfers carried CIA experience into DEA while the narcotics exception kept a foreign-police channel open as OPS closed.

The record does not show CIA becoming DEA or every narcotics officer answering to Langley. It shows two agencies becoming interdependent in a field Congress had left open. That relationship could solve problems neither could solve alone. It could also leave each unable to perform its public function without damaging the other.

For CIA, disclosure could destroy an intelligence operation.

For DEA, secrecy could destroy a criminal case.

And for a major trafficker caught between them, the collision could look very much like immunity.

## FAQ

### What was Operation TWO-FOLD?

TWO-FOLD was a CIA-supported personnel project for BNDD approved in 1971. CIA secretly recruited people for a domestic internal-security unit and supported a smaller nonofficial-cover component for foreign narcotics work.

### Did CIA control DEA?

The evidence does not show institutional CIA control of DEA. It shows covert CIA help building BNDD personnel capacity, continued foreign intelligence cooperation and later movement of CIA-experienced officers into DEA intelligence.

### Was Byron Engle really CIA while running OPS?

Yes, according to CIA’s own 1976 damage assessment. It identified the longtime OPS director as “in actual fact a CIA employee” and said he retired under cover.

### Did TWO-FOLD end in 1973?

Its domestic activity was ordered terminated, and Rockefeller dated the end of CIA recruiting and the two covered-agent arrangements to July 1973. The May 1973 disposition had selected foreign narcotics collection for continuation; missing implementation records leave its exact post-July form unresolved.

### What does TWO-FOLD have to do with USAID?

TWO-FOLD reveals the parallel narcotics channel operating as AID’s foreign-police program contracted. In Laos, an OPS narcotics-advisory function was assigned to DEA, while Congress’s Section 660 ban preserved an exception for international narcotics control.
