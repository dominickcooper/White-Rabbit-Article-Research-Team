# PHASE 3B — EVIDENCE RECALIBRATION & SECONDARY-SOURCE RECOVERY

## USAID & THE CIA, Part 5: Operation TWO-FOLD

**Status:** Research and article architecture only. No article prose has been drafted.

**Purpose:** This memorandum corrects an evidentiary imbalance in the first synthesis. The initial pass was right to prevent unsupported allegations from being converted into fact, but at points it treated the absence of recovered primary corroboration as if it were the absence of evidence. This recalibration preserves the documentary floor while restoring properly attributed firsthand testimony and carefully sourced investigative reconstruction to the usable record.

The controlling distinction is no longer “primary document or exclude.” It is:

- what the record proves;
- what a named participant says happened;
- what an investigator reconstructs from identifiable sources;
- what remains disputed, contradicted, anonymous, or source-unresolved.

The earlier corpus accounting remains unchanged: all 155 Part 5 source files were accounted for; 134 were substantively reviewed, 20 were verified duplicates, and one unusable extraction was marked `OCR_BLOCKED`. Nothing in `sources/` was modified.

## 1. Why the previous evidentiary standard was too restrictive

The previous standard correctly rejected a common investigative failure: beginning with Douglas Valentine’s thesis and treating every document as confirmation. But it leaned too far in the other direction in three ways.

First, it sometimes collapsed several different propositions into the label **“Valentine only.”** That label could mean any of the following:

1. Valentine offered an inference without disclosing a source.
2. Valentine attributed a claim to an anonymous source.
3. Valentine reported a named participant’s firsthand account.
4. Valentine quoted a participant directly but the interview transcript was not in this corpus.
5. Valentine reconstructed an event from a primary document that is itself in the corpus.
6. Valentine’s claim was later or independently corroborated at least in part by an official record.

Those are not evidentially equivalent.

Second, the earlier standard risked treating unrecovered records as negative evidence. Failure to recover the original 19-person roster, William Colby’s termination direction, Patrick Fuller’s working files, or interview tapes does not establish that a named witness was wrong. It limits what the article may state as documented fact. It does not erase the testimony.

Third, covert programs are unusually likely to leave fragmentary, euphemistic, compartmented, or retrospective records. The absence of an operational cable or personnel list is therefore relevant but not dispositive. At the same time, the clandestine context cannot become a license to accept every allegation. It requires clearer attribution, source-proximity analysis, corroboration checks, and explicit treatment of conflicts.

The corrected standard is additive:

- Primary records establish the documentary floor.
- Named firsthand testimony may add personnel, mechanics, intent, and lived experience, but must remain attributed where the record does not independently prove it.
- Investigative secondary work may connect records and testimony, but its inference must not be rewritten as the source’s direct knowledge.
- Extraordinary claims receive more, not less, scrutiny.
- A witness can be credible about one subject and uncorroborated, mistaken, self-protective, or unreliable about another.

This change does **not** lower the threshold for declarative factual prose. It improves the vocabulary used to describe different kinds of evidence.

## 2. Revised evidence hierarchy

### Level A — Contemporaneous primary record

A record created during or close to the event by an institution or participant positioned to know about it: memoranda, personnel records, inspection reports, cables, operational histories, financial records, hearing exhibits, or court filings.

**Permitted use:** Ordinarily may support a declarative factual statement about what the record says or did. The author, institutional purpose, classification context, omissions, and possible self-protection still matter.

**Examples in this project:**

- the May 25, 1973 CIA memorandum naming TWO-FOLD, dating approval, describing both components, and recommending different dispositions;
- the 1974 internal inspection memoranda recording Anthony Triponi’s statements about a covert reporting arrangement with Patrick Fuller;
- the 1976–77 DEA memoranda warning that CIA equities could confer practical immunity and destroy prosecutions;
- official personnel histories describing Thomas C. Tripodi’s FBN, CIA, and BNDD service.

### Level B — Official retrospective or investigative primary record

An official commission report, inspector’s reconstruction, internal history, damage assessment, or sworn institutional review produced after the events.

**Permitted use:** Strong evidence of official findings and institutional knowledge. It may support factual claims when corroborated or when describing the official finding precisely. It is not automatically complete or neutral.

**Examples:**

- the Rockefeller Commission’s Chapter 17 account of 19 recruits, two covered agents, termination, and statutory violation;
- the CIA’s 1976 damage assessment identifying Byron Engle as a CIA employee who directed AID’s Office of Public Safety under cover;
- the CIA Office of Training retrospective covering BNDD’s request for clandestine overseas capability.

### Level C — Named firsthand witness

A named participant or direct observer describing events within personal knowledge, whether in a recorded interview, published oral history, memoir, sworn statement, or interview notes cited with enough specificity to identify the speaker.

**Permitted use:** May be included with explicit attribution. Weight depends on proximity, specificity, internal consistency, timing, motive, corroboration, and contradiction. A participant’s account of his own recruitment or conduct generally carries more weight than his account of another compartment.

**Examples:**

- Patrick Fuller describing the proprietary-company recruitment mechanism;
- Charles Gutensohn describing his CIA service and TWO-FOLD recruitment meeting;
- Terry Burke describing his recruitment and BNDD assignment;
- Joseph DiGennaro describing his own recruitment and alleged overseas operations;
- John Ingersoll describing the purpose and restricted visibility of the foreign covered-agent component;
- George Brosan describing the program’s practical winding down.

### Level D — Investigative secondary reconstruction

A named author’s synthesis based on documents, interviews, and contextual reporting, where the underlying evidence is partly identifiable but the author is not a participant.

**Permitted use:** May guide research and may appear in the article with attribution when it adds a defensible interpretation. It cannot silently inherit Level A or C status. Claims should be reverse-sourced whenever possible.

**Examples:**

- Valentine’s reconstruction of the relationship among TWO-FOLD, Fuller’s internal-security apparatus, and later DEA structures;
- Peter Dale Scott’s network analysis where an exact source chain can be shown;
- contemporary reporting that quotes or identifies knowledgeable officials but does not disclose the complete documentary basis.

### Level E — Secondhand, anonymous, composite, or source-unclear account

Claims resting on hearsay, anonymous sourcing, collective attribution, a footnote that covers several sentences without specifying which source supports which proposition, or an unrecovered citation whose contents cannot be checked.

**Permitted use:** Normally contextual, a research lead, or clearly labeled disputed material. It should not carry a major thesis or establish a person’s participation in a covert or criminal act.

### Level F — Contradicted or disproven claim

A claim materially contradicted by stronger evidence, denied by the person allegedly involved without adequate countervailing support, chronologically impossible, or demonstrated to be a conflation.

**Permitted use:** Only to explain the dispute, correct the record, or show how a contested narrative developed. It must not be presented as fact.

### Corroboration modifiers

The level describes the **kind of source**, not the final truth value. Each claim also receives a modifier:

- **Independently corroborated** — a separate source supports the material proposition.
- **Partially corroborated** — a separate source supports the core setting, mechanism, identity, or some but not all operational details.
- **Uncorroborated** — no independent support has been located; this does not mean disproven.
- **Materially disputed** — credible evidence conflicts on an important point.
- **Contradicted** — stronger or more direct evidence rejects the claim.
- **Source chronology unresolved** — apparent corroboration may have been publicly available to the later author and therefore is not necessarily independent.

### C. Claims resting on investigative synthesis rather than a single disclosed witness

These include the full reconstructed roster, claims that the CIA “infiltrated” or “controlled” BNDD/DEA, the placement of particular men into a unified assassination or rendition capability, and narrative labels such as “torpedo.” Valentine may have assembled these propositions from multiple interviews, documents, and inference. Unless the exact chain is recoverable, they remain Level D or E and must be attributed to him rather than converted into fact.

### D. Claims with anonymous, collective, or unclear sourcing

- Patrick Gregory’s alleged program membership is not tied to a sufficiently clear, checkable source in the available notes.
- Assertions attributed to “persons knowledgeable about Seymour Bolten” are anonymous and cannot establish the proposition alone.
- Some collective citations to several DEA interviews do not reveal which witness supports which sentence.
- Names attached to DiGennaro’s alleged special unit—Joseph Salm, Paul Seema, Mike Campbell, and others—principally depend on DiGennaro or on a contested chain of participant recollection.

These remain leads, not a publishable roster.

### E. Claims sourced to documents rather than recollection

Valentine’s discussions of Anthony Triponi and the 1976–77 “de facto immunity” problem are materially different from purely oral accounts: he was using or quoting records that are now available in the Part 5 corpus. These should no longer be labeled “Valentine only.” They should be cited to the underlying documents.

### F. Secondary authors beyond Valentine

- **Peter Dale Scott:** useful for network identification and hypothesis formation. Any claim used in Part 5 requires an exact citation and an audit of whether Scott relies on a document, named witness, anonymous source, or another secondary author. No load-bearing proposition about TWO-FOLD should rest on an uncrossed Scott citation.
- **Thomas C. Tripodi, *Crusade*:** participant memoir. Level C for matters he directly experienced, Level D/E for other people’s roles or compartmented activities he did not directly observe.
- **Contemporary journalists, including Nicholas Horrock:** potentially strong Level D reporting, particularly when close in time and based on named officials or official records. Journalism does not become primary merely because it is contemporary.
- **Alfred McCoy and other scholarly histories:** useful for regional narcotics, intelligence, and police context. Their claims should be mapped to their own source bases and should not be used as substitute proof of TWO-FOLD membership.

The CIA released the Family Jewels collection publicly in June 2007, before Valentine’s January 2008 CounterPunch article and before the 2009 publication of *The Strength of the Pack*. Valentine may have conducted interviews earlier, but the dates are not disclosed in the notes reviewed here. Therefore, a match between his later publications and those official records is corroboration of substance, not necessarily proof that he discovered the fact before it was official.

### Recalibrated finding on “partial vindication”

Valentine was **partly vindicated as an investigator of institutional relationships**, especially where later-recovered or reverse-sourced records confirm a hidden CIA identity, an interagency training relationship, personnel migration, and the documentary “de facto immunity” conflict. But that conclusion cannot be generalized to every roster name or operational allegation.

The official record independently validates the architecture that made the testimony plausible:

- a CIA-approved recruitment project;
- a proprietary intermediary;
- internal-security placements;
- a separate commercially covered foreign-intelligence component;
- a documented order to terminate one component while preserving another;
- later conflicts between CIA secrecy and DEA prosecution.

It does **not** independently validate a CIA-directed kidnapping or assassination unit, every person Valentine assigns to TWO-FOLD, or a claim that CIA controlled DEA as an institution.

## 6. Operational claim review: kidnapping, assassination, “torpedoes,” and covert control

### 6.1 Kidnapping and rendition

**Source chain:** Joseph DiGennaro told Valentine that he participated in overseas seizures of traffickers, including a Colombian operation in which targets were drugged, handcuffed, and brought into the United States. Gerry Carey separately described indicted people being restrained and held at a safehouse, but did not connect his observation to DiGennaro, the CIA, or TWO-FOLD. Joe Quarequio’s recollection that DiGennaro was not present in the office supports only the fact of unusual absence.

**Official-record fit:** The CIA memorandum and Rockefeller Commission establish a foreign-facing, commercially covered component directed at international traffickers. That makes overseas activity structurally plausible. Those records do not describe abduction, irregular rendition, safehouse detention, or entry into the United States outside normal legal process.

**Conflicts:** The CIA memorandum states that the two agents were unaware of CIA involvement; DiGennaro described CIA clearance, CIA-connected supervision, and a CIA-funded structure. Rockefeller stated that BNDD directed the agents while CIA provided administrative and cover support. DiGennaro described substantially deeper CIA operational control.

**Status:** **DISPUTED NAMED FIRSTHAND; PARTIALLY CORROBORATED CONTEXT, UNCORROBORATED ACTS.** The article may state that DiGennaro alleged participating in an overseas abduction or irregular rendition, followed immediately by what official records do and do not establish. It may not state that TWO-FOLD conducted kidnappings as fact.

### 6.2 Assassination or authorization to kill

**Source chain:** DiGennaro told Valentine that the work involved kidnapping targets and killing them if they resisted. No recovered document authorizes assassination; no second named witness directly confirms such an order; no named victim, mission order, after-action report, prosecution record, or foreign record has been tied to the allegation.

**Credibility:** The statement is notable because it is specific and potentially self-incriminating, which can weigh in favor of candor. But its extraordinary nature, the lack of operational particulars, the retrospective interview setting, and the absence of independent corroboration prevent elevation to established fact.

**Status:** **DISPUTED NAMED FIRSTHAND / UNCORROBORATED EXTRAORDINARY CLAIM.** If used at all, it belongs in a tightly bounded section or sidebar about the limits of the record. It should not appear in the headline, standfirst, thesis, or as a narrative fact.

### 6.3 “Torpedoes”

Searches of Valentine’s text show “torpedo” functioning as his narrative label—such as “typical Twofold torpedo” or “apprentice torpedoes”—rather than a term established in a primary document or clearly quoted from a participant. “Shoofly,” by contrast, appears as period internal-investigation slang in participant accounts and records.

**Status:** **ATTRIBUTED SECONDARY TERMINOLOGY.** Avoid “torpedo” as if it were an official CIA or BNDD designation. If retained for color, introduce it explicitly as Valentine’s term and do not use it to smuggle in an allegation of violence.

### 6.4 CIA control of TWO-FOLD personnel

The official descriptions and some testimony conflict:

- The Rockefeller Commission said CIA relinquished control and contact after the 19 recruits entered BNDD.
- The CIA action memorandum confirms CIA created the mechanism but distinguishes the internal-security recruitment from the separately administered covered-agent component.
- Fuller, Tripodi, Triponi, DiGennaro, Ingersoll, and Brosan collectively describe or allege continuing cutouts, reporting channels, compartmentation, or CIA-linked administration of varying kinds.

The record therefore establishes **CIA-created access and capacity**, not a single proven degree of continuing control across all personnel. “CIA infiltration” may be used only as an attributed interpretation. “CIA takeover” or “CIA controlled DEA” is not supported.

### 6.5 Continuation after July 1973

Three different questions must not be conflated:

1. **Was the internal-security recruitment component formally ordered terminated?** Yes. The 1973 CIA memorandum recommended termination, and the Rockefeller Commission reported the program ended in July 1973.
2. **Was the foreign narcotics-intelligence component preserved?** Yes. The CIA memorandum expressly recommended continuing it only for collection abroad.
3. **Did personal reporting channels, residual funds, or administrative remnants persist after the formal date?** The evidence is mixed. Anthony Triponi’s contemporaneously recorded allegation and George Brosan’s later firsthand account point toward practical residue or delayed winding down. Neither proves that the original domestic project secretly continued intact.

**Status:** The formal termination and foreign continuation are **DOCUMENTED**. A domestic practical continuation into 1974 is **DISPUTED BUT EVIDENTIALLY GROUNDED**, not “Valentine only.”

### 6.6 BUNCIN and later covert programs

BUNCIN has its own documentary and testimonial record. Some personnel may have crossed programs, and Fuller described more than one agenda, including what he characterized as partisan political intelligence. That does not make BUNCIN a component of TWO-FOLD.

**Status:** **SIDEBAR / SEPARATE RABBIT HOLE.** Use only to show the surrounding proliferation of compartmented narcotics-intelligence programs or personnel migration. Do not use it to expand TWO-FOLD’s mission by association.

## 7. Charles Gutensohn case study

### What Valentine reports

In Valentine’s account, Charles “Chuck” Gutensohn said he:

- left the Army in 1964, completed a degree, and joined the CIA in 1968;
- served for roughly two years at Pakse, Laos;
- was offered either a return to Laos as CIA liaison to BNDD or entry into TWO-FOLD;
- knew that his brother Joel had entered BNDD roughly six months earlier;
- met recruiter Jerry Soul and Patrick Fuller, who used the names “Leo Adams” and “Walter DeCarlo”;
- discovered that practical cover was thin because colleagues understood Fuller’s role and saw Fuller’s signature on credentials.

The underlying note identifies an **interview with Chuck Gutensohn**, making this Level C testimony, not merely Valentine’s unattributed reconstruction.

### What can be independently confirmed

Official public records identify a Charles J. Gutensohn in increasingly senior DEA roles: Chief of Cocaine Investigations in a 1988 interagency conference roster, Acting Deputy Assistant Administrator for Operations in the 1991 Congressional Record index, and later Special Agent in Charge in a Justice Department publication. These records strongly confirm the witness’s institutional identity and senior DEA career.

They do not independently confirm CIA employment, Pakse service, TWO-FOLD recruitment, the meeting participants, or the code names.

### Evidentiary assessment

Gutensohn’s account has several credibility strengths:

- it concerns his own career and recruitment;
- it contains specific chronology, location, names, choices, and procedural details;
- his later DEA identity and seniority are independently verifiable;
- the described proprietary/recruitment structure fits the independently documented program architecture;
- the account includes an unflattering detail—the failure of cover—rather than functioning solely as self-promotion.

Its limitations are equally clear:

- no CIA personnel file, Laos assignment record, roster entry, proprietary record, or Fuller credential has been recovered;
- the interview date and transcript are not in the local corpus;
- some details may reflect retrospective understanding of a compartmented program.

### Article decision

Gutensohn should be restored as a **named, attributed case study**. He is particularly valuable because his account supplies the human scale absent from the official memoranda: how an alleged recruit understood the choice, who he says met him, and how quickly cover could become nominal.

Recommended formulation:

> Charles Gutensohn later told Douglas Valentine that, after CIA service in Laos, he was offered a choice between returning as a narcotics liaison or entering TWO-FOLD. Official records located for this investigation confirm Gutensohn’s later senior DEA career, but not his CIA service or placement on the still-unrecovered TWO-FOLD roster.

Do not write that Gutensohn’s Pakse service or TWO-FOLD membership is officially documented. Do not treat his Laos history as proof that OPS personnel were institutionally transferred into DEA; it is evidence of a human bridge between CIA paramilitary experience and narcotics enforcement.

## 8. Patrick Fuller case study

### Documentary position

The official record establishes the functions with which Fuller’s account is associated even where it does not name every intermediary:

- DCI approval on February 12, 1971;
- use of a proprietary recruitment mechanism;
- placement of CIA-recruited personnel into BNDD internal security;
- short CIA instruction in tradecraft according to Rockefeller;
- a separate covered foreign-intelligence component.

Contemporaneous 1974 inspection memoranda separately record Anthony Triponi’s claim that former chief inspector Patrick Fuller selected him during training for a direct covert reporting channel. The memos prove the allegation was made, not that it was true.

### Fuller’s firsthand account

Valentine quotes Fuller:

> “We recruited the CIA officers for BNDD through a proprietary company. A corporation engaged in law enforcement hired three CIA officers posing as private businessmen to do the contact and interview work.”

This is unusually important testimony because it matches the official program’s documented recruitment mechanism while adding operational texture: the purported number and cover posture of recruiters.

Valentine also attributes to Fuller the claims that:

- conventional records were sparse or absent;
- some recruits functioned through cutouts and may not have entered the office openly;
- the intended overseas purpose did not describe every actual placement;
- Fuller did not know all participants;
- another operation existed that even he did not know about.

### Evidentiary assessment by proposition

| Proposition | Assessment |
|---|---|
| Fuller participated in or knew the proprietary recruitment mechanism | **ATTRIBUTED FIRSTHAND, STRONGLY CORROBORATED** by official descriptions of the mechanism |
| Three CIA officers posed as private businessmen to contact/interview candidates | **ATTRIBUTED FIRSTHAND**; plausible within the official mechanism, but precise number and identities remain uncorroborated |
| Records were deliberately absent or minimized | **ATTRIBUTED FIRSTHAND, UNCORROBORATED**; the incomplete archive is consistent but not proof |
| Fuller did not know all recruits or compartments | **ATTRIBUTED FIRSTHAND**; structurally plausible, not independently provable |
| A distinct operation existed outside Fuller’s knowledge | Evidence only that Fuller made this statement; it is a lead, not a description of the unknown operation |
| Fuller maintained a domestic covert reporting channel into 1974 | **DOCUMENTED ALLEGATION** in contemporaneous inspection records; underlying arrangement remains disputed |

### Credibility and motive

Fuller was close enough to know recruitment mechanics and vulnerable enough to have motives both to reveal and to minimize. He may accurately describe the machinery while distancing himself from later operational conduct or compartments. That mixed incentive argues for claim-by-claim assessment rather than a global verdict that he was either truthful or unreliable.

### Article decision

Fuller should become the principal named witness for **how the recruitment machinery worked**, placed directly beside the CIA and Rockefeller descriptions. His testimony should not be made responsible for proving the alleged operational unit, every recruit, or post-1973 continuity.

The archive trail should remain open. Valentine donated a nine-box federal drug-enforcement collection to the National Security Archive, reportedly including interview notes and correspondence. Recovery of Fuller interview notes, dates, recordings, correspondence, or referenced proprietary details could materially sharpen this case study before publication.

## 11. Impact on the independent thesis

### Previous thesis direction

The first synthesis argued that TWO-FOLD reveals two parallel CIA-accessed police systems—AID/OPS abroad and federal narcotics enforcement—and that, as OPS was dismantled and domestic TWO-FOLD formally ended, selected functions were preserved or migrated through narcotics institutions. It rejected both wholesale institutional succession and a CIA “takeover” of DEA, then traced the later consequence in DEA’s own de facto-immunity warnings.

### Recalibrated thesis

> TWO-FOLD was not merely a liaison agreement. Primary records show that CIA created covert personnel capacity inside BNDD while simultaneously supporting a foreign, commercially covered narcotics-intelligence component. Named participants describe how that capacity was recruited, concealed, and—more uncertainly—used. The domestic project was formally terminated in 1973, but the foreign component was expressly preserved, personnel and intelligence relationships expanded through separate programs, and evidence of a messier practical winding down reaches into 1974. By 1976–77, DEA’s own leaders warned that CIA secrecy could obstruct prosecutions and confer practical immunity on major targets.

### What changes

- The article can now show **people inside the machinery**, not only institutional memoranda.
- “Ended in 1973” becomes a precise claim about formal termination, not an unsupported assertion that every contact, fund, or channel instantly vanished.
- The second covered-agent component moves closer to the center because both official records and named testimony establish its importance, even though its operations remain contested.
- Valentine is treated as neither authority nor contaminant. His interviews are disaggregated and tested claim by claim.
- The article can acknowledge a serious allegation of irregular rendition and lethal authority without adopting it as fact.

### What does not change

- The seven later transferees cannot be merged with the 19-person roster.
- Later CIA–DEA programs do not prove that TWO-FOLD secretly survived unchanged.
- OPS and BNDD/DEA were parallel or intersecting access systems, not one demonstrated institutional handoff.
- The strongest ending remains the documentary 1976–77 conflict between intelligence secrecy and criminal prosecution.
- “CIA takeover,” “assassination unit,” and a complete named roster remain beyond the evidence.

The thesis is therefore **strengthened, not replaced**. Testimony supplies mechanism and stakes; it does not authorize a more sensational institutional claim.

## H2 2 — Two parallel doors into policing

**Revision:** Preserve the comparison between overseas police assistance and narcotics enforcement, but avoid claiming a direct organizational succession. Gutensohn’s alleged Pakse-to-BNDD path may appear briefly as an attributed human bridge, explicitly distinguished from proof of an OPS-to-DEA transfer.

## H2 3 — What TWO-FOLD built

**Lead evidence:** CIA memo, Rockefeller, Fuller, Gutensohn, Burke, Ingersoll.

Suggested H3 sequence:

### H3 — The proprietary and the 19

Pair the official mechanism with Fuller’s direct description of three covered interviewers. This is the strongest integration of document and testimony.

### H3 — What recruitment looked like to the recruits

Use Gutensohn as the principal case study and Burke as a contrasting account. Keep every unverified biographical or recruitment detail attributed.

### H3 — The second component

Establish the two covered agents from official sources. Use Ingersoll for purpose and compartmentation. Introduce DiGennaro only as a disputed possible window into what the official account omits.

## H2 4 — A handoff without a merger

**Lead evidence:** formal termination, foreign continuation, Thomas Tripodi’s documented career, later NIO transfers, Triponi memoranda, Brosan testimony.

**Revision:** Distinguish four forms of continuity:

1. expressly authorized foreign continuation;
2. personnel migration through separate mechanisms;
3. institutional knowledge and training;
4. alleged residual domestic channels or delayed wind-down.

This section should carry the strongest correction to the first pass: the practical end date is not as clean as the official date, but the evidence still does not prove covert continuation of the intact project.

## H2 5 — The narcotics exception as a bridge

**Lead evidence:** expansion of overseas narcotics intelligence, documented training, foreign liaison, and separate programs.

**Revision:** Use participant testimony to show why narcotics work was unusually compatible with intelligence methods: overseas targets, cover, informants, liaison services, and restricted files. Do not import BUNCIN’s political-intelligence mission into TWO-FOLD. If BUNCIN appears, confine it to a labeled sidebar showing the crowded ecosystem of compartmented programs.

## H2 6 — When intelligence made prosecution impossible

**Lead evidence:** 1976–77 DEA memoranda.

**Revision:** Retain as the documentary culmination. The point is not that the disputed kidnapping account has been proved; it is that the institutional relationship produced an independently documented legal consequence. Intelligence equities could force DEA to abandon or cripple cases, creating what its officials called practical or de facto immunity.

### Recommended placement of disputed operational testimony

The DiGennaro material should occupy no more than one bounded H3 or sidebar within H2 3 or H2 4. Its structure should be:

1. what DiGennaro said;
2. what official records independently establish;
3. what other named witnesses observed;
4. where the accounts conflict;
5. what cannot presently be concluded.

It should not become the article’s narrative engine. The documentary institutional story is stronger and more consequential.

### 13.1 Attribute at the point of claim

Do not place attribution several sentences after an allegation. Write “DiGennaro told Valentine…” before describing the alleged act. The reader should never need to reverse-engineer whether a sentence comes from a memo, witness, or authorial inference.

### 13.2 Separate the fact of a statement from the truth of the statement

For Anthony Triponi, the primary record proves that he told inspectors about a covert Fuller channel. It does not independently prove that Fuller created the channel. Use formulations such as:

> A 1974 inspection memorandum records Triponi alleging…

not:

> A 1974 memorandum proves Fuller operated…

### 13.3 Identify personal knowledge

Use a witness for what the witness did, saw, received, or was told directly. Do not silently convert a participant’s speculation about other compartments into personal knowledge.

### 13.4 State corroboration and limits together

When a testimony passage is consequential, give the reader both sides in the same paragraph:

> Official records confirm that TWO-FOLD included two commercially covered agents targeting international traffickers. They do not identify DiGennaro or corroborate the abductions he described.

Do not postpone the limitation to a distant footnote.

### 13.5 Do not use “unverified” as a synonym for “false”

Prefer precise language:

- “no independent corroboration has been located”;
- “the recovered roster does not identify him because no roster has been recovered”;
- “the official account conflicts with his description of control”;
- “the allegation remains unresolved.”

### 13.6 Use stronger verbs only for stronger evidence

- **Records show / document / establish** — Level A or strong B.
- **The commission found / CIA reported / DEA warned** — official retrospective or institutional position.
- **X said / recalled / told Valentine / alleged** — Level C.
- **Valentine argues / reconstructs / identifies** — Level D.
- **An anonymous source claimed** — Level E, usually omit.
- **Evidence contradicts** — Level F or a clearly stated conflict.

Avoid “revealed,” “proved,” or “confirmed” for a single retrospective interview.

### 13.7 Make source chronology explicit

Do not call an official document “later corroboration” unless its public release postdates the author’s claim or there is evidence the author did not have it. Rockefeller’s report was public in 1975, and the CIA publicly released the Family Jewels in 2007. Valentine’s 2008 article and 2009 book may agree with them without having predicted them.

### 13.8 Treat motive as a modifier, not a verdict

Fuller may minimize responsibility; Burke may emphasize that he did not spy; Ingersoll may defend legality; DiGennaro may exaggerate clandestine importance; Tripodi may magnify his role. These are reasons for scrutiny, not automatic disqualification.

### 13.9 Extraordinary claims require visible evidentiary friction

Kidnapping, assassination, covert detention, or hidden funding allegations require:

1. named attribution;
2. the witness’s proximity;
3. available corroboration;
4. contrary official evidence;
5. a plain statement of what remains unproved.

If the prose cannot carry all five without derailing the section, move the claim to a sidebar or omit it.

### 13.10 Do not construct a composite witness

Carey’s safehouse observation, Quarequio’s office observation, Ingersoll’s program description, and DiGennaro’s operational allegations cannot be fused into one collectively “confirmed” account. Each witness saw or claimed something different.

### 13.11 Preserve distinctions among programs and people

- TWO-FOLD is not BUNCIN.
- The 19 recruits are not established as the seven later transferees.
- The internal-security component is not identical to the two-agent foreign component.
- Thomas C. Tripodi is not Anthony Triponi.
- Formal termination is not identical to instantaneous practical disappearance.
- A human career bridge is not proof of institutional succession.

### 13.12 Quote sparingly and verifiably

Use short quotations only where the witness’s exact language materially matters. Confirm the quotation against the local source. Paraphrase the rest. Never modernize uncertainty out of the witness’s words.

### 13.13 Cite the underlying record whenever recovered

If Valentine quotes a DEA memorandum that is now in the corpus, cite the memorandum, not Valentine. Valentine may still be cited for locating it or for his interpretation. The same applies to the Triponi inspection memoranda and official personnel records.

### 13.14 Use “documented,” “attributed,” and “disputed” consistently

These are editorial signals, not rhetorical hedges:

- **Documented:** the claim is supported by a recoverable record.
- **Attributed:** a named source with relevant proximity made the claim.
- **Disputed:** meaningful evidence conflicts or an extraordinary claim lacks sufficient corroboration.

### 13.15 Keep the thesis independent of the most fragile claim

If every kidnapping, assassination, and roster allegation were removed, the article’s central argument would still stand on primary records: CIA approved and built covert capacity inside BNDD; it preserved a foreign component; broader personnel and intelligence ties continued through separate mechanisms; and DEA later documented the prosecution-threatening consequence. Testimony should deepen that argument, not become the single pillar holding it up.
