# Part 7 Book Leads

## Lindsey A. O'Rourke — *Covert Regime Change*

- PDF pp. 8–10 (file pp. 24–26): covert action lowers expected military, economic, and reputational costs through plausible deniability, but secrecy constrains scale. Attempted covert regime changes failed more than 60 percent of the time; only 39 percent replaced the target during the Cold War.
- PDF p. 108 (file p. 124): Kennan-era planning proposed government direction and money routed through private American organizations to foreign resistance networks. Footnote trail points to NSC directives and archival policy papers.
- Chapters 3–4: successful operations generally required time, intelligence access, and viable domestic partners; later exposure and blowback were common.
- Part 7 use: predicts local-partner dependence, intermediary finance, role separation, and failure—not omnipotent hidden control.
- Rabbit holes opened: NSC private-intermediary directive; comparative election-intervention cases; Syria as a large-operation/secrecy tradeoff.

## Dov H. Levin — *Meddling in the Ballot Box*

- Chapter 3: process-tracing expectations include an intervener who sees a local actor as unacceptable, a willing and politically vulnerable domestic partner, and close cooperation over specific acts. Initiative may come from the local partner.
- Chapter 6 / dataset discussion: intervention is not rare; 43 percent of interventions in the dataset were repeat interventions. Overt and covert efforts have distinguishable effects and disclosure patterns.
- Venezuela 1958 and West Germany 1953 cases warn against coding all influence as one mechanism; election intervention requires acts intended to affect the vote, not merely post-election pressure.
- Part 7 use: supplies a stricter event-level test for Ukraine and Bangladesh. Both show environment-building; neither yields the predicted operational bridge to the uprising.
- Rabbit holes opened: CEPPS partner communications; embassy-party cooperation; participant requests or invitations; repeat-country intervention patterns.

## Hugh Wilford — *The Mighty Wurlitzer*

- PDF pp. 19–20: the CIA subsidized the National Student Association first through wealthy people posing as donors and later through fake charitable pass-throughs. A small witting circle concealed funding from unwitting participants.
- Introduction, PDF pp. 20–22: front groups were not simply lifeless puppets. Their genuine associational energy and internal autonomy helped make them useful; some participants resisted CIA direction.
- Chapters on students, labor, women, religion, culture, and aid show a portfolio of purpose-built and captured relationships rather than one organizational template.
- Part 7 use: the crucial historical precedent. Independence and strategic utility are not opposites. The modern test therefore asks about money, metrics, tasking, and consequences—not only formal editorial control.
- Rabbit holes opened: pass-through foundations; witting/unwitting governance; student and labor networks; genuine autonomy as an operational asset.

## Frances Stonor Saunders — *The Cultural Cold War*

- Chapter “A Bad Bargain”: the Congress for Cultural Freedom could exercise meaningful intellectual independence while secret subsidy still served U.S. strategic purposes and compromised democratic legitimacy.
- Same chapter: after the 1967 exposure, Ford Foundation financing changed the formal patronage structure, but participants described continuing inter-service interest and conflict.
- Part 7 use: helps frame OCCRP without the false binary “independent or a tool.” The modern record proves strategic funding and downstream-effect metrics; it does not prove covert editorial assignment.
- Rabbit holes opened: foundation succession, donor firewalls, named managers, post-exposure institutional migration.

## William I. Robinson — *Global Capitalism and the Crisis of Humanity*

- Chapters 1–2: transnational power operates through states, corporations, policy boards, foundations, civic associations, media, academia, and international financial institutions.
- Chapter 5: war, security, surveillance, and social control are increasingly privatized; state direction and private implementation can coexist.
- Part 7 use: an interpretive framework for network power and contractorization, not direct proof of any case.
- Rabbit holes opened: contractor markets, IFI conditionality, foundation-state linkages, transnational elite circulation.

## William I. Robinson — *Global Civil War*

- Chapters 2–4: crises generate coercive, surveillance, and privatized security responses across public/private boundaries.
- Country discussions emphasize domestic movements and material grievances, a useful antidote to theories that convert every uprising into foreign engineering.
- Part 7 use: strengthens both network analysis and the control-case discipline.

## William I. Robinson — *Promoting Polyarchy*

- Supplied article/book-derived corpus: U.S. democracy promotion is interpreted as support for elite-managed political systems compatible with transnational economic order.
- Part 7 use: informs the maximum theory and the Venezuela/Ukraine questions. Because the supplied item is secondary argument, country-specific program records carry the factual load.

## Earlier-series books mined

- Alfred W. McCoy, *The Politics of Heroin*: opens the narcotics/intelligence/law-enforcement relationship trail later anchored in declassified CIA–DEA records.
- Peter Dale Scott, *American War Machine*: generates network-state and covert-finance leads; claims retained as attributed analysis unless independently documented.
- Jeremy Kuzmarov, *The Quiet Violence of Empire*: identifies police-assistance continuity and Afghanistan counterinsurgency leads; official records are used for program facts.
- Martha K. Huggins and political-policing scholarship: comparative police-assistance patterns; treated as secondary synthesis.
- Douglas Valentine / John Prados leads: personnel and operational claims sent to primary-source escalation; not all were recovered.

## Book-driven conclusion

The books did not validate a universal CIA command system. They produced a more diagnostic claim: state strategy can be executed through institutions with real autonomy, and the resulting system is often most visible in money routes, role allocation, partner selection, metrics, and downstream effects. That claim survived modern testing.
