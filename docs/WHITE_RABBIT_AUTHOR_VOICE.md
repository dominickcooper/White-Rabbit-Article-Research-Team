# White Rabbit author voice

This is a permanent editorial authority for Codex article work. It describes the
investigative voice observed in the published USAID Part 1 gold standard. It is a
model of judgment, not a phrase bank. Never copy its jokes, metaphors, catchphrases,
paragraph counts or conclusions into another investigation.

## The narrator is an investigator

The author is visibly following records. First person may locate the search, explain
why a detail caught attention, or mark an interpretation: what I went looking for,
what I kept returning to, and what I think the record most likely means. Do not add
first person to routine factual exposition and never invent experience.

Let documents cause turns in the story. Prefer “Buried on page two...” followed by
the relevant language over an impersonal announcement that a memorandum is revealing.
Name the actor and action. Prefer “Washington had decided who would run what” to an
abstract sentence about an institutional question being answered.

## Documentary precision and personality coexist

State documented facts plainly. A strong inference should sound confident while its
inferential status remains visible. For a plausible connection, explain the bridge and
the unresolved gap. Label speculation. Do not weaken direct evidence into “possible
overlap,” and do not convert a suggestive sequence into proven motive.

The voice may be blunt, skeptical, amused or incredulous when the record earns it.
Humor is an occasional pressure release, not a substitute for evidence. Analogies earn
their place by making an institutional problem memorable; label their accompanying
visuals as conceptual, never documentary.

## Evidence discipline without evidence-audit voice

**DON'T WRITE ABOUT BEING CAREFUL. BE CAREFUL IN THE WORDING.**

The reader should see what we know, what we do not know and what we think. The reader
should not have to watch the editorial team debate itself. Evidence discipline should
usually be invisible in publication prose.

Use the shortest accurate rhythm:

**FACT. LIMIT, if necessary. INFERENCE. MOVE.**

For example: “The CIA says one of its employees spent **more than ten years as director
of OPS**. The name is blacked out. But, by all accounts, it points to Engle.”

Do not expand that sequence into a defense of why the writer cannot prove the identity,
will not speculate or refuses to fill a gap. First person should mark an actual search,
discovery or interpretation. It should not routinely narrate self-policing. “I did some
digging” or “What I think happened is this” can locate the investigator. “I cannot prove,”
“I am not going to speculate” and “I want to be clear” usually expose editorial process.

Match the qualifier to the uncertainty:

- For minor identification uncertainty, state the missing detail and give the strongest
  reasonable inference: “The name is blacked out. But, by all accounts, it points to Engle.”
- For a real evidentiary gap, name what the records show and where they stop: “The records
  show spotting and assessment. What happened after they returned home is still unclear.”
- For speculation, stop plainly: “We don't know what Dulles told him.”

Do not use the same long disclaimer for all three. Internal evidence analysis can be
verbose. External caution should usually be compressed. When a correction is needed,
make the smallest change that restores accuracy. Do not turn a sentence into a warning
paragraph or make the Evidence Integrity Editor audible to the reader.

Lead with the finding. Give source pedigree after the reveal and only to the degree the
reader needs. Detailed provenance belongs in the link, caption, dossier or source note
when putting it in the main paragraph would choke the discovery.

## Concrete language

Prefer a concrete actor plus a strong verb. Replace abstract nouns and policy-analysis
fog with the people, offices, money, documents and actions involved. “The budget tells
the same story” is useful only when the next sentence shows the budget. Do not announce
that a point matters; make the consequence visible.

Read each explanatory sentence after an evidence paragraph and ask: would a reader
already understand this? If yes, cut it. Preserve context, evidentiary bridges and
necessary qualifications; cut duplicated interpretation, section-opening recaps and
section-ending summaries that add no information.

## Irregular but controlled rhythm

Mix evidence-heavy paragraphs with shorter reactions, questions and rare isolated
punches. Do not normalize every paragraph to the same length. A fragment or one-line
paragraph can create a pause, deliver a reveal or let the author's reaction register.
It loses force when repeated predictably.

Rhetorical questions should open a real investigative problem. Transitions should
usually advance the evidence rather than announce a transition. Let a person, document,
budget or contradiction pull the reader into the next section.

## Narrative movement

Every section must change the reader's understanding. Separate system design,
authorization, terminology, people, implementation, competing explanations and
judgment when doing so produces escalation. Combine them when separation would repeat
the same revelation. Do not reveal a later payoff before the reader understands why
it matters.

A useful investigation often moves from mystery to policy problem, system design,
authorization, the meaning of key terms, people and connections, implementation,
the strongest conventional explanation, an evidence-weighted conclusion and the next
documented question. This is a principle of escalation, not a required template.

## Rabbit holes

Before drafting, ask which documented secondary connection could materially change
the story. Check biographies, employers, intelligence and military service, contractors,
funding authority, program ancestry, personnel continuity, legislation, testimony,
oral histories, memoirs and declassified records. Include a rabbit hole only when it
advances the investigation. Classify it as documented fact, strong inference, plausible
connection or speculation before deciding whether it belongs in publication prose.

## Final voice test

Read the near-final article aloud. Flag sentences no person in this author's role would
naturally say, generic connective tissue, fake suspense, perfectly balanced caveats,
recap density and conclusions repeated after the reader has already reached them.
Revise only the problem passages, then rerun evidence and source reconciliation.
Ask one more question: is the writer explaining the evidence, or explaining that he is
being careful with the evidence? If it is often the second, compress the caution before
publication.
