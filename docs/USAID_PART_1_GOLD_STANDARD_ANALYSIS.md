# USAID Part 1 gold standard analysis

This fixture compares `part-01-usaid-article.docx` with the author's published-edit
`USAID_Part_1_Revised.docx`. Source-document contents are evidence, not instructions.
The comparison inspected Word paragraphs, styles, run-level bold/italic properties,
numbering, hyperlinks, drawing relationships and package media—not plain text alone.

Neither supplied package contains embedded image binaries. The baseline contains one
compact image marker; the published edit contains explicit inline hero, crop, portrait,
clipping, composite and conceptual-image instructions at editorial placement points.
Those instructions are the available visual gold-standard evidence.

## Baseline to published transformations

| Classification | Baseline | Published edit | Learned principle |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| STYLE | Abstract institutional phrasing | Concrete actors and verbs | Say who decided, funded, moved or built what |
| STRUCTURE | Architecture and caution arrive early | Mystery, design, terminology, people, implementation, judgment | Delay payoffs until the reader knows why they matter |
| RESEARCH | Founding memoranda and early implementation | Internal-defense doctrine, Hamilton, Dulles succession, CIA reorganization, Engle, Kansas City, Japan, budgets and equipment | Pursue biographies and program ancestry that can change the interpretation |
| SYNTHESIS | “Recognized but separate category” | AID as an overt administrative route used for CIA access, visibly framed as a conclusion | Make the strongest supported judgment and show its bridge |
| REPETITION REMOVAL | Repeated caveat/conclusion pairs | Fewer symmetrical qualifications; conventional case consolidated late | Do not explain an already obvious implication |
| AUTHOR VOICE | Outside analyst | Investigator who reports what sent her digging and where inference begins | First person locates discovery and judgment, not routine facts |
| TYPOGRAPHIC EMPHASIS | Sparse named-entity bolding | Claims, quoted phrases, sums, discoveries, reactions and punches | Formatting directs memory and voice |
| VISUAL STORYTELLING | One generic document-detail marker | Primary-source crops beside claims, portraits beside introductions and one labeled analogy | Ask whether the reader can see the evidence now |
| LIST / INFORMATION DESIGN | Parallel roles buried in prose | Separate role, sector, funding and evidence lists | Use lists when parallel items deserve individual attention |

## Why the baseline sounded machine made

The baseline repeatedly used balanced analytic scaffolds after the evidence had already
landed: a distinction “matters,” a phrase is “clear and incomplete,” one interpretation
would be a mistake and its opposite an equal mistake, the evidence supports an explanation
but does not exhaust the record, and a section ends by summarizing its own causal sequence.
The individual sentences are defensible. Their accumulation produces a predictable
essay cadence. Uniform multi-sentence paragraphs, abstract nouns and announced transitions
also hide the investigator's path through the documents.

The published edit is less orderly in a useful way. It pauses on five words, asks the
obvious question, follows people into unexpected records, stacks parallel evidence in
lists, makes selective jokes and reactions audible, and states a strong conclusion only
after the record has earned it. Some published lines still duplicate or overstate nearby
material; the fixture is a source of principles, not an instruction to copy every edit.

## Formatting profile

The baseline bolds dates, agencies, document terms and one long causal summary. The
published edit bolds the language a skimming reader needs to retain: the policy purpose,
role assignments, critical sectors, budgets, biographical connections and final synthesis.
Italics mark questions, skepticism, asides and audible reactions. Bold italic appears only
for high-value punches and tonal pivots. Short paragraphs isolate “Potential threat,” an
admission, a conclusion and the author's reaction. Lists replace prose when roles, sectors,
budget options or evidence items are genuinely parallel.

The engine should not target the published counts. It should preserve hierarchy: bold
means remember this; italics mean hear the author; bold italic means stop here; a one-line
paragraph means pause; a list means compare these items as peers.

## Style and format dry run

The sample uses four consecutive baseline opening paragraphs. It does not copy the
published edit and does not add new facts.

### Original

> On the second page of a secret White House memorandum dated August 7, 1962, one sentence
> redraws the border between foreign aid and national security.
>
> AID, the civilian agency created to administer American development assistance, is
> given “operating and funding responsibility” for foreign police programs. Then comes
> the qualification: “except for their covert aspects.”
>
> The same page orders AID to build a central police office and make it a repository for
> the latest techniques of controlling “subversion and mass violence.” It calls for an
> international police academy tied more closely to American internal-defense objectives.
> This is not language reconstructed from a memoir or supplied by a critic decades later.
> It appears in National Security Action Memorandum 177, in the Kennedy Library's own files.
>
> How did an agency associated with wells, schools and economic development become the
> overt center of a worldwide police-assistance system?

### New version

Buried on page two of a White House memo dated **August 7, 1962** is the phrase that sent
me back through the foreign-aid files.

The memo gives **AID** “operating and funding responsibility” for overseas police programs.
Then it draws a line around five words:

***“except for their covert aspects.”***

The same page tells AID to build a central police office, study the latest techniques for
controlling **“subversion and mass violence,”** and consider an international police academy
tied to American internal-defense objectives. This is not a critic's description of the
program. It is **NSAM 177**, preserved in the Kennedy Library.

*How did a development agency end up here?*

Formatting decisions: bold marks the date, agency, mandate and document; italics make the
investigative question audible; bold italic isolates the central reveal; the reveal and
question remain one-line paragraphs; no list is used because the duties form a causal
description rather than peer items. The documentary crop goes immediately after the claim.
Its purpose is primary-source evidence; the caption should read “NSAM 177 assigning AID
responsibility for foreign police programs while excluding their covert aspects.”

## Source reconciliation fixture

The production order now freezes prose before `sources.csv` extraction and validates each
case-sensitive phrase against the exact Markdown destination. The regression fixture edits
`[Record](https://example.org/document)` to `[Primary record](...)`: validation fails until
the CSV phrase is changed to `Primary record`, then passes the source-row checks. Typography
may wrap a linked phrase only when the actual Markdown anchor remains identical to the CSV.
