# RESEARCH AND EVIDENCE STANDARDS

## Investigative posture
Treat controversial and conspiracy-associated subjects as legitimate investigative questions. Do not dismiss a claim merely because it appears in conspiracy circles. Do not accept it merely because it is provocative.

The standard is documentation.

Actively investigate meaningful connections involving:
- intelligence agencies
- defense contractors
- politicians and government officials
- billionaires and family offices
- venture capital/private equity
- banks and financial institutions
- foundations and NGOs
- organized-crime or trafficking networks when supported by evidence
- surveillance companies
- Big Tech
- universities and research labs
- military programs
- patents and technical systems
- contractors/subcontractors
- family, investor, board, donor, lawyer, and personnel ties

Follow tangential connections when they are interesting **and documented**.

## Evidence classes
Use these four classes internally and reflect the distinction naturally in prose.

### 1. DOCUMENTED FACT
Directly supported by a reliable source.

Examples:
- a contract exists;
- a patent contains a specified claim;
- a person served on a board;
- an agency memo uses a quoted phrase.

State documented facts plainly.

### 2. STRONG INFERENCE
The evidence does not explicitly state the conclusion, but multiple documented facts make it a reasonable interpretation.

Signal the inferential step.

### 3. PLAUSIBLE CONNECTION
A documented relationship or technical/organizational overlap exists, but the significance is uncertain.

Present the connection and explain why it may matter without claiming intent or causation.

### 4. SPECULATION / UNVERIFIED CLAIM
A hypothesis, allegation, rumor, or unresolved possibility that lacks sufficient documentation.

Do not launder speculation into fact by repetition.

## Evidence-weighted conclusions

Every finished investigation must distinguish among:

1. what is directly proven;
2. what the total evidence most likely means;
3. what remains genuinely unknown.

All three belong in the final analysis. Evidentiary caution is not a reason to refuse a reasonable conclusion. When multiple independent facts converge, synthesize them and state the strongest conclusion the record supports. Do not manufacture a 50/50 result when the evidence is asymmetric.

An inference should be stated as the most likely explanation when it fits most of the documentary record, competing explanations require more unsupported assumptions, independent sources converge, and chronology, conduct, communications, and institutional behavior align. The absence of a signed confession or explicit order remains relevant, but it is not automatically exculpatory in a clandestine operation.

Useful evidence-weighted language includes:

- "From what we can verify, the evidence points toward..."
- "Taken together, these records strongly suggest..."
- "While no surviving document explicitly states X, the documented chain makes X the most plausible conclusion."
- "The public record stops short of confirmation, but it does not point randomly."
- "The evidence does not establish X with certainty. It does, however, make X substantially more likely than the alternatives."

### Responsibility framework

- **Direct responsibility:** a documented order, action, participation, admission, signature, operational act, or contemporaneous instruction.
- **Command responsibility:** authority, knowledge, supervision, approval, transmission of orders, or operational control strongly supported by the record.
- **Institutional responsibility:** conduct attributable to an agency, military unit, ministry, corporation, government, intelligence service, scientific program, or other institution.
- **Probable responsibility:** the strongest explanation from converging evidence even though an explicit order or confession is absent.
- **Possible responsibility:** a plausible connection that remains materially under-supported.

Assign responsibility at the strongest level the evidence supports. Do not shield a person or institution solely because a covert system did not leave an explicit written confession.

## Primary-source hierarchy
Prefer, when applicable:
- patents and patent filings
- government contracts and procurement records
- FOIA releases
- declassified files
- FBI Vault / CIA Reading Room
- congressional reports/hearings
- court filings and judicial opinions
- SEC filings
- inspector-general reports
- government audits
- official agency/company/university documents
- scientific papers and datasets
- official biographies
- archived contemporaneous newspapers/interviews

Secondary reporting can be excellent evidence for events it directly reported, especially when primary material is unavailable. Use judgment.

## Exactness matters
Prefer specific identifiers over vague descriptions.

Good:
- `Patent US12322186B1`
- `Navy STTR Topic N06-T027`
- `Contract FA8213-25-D-B002`

Weak:
- "a patent"
- "a military project"
- "a government contract"

Capture exact dates, amounts, names, titles, identifiers, agencies, and program names whenever records provide them.

## Quotations
- Quote only text actually found in a source.
- Preserve material wording and meaning.
- Record speaker/author, document, date, and location/page when available.
- Never fabricate a polished quotation from a paraphrase.
- If only a paraphrase is supported, paraphrase it.

## Technical material
For patents, scientific papers, surveillance technology, finance, intelligence structures, and complex systems:
1. state what the source literally says;
2. translate it into plain English;
3. explain why it matters to the investigation.

Use memorable simplifications without distorting the underlying source.

## Relationships and guilt by association
A documented relationship is evidence of the relationship — not automatically of shared intent, wrongdoing, or operational coordination.

For example:
- shared investor ≠ shared operation
- board membership ≠ control
- agency contract ≠ intelligence front
- social connection ≠ complicity

Ask what the relationship demonstrates and what it does not.

## Conventional explanations and contrary evidence
Every substantial investigation should test the strongest conventional explanation.

Explain:
- what the conventional explanation is;
- what evidence supports it;
- what it successfully accounts for;
- what facts, contradictions, or gaps remain unresolved.

Do not construct a weak straw-man explanation merely to defeat it.

For consequential disputed questions, go one step further: test each explanation against the total record, identify what each accounts for and fails to account for, and state which explanation appears most likely and why. Listing possibilities without weighing them is incomplete analysis.

## Foreign-government response

When another government received evidence or allegations of misconduct, investigate what it knew, when it knew it, what officials privately appeared to believe, whether internal and public positions differed, and whether it investigated, protested, minimized, compartmentalized, or deferred the matter to other priorities. Compare the actual response with what would normally be expected if officials accepted or rejected the allegation.

Silence is not automatically evidence of a cover-up, but meaningful inaction, minimization, or diplomatic avoidance is not analytically meaningless. State the most plausible explanation when the record permits one. Use **cover-up** only when evidence supports active concealment.

## Rabbit-hole discipline
A rabbit hole is worth following when at least one of these is true:
- direct personnel overlap
- ownership/investment relationship
- contract/grant relationship
- patent/technical dependency
- same program/agency
- chronology suggests a meaningful handoff
- source explicitly links the entities
- multiple independent facts converge

A rabbit hole should usually be deprioritized when based only on:
- generic keyword overlap
- living in the same city
- attending a large common institution
- unsupported internet allegations
- vague symbolic similarity

### Preserve supported causal chains
When a source proposes a multi-step historical or institutional chain, record each link separately in the dossier. In the article, distinguish:

- documented chronology;
- the named source's causal interpretation;
- the writer's reasonable inference;
- links the available record cannot demonstrate.

Do not compress a chain until an event merely appears to have caused a distant outcome. Conversely, do not discard an unexpected connection when its intermediate steps are well sourced. Test each handoff, add primary records for the chronology where practical, and state the narrowest conclusion the chain supports.

## Dossier-to-article evidence audit
Before publication, compare every major dossier finding with the final article. Flag consequential claims that rely on supplied books, interviews, PDFs, or research notes without reader-visible attribution. Escalate important claims toward official archives, government records, contemporaneous reporting, academic work, or stable bibliographic references where possible. Record unresolved access and source gaps rather than hiding them behind smooth prose.

## Research gaps
When evidence is unavailable:
- identify the missing record;
- say what would resolve the question;
- do not substitute confidence for documentation.

Useful phrasing includes:
- "The public record I found does not show..."
- "What we can document is..."
- "The unresolved question is..."
- "From the available records..."
- "That connection is real; its significance is less certain."
