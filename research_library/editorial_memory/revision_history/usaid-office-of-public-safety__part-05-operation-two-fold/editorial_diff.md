## SIGNIFICANT ADDED BLOCKS

### 1

> The staggered close also explains why apparently contradictory dates circulate. An adviser could leave before a commodity already ordered arrived. A country project could close while Washington completed an evaluation. A narcotics adviser could remain in the same country under a different, expressly authorized mission. None of those facts means OPS remained open; none permits the historian to treat every activity after December 1973 as cleanly severed from earlier commitments. Authorization, obligation, delivery and personnel departure are four different administrative events.

### 2

> First, the prohibition did not apply to assistance under section 515(c) of the Omnibus Crime Control and Safe Streets Act. Second, it preserved DEA and FBI authority concerning crimes of a nature unlawful under American law. Third, it exempted narcotics-control assistance authorized by section 482 of the Foreign Assistance Act. Fourth, it allowed pre-enactment contracts supplying program personnel to run, while expressly applying the ban to renewals or extensions made after enactment.

### 3

> Defense was harder. The military-assistance program provided support to military units performing law-enforcement functions and, before the cutoff, to civilian police as part of internal-security policy. Defense removed identified ineligible recipients. Yet GAO found four ways indirect benefit could continue: shared facilities, trained military personnel later rotated into police work, dual-role units not identified as such, and armed forces performing civilian policing under martial law.

### 4

> GAO's central finding supports the conventional explanation: AID generally phased out its public-safety programs as Congress directed. The report did not describe OPS secretly reconstituted in another civilian agency. It identified questionable Thai commodity purchases made after the 1973 act, but the dispute involved pre-existing project obligations and procurement timing. That was serious enough for scrutiny and too ambiguous to prove deliberate evasion.

### 5

> The same restraint applies to proposals to “move it to Justice.” The 1973 debate proves that senators protected particular Justice, FBI and DEA activities. It does not show an en bloc transfer of OPS to Justice. A later Justice program performing similar work would require evidence of authority, personnel, records and organizational descent before it could be called an OPS successor.

### 6

> Abourezk asked whether the exception would permit Justice or FBI training for governments receiving foreign aid. McClellan said no: the purpose was international crime affecting the United States, not training foreign police forces for domestic control. After that explanation, Abourezk accepted it. The [October 1973 debate](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/GPO-CRECB-1973-pt25/pdf/GPO-CRECB-1973-pt25-3-2.pdf) therefore documents both intentions: stop the OPS model and preserve bounded federal law-enforcement cooperation.

### 7

> Dan Mitrione was part of the answer, but no longer the whole problem. Brazil, Uruguay and Vietnam had shown what the Office of Public Safety built: training systems, communications, records, intelligence capacity and riot-control forces. The most famous personal torture allegation remained unproved. The institutional record was enough to make the program politically toxic.

### 8

> By October 2, 1973, the Senate was considering section 115 of S. 2335. Its language would have barred appropriations under “this or any other provision of law” for foreign police training or a related program. That was broader than the statute Congress finally enacted because it reached beyond Foreign Assistance Act funds.

### 9

> AID ordered a phaseout. According to the later [GAO implementation review](https://www.gao.gov/products/id-76-5), country programs closed from February through August 1974. Records showed that every public-safety adviser had left an in-country post before the August deadline, although five remained overseas as full-time narcotics advisers. The International Police Academy held its final classes and the Washington apparatus contracted.

### 10

> The stronger critical interpretation also survives, in narrower form. Congress never prohibited every form of foreign-police cooperation. It deliberately preserved narcotics and specified Justice channels. Military aid created classification and indirect-benefit problems. Intelligence activities financed outside the aid act were not clearly resolved by Section 660 itself. Functional demand outlived the office.

### 11

> Section 660 did what its text said: it killed a funding model centered in foreign aid. Within years, police assistance would grow through a patchwork of authorities carrying different missions, safeguards and institutional histories. Determining whether any of those bodies inherited OPS—or merely performed similar work—requires following each agency separately.

### 12

> Abourezk's campaign began before the law that bears the argument's final shape. On February 4, 1972, the freshman Democratic senator offered an amendment aimed at AID public-safety spending outside Southeast Asia. He argued that association with local brutality damaged American interests and that foreign governments should fund their own police priorities.

### 13

> The text clearly covered foreign internal-intelligence or surveillance programs financed with Foreign Assistance Act funds. It did not clearly say that separately appropriated CIA intelligence liaison was prohibited. The same 1974 law added a separate reporting rule for CIA foreign operations, underscoring that Congress knew how to address intelligence funds directly.

### 14

> A June 1974 CIA legislative-counsel file shows the agency was analyzing the proposed police restriction. The available extraction is too poor to establish a definitive legal conclusion. The responsible judgment is that Section 660 closed the AID-funded route while the separately funded CIA boundary remained ambiguous in the evidence reviewed here.

### 15

> But a radio network can serve police, ministries and emergency services at once. A general-administration line can hide a police project or fund ordinary government operations. The reviewed records establish functional overlap and the risk of indirect benefit. They do not establish an organized renaming scheme intended to evade Congress.

### 2

> Joseph DiGennaro’s account sits at the outer edge of this evidence. He told Valentine that he had been recruited into the covered foreign side and participated in an overseas seizure and forcible return of a trafficker; he also alleged that lethal force had been authorized if the man resisted. Official records establish that a two-agent, nonofficial-cover foreign component existed, but no recovered operational order confirms DiGennaro’s rendition story or the claimed lethal authority. Other witnesses offered observations consistent with unusual cover arrangements, while the official account said the two agents were directed by BNDD and did not conduct operations in the United States.

### 3

> The practical wind-down was less tidy. Former officials later described cases and personnel arrangements that did not obey the clean boundary implied by the closing date. A 1974 inspection memorandum recorded agent Anthony Triponi’s allegation that he and another recruit had been selected in a covert process and were expected to report through Patrick Fuller. Inspectors also recorded that Triponi was acutely ill, having difficulty determining reality and at times becoming incoherent. The memorandum proves the allegation was made contemporaneously; it does not establish that the channel worked exactly as he described it.

### 4

> The recruits’ stated mission was internal security. BNDD had a genuine corruption problem, and the bureau wanted a cadre capable of investigating its own people. Terry Burke later told Valentine that after his December 1970 hiring he was assigned to examine allegations involving senior narcotics agents. His account is useful precisely because it is less cinematic than the mythology surrounding TWO-FOLD: he described building cases and credibility inside a troubled agency, not conducting a free-floating CIA operation.

### 5

> In another example, a prosecutor was prepared to indict the head of a trafficking organization and a major domestic distributor. After learning of the CIA collection effort, he declined. Strickler wrote that a separate CIA surveillance “immunized” not only the principal but numerous contacts surfaced through the operation. He also described a DEA target recruited by CIA with the help of confidential DEA information; CIA commitments then precluded a successful prosecution.

### 6

> Now put the pieces in chronological order. CIA had covert access at the top of OPS through Engle. It had a longstanding working relationship with federal narcotics enforcement. BNDD asked for clandestine instruction in 1969; TWO-FOLD recruited personnel beginning in 1970; its foreign component was preserved in 1973; CIA-experienced officers moved into DEA intelligence in 1973–74; and Section 660 preserved narcotics work as the wider police-assistance system was dismantled.

### 7

> A separate personnel bridge is better documented. In 1973 and 1974, seven former CIA special agents moved into BNDD and DEA as the new agency built a more sophisticated narcotics-intelligence capability. Administrative litigation over their retirement credit later preserved testimony about the arrangement. CIA supported the recruitment, and William Colby backed it as a way for DEA to develop its own capacity and relieve CIA of an intelligence obligation.

### 8

> Readers of this series already know the first door. AID’s Office of Public Safety, or **OPS**, trained, equipped and advised foreign police forces across the Cold War world. Its programs were presented as technical assistance: communications, records, patrol, criminal investigation, riot control and police management. In practice, that work offered access to the institutions that watched political movements, developed informants and enforced the security priorities of allied governments.

### 9

> The man at the center of that apparatus was **Byron Engle**, OPS director for more than a decade. His institutional reach extended through the International Police Academy in Washington and into overseas public-safety missions. The academy brought foreign officers to the United States for instruction, while American advisers worked within police organizations abroad. Narcotics enforcement was already part of that ecosystem, including instruction supplied by federal narcotics personnel.

### 10

> Jerry Strickler, a DEA operations official responsible for Europe and the Middle East, supplied cases rather than abstractions in a **February 11, 1977** memorandum. In one, foreign officials offered to deport a major fugitive to the United States. CIA then disclosed that the man had been subject to a CIA-coordinated electronic surveillance likely to trigger dismissal after discovery. The offer was not accepted, and the trafficker was released.

### 11

> The second door was older and less visible to the public. The Federal Bureau of Narcotics had long relied on overseas relationships, informants and liaison arrangements. When BNDD replaced it in 1968, the new bureau inherited an international problem that ordinary domestic policing could not solve. Heroin supply chains crossed borders; foreign services controlled access; and intelligence gathered overseas did not arrive in courtroom-ready form.

### 12

> In **November 1969**, Ingersoll asked CIA for guidance, initially focused on the clandestine development and handling of agents and informants abroad. A CIA training survey followed in March 1970. The Agency concluded that BNDD personnel needed broader clandestine tradecraft, not a single narrow course. By April 1972, according to the CIA Office of Training’s [fiscal-year report](https://www.cia.gov/readingroom/document/cia-rdp78-06207a000100060002-9), CIA instructors had briefed 18 BNDD Regional Intelligence Officers.

### 13

> The story is not that CIA “became” DEA, or that every narcotics officer answered to Langley. It is that American policing and intelligence had become interdependent in one of the few foreign-police channels Congress left open. That interdependence could solve problems neither agency could solve alone. It could also leave each agency unable to perform its basic public function without compromising the other.

### 14

> But the conventional explanation does not account for the chosen machinery. Advice does not require a proprietary company to conceal recruitment. Foreign liaison does not require recruits to be kept unaware of CIA’s role in their own employment. And protecting an agency from corruption does not erase the statutory problem created when an intelligence service secretly helps build a domestic law-enforcement unit.

### 15

> Charles Gutensohn’s account gives the arrangement a human scale. Gutensohn later told Valentine that he joined CIA in 1968 and spent roughly two years at Pakse, Laos. When that tour ended, he said, the Agency offered him a choice: return to Laos as CIA liaison to BNDD or enter TWO-FOLD in the United States. His brother Joel had already joined the program.

## OPENING COMPARISON

### Draft

> STATUS: DRAFT — REQUIRES HUMAN EDITORIAL REVIEW

> On **May 25, 1973**, the CIA’s director of security, Howard J. Osborn, sent a memorandum upstairs about a project most Americans had never heard of. The memo was only two pages long. Its subject was **TWO-FOLD**, and its closing instruction contained a small bureaucratic mystery: end one part of the operation, but keep the other.

> The part marked for termination had placed secretly recruited personnel inside the Bureau of Narcotics and Dangerous Drugs, or **BNDD**, to detect corruption. The part to be preserved involved nonofficial-cover agents assigned overseas against major drug traffickers. William Colby’s recommendation, approved by the director of central intelligence, was to [“terminate paragraph 2 activity and continue paragraph 3”](https://www.cia.gov/readingroom/docs/DOC_0001451843.pdf)—but only for foreign narcotics collection.

### Final

> By 1972, James Abourezk had reduced the argument over American foreign-police assistance to an uncomfortable question. Why should American taxpayers help governments police their own people when that help associated the United States with brutality and preservation of the political status quo?

> Dan Mitrione was part of the answer, but no longer the whole problem. Brazil, Uruguay and Vietnam had shown what the Office of Public Safety built: training systems, communications, records, intelligence capacity and riot-control forces. The most famous personal torture allegation remained unproved. The institutional record was enough to make the program politically toxic.

> Congress did eventually kill OPS. It did not enact a universal ban on Americans working with foreign police. The distinction was written into the law from the start.
