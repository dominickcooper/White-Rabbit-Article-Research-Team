# The Office Died. The Foreign Police System Did Not.

In July 1975, the United States Agency for International Development got out of the foreign-police business.

That was the official result. Congress had passed Section 660 of the Foreign Assistance Act, USAID's Office of Public Safety was shutting down, and the International Police Academy in Washington was closing with it. The office that had trained and equipped police from Latin America to Southeast Asia—the system we followed through the first five parts of this investigation—was dead.

Then the trainers kept appearing.

They appeared under the Justice Department. They appeared in State Department narcotics and antiterrorism programs. They appeared beside American troops in Panama, Haiti, Somalia and Bosnia. Later they arrived through private contractors, multiagency academies and “rule of law” missions.

So I went back to the prohibition that was supposed to have ended it.

What Congress killed was real. But the thing that grew after OPS was not another office with another name. It was harder to see than that.

It was an ecosystem.

## The Ban Had a Boundary

Part 5 showed how Congress closed OPS. The [1974 statutory text](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/STATUTE-88/pdf/STATUTE-88-Pg1795.pdf) prohibited funds made available under the Foreign Assistance Act from supporting foreign police, prisons, internal intelligence or surveillance.

Read that sentence once for what it did. Then read it again for where it stopped.

Section 660 governed Foreign Assistance Act money. It did not erase the independent authorities of every federal agency. The statute also preserved specified work by the Drug Enforcement Administration and FBI involving crimes illegal in the United States, along with international narcotics assistance. Congress later added exceptions for maritime enforcement, antiterrorism, selected countries and police forces in nations emerging from instability.

The prohibition therefore created a strange incentive. A broad, centralized police office inside AID became politically impossible. A specialized program tied to narcotics, terrorism, criminal investigations or post-conflict reconstruction could still be authorized.

The first surprise in the post-OPS record is not a secret memorandum. It is the law's architecture.

The second surprise is the money transfer.

## USAID Money, Justice Department Program

In 1983, Congress authorized an “administration of justice” program in Latin America. Two years later it expanded the exception to cover programs that enhanced investigative capabilities under judicial or prosecutorial control.

That language became the legal foundation for the International Criminal Investigative Training Assistance Program—ICITAP.

A [1992 GAO police-assistance report](https://www.gao.gov/products/nsiad-92-118) describes the founding transaction without drama: during fiscal year 1986, AID transferred funds to the Justice Department to design and implement projects improving the investigative capabilities of law-enforcement agencies in Latin America and the Caribbean. Justice established ICITAP. State oversaw the foreign-policy side.

That arrangement matters because each verb marks a different kind of control.

AID **funded**. Justice **administered and operated**. State **guided policy**. The host government **received** the program.

Calling all four roles “USAID activity” would hide the handoff. Calling ICITAP wholly independent of foreign aid would hide the money.

The institutional history in the National Defense University volume *Policing the New World Disorder* makes the design even stranger. ICITAP reported inside Justice, received policy guidance from State and, in its early years, received AID money routed through State. The agencies had built a program whose funding, policy and operations did not sit in the same department.

That was not an accident. According to officials interviewed for the history, AID wanted distance from the reputation of OPS. The FBI reportedly declined to house the new program because it feared association with “another OPS experience.” State could direct foreign policy but lacked the machinery to run a police-development program. The Pentagon had its own legal limits.

The compromise was a deliberately awkward hybrid.

## The Man in the Seam

Bureaucratic diagrams make this sound bloodless. David J. Kriskovich makes it concrete.

Kriskovich was an FBI inspector. In 1981 he developed a four-week criminal-investigations course known as the Caribbean Police School. In 1985 he trained El Salvador's Special Investigative Unit. The official history says the Defense Department funded that early SIU training while Kriskovich himself was funded by AID.

On January 6, 1986, he became ICITAP's founding director. He remained on loan from the FBI until retiring from ICITAP in 1994.

This is the cleanest personnel bridge I found: FBI expertise, AID money, Defense funding, a Salvadoran recipient unit and the Justice Department's new international police program converged in one officer before ICITAP formally opened its doors.

It is also where discipline matters most. I found no document placing Kriskovich in OPS or the CIA. He is not proof that the old office secretly changed its stationery. He is evidence that personnel, course designs and relationships crossed the seams between agencies even when the organization itself did not.

Under Kriskovich, ICITAP first concentrated on investigations and forensics. Congress had not authorized a return to the OPS menu of public-order tactics, weapons and counterinsurgency. By 1987, however, lawmakers allowed police management and academy-curriculum work. Beginning in 1990, country-specific and post-conflict authorities widened the mission again.

The same man who had organized a Caribbean investigations course was soon leading an agency that helped redesign entire police institutions.

In April 1997, Kriskovich became deputy commissioner for development in the U.N. International Police Task Force in Bosnia—the top American post in the mission. Five months later, he and other international police officials died in a helicopter crash on a fog-covered mountain.

The NDU volume is dedicated to them.

## One Program Was Not the System

If ICITAP were the whole story, Part 6 would be a familiar tale of bureaucratic rebirth. Congress closes OPS; Justice opens ICITAP; the work continues.

The numbers wreck that simplicity.

GAO tried to count U.S. foreign-police assistance in fiscal year 1990. It could not produce a complete total because agencies did not maintain compatible records. It still identified 125 countries receiving training or assistance at a cost of at least $117 million.

ICITAP accounted for roughly $20 million. State's international narcotics program accounted for about $45 million. Defense spent approximately $42 million on police assistance, much of it for counternarcotics forces in Latin America. State's Antiterrorism Assistance program added about $10 million.

Two or more programs operated in 46 countries.

[IMAGE: Bar chart using GAO FY1990 figures—State narcotics $45m, DOD $42m, ICITAP $20m and ATA $10m | ALT: GAO minimum foreign police assistance spending by program in fiscal year 1990]

This was not OPS restored. It was OPS decomposed.

Justice handled institutional development and criminal investigations. State ran narcotics and antiterrorism programs and supplied policy direction. DEA and FBI trained foreign counterparts under crime-specific authorities. Defense worked through military, counternarcotics and later post-conflict channels. Customs, Coast Guard, Marshals and other agencies had pieces of the field.

In 1995, President Bill Clinton announced the international law-enforcement academy system, promising multiagency training against narcotics, organized crime and terrorism. The [ILEA program](https://www.state.gov/international-law-enforcement-academy-ilea/) became another hub in the network, not its headquarters.

The foreign-police system had no single door anymore.

## Fragmentation Was the Accountability Problem

GAO's most important finding was not the minimum dollar figure. It was that no one in Washington could see the entire machine.

Officials described limited headquarters guidance, no clear government-wide policy, no central decision point and no reliable way to measure whether separate programs supported a common strategy. Narcotics, antiterrorism and ICITAP officials did not routinely coordinate across their program boundaries. Similar courses could be offered by different agencies. Even trainee totals might be counted twice—once by the funder and once by the trainer.

Coordination often fell to an embassy. That meant the quality of oversight depended on the ambassador, the staff available and whether anyone in country knew the full menu of Washington programs.

This is the conventional explanation for the fragmentation: the American government is divided by statute, committee jurisdiction, appropriations and professional culture. Congress wanted police aid constrained by purpose. Agencies protected their mandates. Transnational narcotics and terrorism were real problems that did not fit neatly inside a development agency.

All of that is true.

It is also why abolishing the notorious office did not abolish the capability. The work survived in pieces too specialized to look like the old whole.

## Post-Conflict Missions Put the Pieces Back Together

The next expansion did not begin with a theory of covert action. It began with governments collapsing.

After the U.S. intervention in Panama, ICITAP helped develop a new civilian Public Force. In El Salvador, the peace accords required a new National Civilian Police and police academy. In Haiti, Somalia and Bosnia, military and civilian missions confronted the same basic question: who keeps order when the old security force is discredited, broken or part of the conflict?

Congress eventually wrote a broad post-conflict exception into Section 660. It allowed assistance to reconstitute civilian police authority in nations emerging from instability, including training in human rights, rule of law, anticorruption and democratic civilian policing.

The rationale was not fictional. A peace settlement can fail if there is no legitimate institution to investigate crimes, manage public order or protect returning civilians. The NDU case studies repeatedly show soldiers being pulled toward police work because civilian missions arrived late or lacked personnel and logistics.

But post-conflict authority also restored much of the practical menu Congress thought it had removed from foreign aid: embedded advisers, academies, organizational design, recruitment, equipment, information systems and whole-force development.

ICITAP's [official historical milestones](https://www.justice.gov/criminal/criminal-icitap/icitap-historical-milestones) trace the widening route: Panama, El Salvador, Haiti, Bosnia, Kosovo, Afghanistan and Iraq. By 1996 the program's annual budget had passed $30 million—twenty times its first-year amount.

And much of the field work no longer had to be performed by federal employees.

## The Contractor Seam

By the mid-1990s, ICITAP relied heavily on contractors and subcontractors for overseas training and advising. Contracting solved a staffing problem. It also opened another gap between the agency that set policy, the department that paid, the company that employed personnel and the country expected to enforce the law.

Bosnia made that gap visible.

The State Department contracted DynCorp to supply the American police contingent to the U.N. International Police Task Force. Defense separately used DynCorp contractors for support to the NATO-led Stabilization Force. Those were different contracts serving different institutions, a distinction that disappears in many retellings.

Human Rights Watch's [2002 Bosnia trafficking investigation](https://www.hrw.org/reports/pdfs/b/bosnia/bosnia1102.pdf) drew on victim accounts, internal U.N. records, Army criminal-investigation files and sworn statements. It found evidence that some international police monitors and American civilian contractors purchased women or engaged in trafficking-related misconduct. In one Army investigation cited by HRW, a DynCorp employee admitted purchasing a woman from a brothel.

The employees could be sent home faster than local authorities could prosecute them. Bosnian officials believed immunity blocked their jurisdiction. U.S. investigators found gaps in federal law or interest. Repatriation became discipline, and discipline substituted for a public criminal process.

Kathryn Bolkovac, a DynCorp-employed IPTF human-rights officer, reported international involvement in trafficking. DynCorp fired her in 2001, officially for a timesheet violation. A British employment tribunal later unanimously found the company had unfairly dismissed her.

This evidence is severe enough without inflating it. It documents misconduct by named individuals, failures of supervision, retaliation allegations and a jurisdictional vacuum. It does **not** prove that DynCorp as a corporation operated a trafficking enterprise. A Defense review also found negligible evidence of widespread prostitution patronage by U.S. service members, while criticizing inconsistent contractor controls.

The point is the seam. The ecosystem could move personnel across borders more efficiently than accountability could follow them.

## Was the CIA Still Behind It?

Earlier parts of this series established something specific: CIA officers operated inside and beside OPS, and the International Police Academy included a concealed intelligence channel. Part 4 then showed how certain people, aircraft and functions migrated into later narcotics and private networks.

That history makes the CIA question unavoidable.

The post-OPS record shows the Agency active in counternarcotics and in interagency discussions involving foreign law enforcement. It shows geographic overlap with USAID, State, DEA, Defense and Justice. What it does not show—at least in the primary record assembled for this article—is CIA operational control of ICITAP, ILEA or later USAID justice programming.

No located document recreates the old IPA screening-and-referral pipeline. No founding paper calls ICITAP a covert replacement. No personnel file links Kriskovich to the Agency.

That is a negative finding, not proof that no classified relationship ever existed. But it changes the responsible conclusion. The documented mechanism of survival is law, money, personnel and interagency specialization. We do not need an invisible commander to explain it.

## The Modern Leads—and Their Limits

The ecosystem did not stop evolving in Bosnia. Modern USAID records show contractors running politically sensitive transition and information programs far outside traditional development work.

ZunZuneo is the strongest example in this corpus. A [USAID inspector-general review](https://oig.usaid.gov/sites/default/files/2018-06/9-000-16-001-s.pdf) confirms that the Office of Transition Initiatives awarded Creative Associates International a $15.5 million task order for Cuban civil-society programming. Twelve grants built a Twitter-like SMS and web platform. Implementers concealed U.S. sponsorship, used offshore arrangements and tried to measure user receptiveness and political tendencies.

The same review also draws boundaries. Most messages were sports and trivia. “Smart mobs” appeared in brainstorming, but investigators found no evidence they were organized. The inspector general did not determine that ZunZuneo was covert action.

Other viral claims weaken further under inspection. A military story about USAID-backed job fairs helping Special Forces block a Chinese port begins as a fictional vignette in the invented country of Naruvu; a later official-looking publication retells it as Nigeria. It is evidence of a contemplated method, not an operation that happened.

An alleged IRI report saying activists would “destabilize Bangladesh's politics” is also unusable as proof. The media-hosted file contains the sentence, but IRI says the clause was doctored and has not released the version it calls authentic. The underlying State grant and monitoring record remain missing.

Afghanistan presents a real collision among development, counternarcotics, counterinsurgency and an opium-dependent rural economy. That can produce perverse effects. It does not establish that USAID intended to support heroin production. The [USIP analysis of the Taliban poppy ban](https://www.usip.org/publications/2023/06/what-talibans-poppy-ban-means-afghan-poverty-and-migration) warns about poverty, migration and humanitarian shock; it does not advocate narcotics.

Those are Part 7 questions. They belong there because Part 6 has already answered the institutional one.

## The Successor Was the System

Congress did abolish OPS. The advisers left their posts. The academy closed. A notorious office lost its budget and authority.

But Congress did not abolish the problems to which police assistance was attached, the agencies that claimed jurisdiction over them, or the political demand for American influence over foreign security institutions. Instead, it forced those functions into narrower legal containers.

Investigations moved to Justice. Narcotics relationships expanded through State, DEA and Defense. Antiterrorism built another training route. Post-conflict authorities restored whole-force development. ILEA connected agencies through academies. Contractors provided bodies, logistics and plausible administrative distance.

The result was not a conspiracy chart in which every line means control. It was a federal ecosystem in which different lines meant different things: money, policy, training, personnel, information, contracts or shared recipients.

That distinction is the story.

OPS had been visible enough for Congress to kill. Its successor had no single director to summon, no single budget to cut and, as GAO discovered, no complete ledger to audit.

The office died.

The system learned to live without one.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Did ICITAP simply replace the Office of Public Safety?

No. ICITAP inherited some functions, began with AID-linked funding and worked in many familiar countries, but it was a Justice Department program with a narrower original mandate and State policy guidance. Other OPS-like functions dispersed across separate programs.

### Why would Congress allow foreign police assistance after banning it?

Congress distinguished broad support for internal-security forces from bounded missions involving U.S.-type crimes, narcotics, terrorism, maritime enforcement and post-conflict civilian policing. Those conventional policy goals were real, even as the exceptions rebuilt aggregate capacity.

### What connection is directly documented?

The clearest institutional connection is AID's 1986 transfer of funds to DOJ for the program that became ICITAP. The clearest personnel connection is David Kriskovich's path from FBI and AID-funded Salvadoran training into ICITAP and Bosnia.

### What is inferred rather than proved?

The ecosystem thesis is an inference from documented statutes, budgets, programs and personnel paths. A continuing post-OPS USAID–CIA command structure is not proved. Neither is a direct OPS/IPA personnel migration into ICITAP.

### What is the broader implication?

Abolishing one office can end a specific institution without ending the policy functions it performed. When those functions migrate across agencies and contractors, democratic oversight may become harder because responsibility is distributed even when capability expands.
