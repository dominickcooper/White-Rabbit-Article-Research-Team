## HISTORICAL STRIKES

### **1934 West Coast Longshore Strike**

The **[1934 West Coast Longshore Strike](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1934_West_Coast_waterfront_strike)**, which lasted from **May 9 to July 31, 1934** (a total of 83 days), saw over 12,000 longshoremen on strike across the entire West Coast, from San Diego to Seattle. This strike brought commerce to a halt as workers demanded better wages, shorter hours, and control over hiring. The strike was especially important because it paralyzed the flow of goods, including **grain, lumber, and other essential exports**, which affected not only the shipping companies but also the wider economy.

In terms of numbers:

- Nearly **50,000 workers were laid off** as shipping companies halted operations due to the strike.
- In Portland alone, approximately **15,000 workers** were affected.
- Violent confrontations between strikers and police resulted in **five deaths** and numerous injuries, with the infamous “**[Bloody](https://www.ilwu.org/bloody-thursday-1934-the-strike-that-shook-san-francisco-and-rocked-the-pacific-coast/)****[Thursday](https://www.ilwu.org/bloody-thursday-1934-the-strike-that-shook-san-francisco-and-rocked-the-pacific-coast/)**” in San Francisco where two strikers were killed on **July 5, 1934**.

Due to the standstill in shipping, **essential goods like lumber, grain, and food** were delayed, leading to shortages and higher prices. The halt in shipping also meant that the U.S. economy, already strained by the **Great** **Depression**, suffered further financial losses, with **ports** **idling** and **cargo** **piling** **up**, amounting to significant financial losses for businesses along the coast.

Ultimately, the strike ended with a **significant victory for the unions**, which gained control of **hiring** **halls**, **increased** **wages**, and **better** **working** **conditions**.

### **2015 West Coast Port Strike**

In 2015, the The Maritime Law Association of the United States [published a synopsis](https://mlaus.org/wp-content/uploads/bp-attachments/3647/Porter-Jennifer-KYL_SF-689608-v1-PDF_Porter_Paper_on_West_Coast_Port_Strike.pdf) of the 2015 strike. Here are the highlights:

- **Duration**: **July 2014 – February 2015**.
- **Impact**: This labor dispute between the **International Longshore and Warehouse Union** (ILWU) and the **Pacific Maritime Association** (PMA) severely disrupted operations at 29 West Coast ports. These ports handle a large portion of the nation’s imports and exports, **particularly trade with Asia**. The negotiations centered around **wages**, **benefits**, and **arbitration systems** for labor disputes. After months of **slowdowns** and **productivity** **reductions**, a tentative agreement was reached in **February** **2015**.
- **Effect on Shipping and Consumers**: The slowdowns and work stoppages caused **significant congestion** at West Coast ports. For example, at the height of the crisis, **33 cargo ships** were anchored outside of Los Angeles-Long Beach terminals. It took **up to three months** to clear the backlog of cargo even after a resolution was reached. The **U.S. economy lost an estimated $2 billion per day** during the peak of the strike, affecting industries across the board:

  - **20% of fresh fruit and vegetable exports** to Asia were delayed by 3-4 weeks.
  - **Retailers incurred $7 billion** in costs and losses due to the disruptions.
  - The **U.S. meat industry** lost about **$85 million per week**.
  - U.S. exports dropped by **15% in January 2015** compared to the previous year.

## [WHAT TO EXPECT](https://www.barrons.com/news/us-port-officials-gird-for-strike-as-labor-talks-stay-stuck-4273737c)

According to an article by [The Washington Post](https://www.washingtonpost.com/business/2024/10/01/port-strike-shutdown-east-coast-dockworkers-union/), this is the first large-scale strike of its kind since 1977. The **International Longshoremen's Association (ILA)** workers are protesting for higher wages and opposing the automation of crucial tasks at ports, such as crane and truck operations.

The strike affects **36 ports** that handle nearly **half of the nation’s ocean shipping**, which could lead to significant economic fallout, particularly as it coincides with the peak holiday shopping season. Essential goods like **cars, coffee, and vegetables** could face delays, and **price increases** are expected as retailers struggle with supply chain bottlenecks. According to [Peter Sand](https://uk.finance.yahoo.com/news/us-east-coast-dockworkers-head-043104467.html?guccounter=1&guce_referrer=aHR0cHM6Ly9jaGF0Z3B0LmNvbS8&guce_referrer_sig=AQAAADGMfErMI-DfXVVdv9UxRARK0W0ygW_uKvBF3yA6n9UVbSl-QUVYIPTeUggQ3O1LW-C8gjtnEcHYGwTAeh8k87aI6oxPWm1T2SSI4B6q1l7yaOEy7WXut2dQzxyTsiCzLrPp6L1g-XKnamkh0X0V9F8ZPC1vGfmAed9p0uFR80rI), chief analyst at shipping pricing platform Xeneta:

> "We expect the strike itself to last for five to seven days until a government intervention ... but the ripple effect is likely to be felt across the whole network into Europe, into Asia for at least into January, February.”

[Rick Cotton](https://uk.finance.yahoo.com/news/us-east-coast-dockworkers-head-043104467.html?guccounter=1&guce_referrer=aHR0cHM6Ly9jaGF0Z3B0LmNvbS8&guce_referrer_sig=AQAAADGMfErMI-DfXVVdv9UxRARK0W0ygW_uKvBF3yA6n9UVbSl-QUVYIPTeUggQ3O1LW-C8gjtnEcHYGwTAeh8k87aI6oxPWm1T2SSI4B6q1l7yaOEy7WXut2dQzxyTsiCzLrPp6L1g-XKnamkh0X0V9F8ZPC1vGfmAed9p0uFR80rI), the executive director of the Port Authority of New York and New Jersey shared that **there are are nearly 100,000 containers** in New York City-area ports alone waiting to be unloaded, now frozen by the strike, and **35 container ships headed to New York** over the coming week.

### **Supply Chain Disruption**

- **Short-Term Impact**: In the immediate aftermath, key ports such as **New York, Baltimore, Norfolk, Savannah,** and **Houston** being closed will create bottlenecks in the supply chain. This affects industries ranging from consumer goods to manufacturing. **Perishable** **goods**, such as **bananas (75% of imports)**, will be particularly vulnerable, leading to shortages.
- **Medium-Term Impact**: Auto manufacturing will be hit due to disruptions in part deliveries. With **four of the five largest ports** for receiving imported cars affected, the auto industry could see significant slowdowns, which may contribute to further **supply** **shortages** and **price** **increases**.

### **Economic Cost**

- **Projected Losses**: Analysts estimate that a **week-long strike** could cost the economy **[$3.78 billion](https://www.barrons.com/news/us-port-officials-gird-for-strike-as-labor-talks-stay-stuck-4273737c)**. This number will climb as the strike persists, with ripple effects in various industries, especially smaller businesses unable to divert shipments to the West Coast or secure air transport.
- **Inflationary Pressures**: The cost to consumers **will increase** as shortages begin to affect a broader range of products. Though larger retailers may have prepared by shifting shipments early, **smaller** **businesses** will face immediate shortages, leading to **price hikes**, particularly in **food and consumer goods**.

### **Political Pressure**

- **Potential Government Intervention**: [Calls for invoking the](https://nam.org/manufacturers-call-on-president-to-invoke-taft-hartley-act-to-stop-port-strike-32188/?stream=policy-legal) **[Taft-Hartley Act](https://nam.org/manufacturers-call-on-president-to-invoke-taft-hartley-act-to-stop-port-strike-32188/?stream=policy-legal)** are already growing, particularly from business groups and Republicans in Congress. However, **President** **Biden** has expressed a reluctance to use his legal powers to end the strike, believing in the importance of **collective** **bargaining**.
- **Political Ramifications**: With the strike happening just five weeks before the election, it could become a **significant** **political** **issue**, especially if it leads to visible shortages or economic strain. If the strike persists, it may put pressure on **Vice President Kamala Harris's campaign**, as well as on the broader **Biden** **administration**, to take more direct action.

### **Labor Movement Implications**

- **Strengthened Union Power**: The militancy of the union suggests that they are prepared for a prolonged fight, which may inspire **other labor movements across industries**. As with past strikes, the longer the strike continues, the more leverage the union may gain, especially as public sympathy grows due to potential **economic** **disruptions**.

## **What should you do?**

In anticipation of a long strike on the East Coast and Gulf Coast ports, the **average American** should prepare for potential **disruptions in imports**, particularly those coming from **Europe, South America, and parts of Asia**, as these regions primarily ship goods to the affected ports. Here’s a breakdown of what you should consider:

### **Steps to Take:**

1. **Stock up on non-perishable staples**: Focus on items with a long shelf life such as canned goods, pasta, rice, and frozen foods, which are less likely to experience price fluctuations or shortages in the immediate term.
2. **Purchase fresh produce and other perishables strategically**: Buy fresh fruits and vegetables that are locally sourced or in-season, as imported produce might see delays.
3. **Monitor prices and availability of imported goods**: Begin tracking prices for items that may be impacted by the strike. Consider purchasing extra now if you can, especially for products that come from international markets.
4. **Alternative shopping options**: Check out local markets or alternative supply sources like community-supported agriculture (CSA) programs, which may have more stable supplies of fresh produce.

### **Products Likely to Be Affected**:

#### **Highly Likely to See Price Increases or Shortages**:

1. **Imported [Fruits & Vegetables](https://www.ers.usda.gov/data-products/chart-gallery/gallery/chart-detail/?chartId=107008)**:

   - **Bananas, avocados, grapes** (often imported from Latin America)
   - **Citrus fruits** (oranges, lemons) from Europe and South America
   - **Likelihood**: **Highly Likely**. Imported perishables depend on fast turnover, so delays will quickly lead to shortages.
2. **Seafood**:

   - **Shrimp, salmon, tuna** (imports from Asia and South America)
   - **Likelihood**: **Highly Likely**. The U.S. imports [a significant portion](https://www.ers.usda.gov/amber-waves/2024/may/u-s-seafood-imports-expand-as-domestic-aquaculture-industry-repositions-itself/) of seafood from global markets, and any disruptions will create gaps in availability​.
3. **Beef**:

   - While overall meat supplies would not be drastically affected, **specific cuts** or types of beef that are imported (such as processed beef or certain lower-cost cuts) might see **price increases** due to delays in shipments. This is particularly true for **[Brazilian and Uruguayan beef](https://www.tsln.com/news/on-the-move-more-beef-imports-from-australia-brazil-uruguay/)**, which is often used for these purposes.  
       
     Since some processed meats incorporate imported beef, products like **frozen beef patties, ground beef blends**, and certain **canned or frozen processed meat products** might see **price increases** or **temporary shortages**, especially if the strike lasts several weeks.
   - **Likelihood**: **Likely**. The strike could disrupt the flow of imports, leading to shortages or price increases on processed and imported meat​.
4. **Chicken**:

   1. Poultry imports from South America are **minimal**, with most [U.S. poultry](https://www.ers.usda.gov/topics/animal-products/poultry-eggs/) being produced domestically. Therefore, **poultry supplies** would likely remain **stable** during a strike, with little to no price increases expected due to import disruptions.

While the strike could lead to **short-term price hikes** on specific types of imported meat (particularly **processed beef**), it is unlikely to result in significant **shortages** or **drastic price increases** for the overall meat and poultry supply. The domestic production infrastructure should be able to mitigate most of the impact.

#### **Likely to See Price Increases**:

1. **Electronics and Appliances**:

   - Laptops, TVs, and kitchen appliances rely heavily on international supply chains, [particularly from Asia](https://www.statista.com/chart/23356/largest-manufacturers-of-electrical-and-electronic-equipment-by-revenue/).
   - Prices may rise as shipping delays worsen and inventory decreases, especially with holiday shopping around the corner.
2. **Clothing and Footwear**:

   - Many garments are [imported](https://wits.worldbank.org/CountryProfile/en/Country/ECS/Year/LTST/TradeFlow/Export/Partner/by-country/Product/50-63_TextCloth) from Asia and Europe.
   - Retailers may experience delays, particularly on new fashion lines for the fall/winter season​.
3. **Coffee and Cocoa Products**:

   - Coffee beans from South America and Africa, along with chocolate products.
   - Although coffee prices are already high, further delays could exacerbate the situation, leading to limited supplies and price hikes​.

### **Less Likely to Be Affected**:

1. **Dairy Products**:

   - Most [U.S. dairy products](https://www.idfa.org/news/u-s-dairy-industry-shatters-export-records-in-2022) are domestically produced.
   - Domestic supply chains will likely cover demand unless other strikes or issues arise in the trucking or dairy industries.
2. **[Grains](https://apps.fas.usda.gov/psdonline/circulars/grain.pdf) (Wheat, Corn, Rice)**:

   - Grains are primarily produced domestically, and while some are exported, most will remain unaffected by the strike.
   - Domestic production and availability will prevent major price swings.

In summary, the **most vulnerable items** to price increases and shortages will be **fresh produce, seafood, meats, and electronics**, especially those imported through the affected East and Gulf Coast ports. Prepare accordingly by stocking up on **non-perishables** and **locally sourced products** where possible. Expect price hikes and limited availability within **two to three weeks** of a prolonged strike.
