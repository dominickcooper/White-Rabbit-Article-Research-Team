In 1972, a [CIA inspector-general inquiry](https://www.intelligence.senate.gov/sites/default/files/94755_I.pdf) investigated reports that **Air America** was carrying opium out of Laos. It rejected the idea of a corporate trafficking policy or an order from senior management.

*AKA: Drug running? Not the CIA.*

But the same inquiry also found that opium could—and did—get aboard, that local passengers loaded cargo beyond the airline’s full control, and that the CIA knew allied figures and territory were entangled with narcotics.

Suppressing that trade ranked below the war.

But here’s the rub: *What if “the war” and the drug trade weren’t really separate systems? What if the aircraft, roads, military alliances, police networks and covert relationships built to fight the war also created the infrastructure through which the drug trade could move?*

There is a much bigger question buried inside that one—how deeply the Southeast Asian heroin economy became intertwined with the Vietnam War itself. That’s an investigation of its own.

Because once I started following the **CIA’s connections to the drug trade**, the trail didn’t stop in Vietnam.

It led to Laos. To Long Tieng. To Washington police classrooms. To Air America veterans flying for the Contras. To cocaine traffickers inside covert supply networks. To Justice Department reporting rules that treated CIA assets differently from CIA employees.

And one institution kept appearing in the middle of those connections.

**USAID.**

This article follows the money and the documents from mountain villages in Laos to police classrooms in Washington, from a disputed C-123 to the Contra supply network—and ultimately to the overt aid and police machinery that gave covert operations something extraordinarily valuable: **logistics, access, institutional cover, and a pipeline to foreign personnel.**

The CIA inquiry found no evidence that Air America adopted a trafficking policy, that senior officers ordered narcotics carriage, or that American pilots knowingly participated. It also described incomplete controls over locally loaded cargo and acknowledged occasional carriage. CIA officers were working with commanders and through territory already implicated in the opium economy.

**TRANSLATION**: *Yeah, some drugs got through on our CIA planes, but that wasn’t us. I mean humans are as humans do - these were just opportunistic pilots and locals trying to make a buck. Besides, we were too busy with the war to really crack down on it - limited resources and all.*

## **1962: STAY IN THE BACKGROUND**

Before we get to the opium, we need to go back to **1962**.

On **July 23**, fourteen nations signed **[the Geneva agreements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Agreement_on_the_Neutrality_of_Laos)** declaring Laos neutral. The accords required the withdrawal of foreign regular troops, irregular troops, paramilitary formations, and military personnel. Washington complied publicly by withdrawing its military advisory teams. But North Vietnam did not fully withdraw its forces, and the United States continued supporting **Lao and Hmong forces** after the agreement.

**That created an obvious problem.**

***How do you keep fighting a war**   
in a country where you’re not supposed to be fighting a war?*

A month later, on **August 24, 1962**, President **John F. Kennedy** approved **[NSAM 182](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1961-63v08/d105)**, establishing a government-wide doctrine for what Washington called **“Overseas Internal Defense.”**

This was national policy for **State, Defense, AID, USIA, and CIA**, distributed to American missions and military commands around the world.

The September policy paper that followed was remarkably candid about how this kind of war should work.

American involvement, it said, should be **“ancillary to the indigenous effort.”** The United States should **“remain in the background”** and, whenever possible, restrict its visible role to training, advice, and materiel.

The objective was to build **local forces** capable of fighting the internal war themselves while **minimizing overt American intervention**.

Plain English?

***Keep our hands clean.***

And that doctrine wasn’t limited to soldiers.

The document identified **police as the first line of defense against subversion and insurgency**.

*Remember part 2 of this series??*

Washington would provide **friendly foreign police** with equipment, training, and technical assistance—and the normal channel for doing so would be **AID**, although Defense or CIA could take the role depending on the circumstances.

For indigenous paramilitary forces,   
the policy explicitly called for Defense to work   
**“in collaboration with AID and CIA.”**

## **THE GOLDEN TRIANGLE**

Now, Northern Laos already had an opium economy. The mountain communities cultivated a portable cash crop in terrain where roads, buyers and alternative markets were scarce; Chinese networks, merchants, soldiers and officials moved it through a regional trade.

**Very agrarian.** Very simple.

Then roads, aviation, military support, USAID, and covert alliances entered the same valleys.

*What are the chances?*

One of the most discussed accounts from that region has to do with **[Edgar “Pop” Buell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pop_Buell)**.

He was an aid worker through the [International Voluntary Services](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Voluntary_Services) (a precursor to the **Peace Corps**) who became a legendary figure among Hmong communities. [According to Don Schanche’s telling](https://archive.org/details/misterpop00scha), Buell first tried to replace poppy cultivation with sweet potatoes.

The crop grew. The market did not.

Villagers lacked transport and dependable buyers. And then there was shelf life. Sweet potatoes spoiled; opium did not.

**Plus opium was much more lucrative.**

A small amount of opium could be carried across difficult terrain and exchanged for cash in a way bulky food crops could not. So the substitution failed because the surrounding economy offered no workable substitute market.

### **“If You’re Gonna Grow It, Grow It Good”**

Buell changed tactics.

> ***“If you’re gonna grow [opium], grow it good.”***
>
> — [Edgar “Pop” Buell](https://archive.org/details/misterpop00scha)

Buell believed immediate eradication of the poppies would impoverish the villages; however, he also tried to discourage local smoking.

Basically *“people are going to buy it, either from us or someone else. May as well be us.”*

Now Buell **wasn't a random white man** in a remote village. The [International Voluntary Services](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Voluntary_Services) he was a part of received 70-90% of its funding from USAID.

And this introduction of American aid relief added an uncomfortable economic turn.

**American medicines** reduced some of the medicinal reasons villagers consumed opium, while production continued to rise. **Less local use** then left **more of the crop** available for sale.

The crop remained, and so did **the need for a buyer.**

***Enter CIA.***

### **The Buyer Changed**

At [Phou Vieng](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dong_Phou_Vieng_National_Protected_Area), a deteriorating crop presented an immediate crisis. **Blea Vu** and Buell discussed selling it to the [Pathet Lao](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pathet_Lao) (*the communists*).

**Buell liked the idea** - make some money and get the enemy doped up.   
So fourteen ponies carried the opium out.

Unfortunately, the buyer tricked them and **paid them in counterfeit money.**

*SOB.*

So word spread and Phou Vieng **stopped dealing with the Communist buyers**. At least Blea Vu’s village did.

At first.

Later the Pathet Lao market completely disappeared.   
But only in the areas served by **Air America.**

## **BOOTS ON THE GROUND: ALFRED McCOY’S ACCOUNT**

Now Alfred McCoy, unlike Don Schanche, was not simply writing about the drug trade during the Vietnam War.

**He was actually in it.**

There are several observations and interviews (done **during the war**, mind you) and they were all put down in his book “[The Politics of Heroin in Southeast Asia](https://archive.org/details/politicsofheroin0000mcco).”

McCoy’s interviews describe buyers, prices, packing methods and seasonal routines.

### **Before the Helicopters**

In the Long Pot area, McCoy reported that **Hmong officers** entered villages to test and purchase opium.

**Hmong.**The side that the US was helping. (*for clarity*)

The Hmong officers were paying **$60 per kilogram**, compared with **$40 to $50** offered by other merchants. The purchased opium went into bamboo containers, onto ponies and toward **[Long Tieng](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Long_Tieng) in Laos**. Collection circuits could repeat two or three times during a season.

Village-to-village buying, consolidation and pack-animal movement linked producing communities to the military center.

### **Then the Helicopters Arrived**

**Ger Su Yang**, a village leader [interviewed by McCoy in 1971](https://archive.org/details/politicsofheroin0000mcco), described a change in 1969 and 1970. The officers no longer needed to ride the whole route. They arrived **by helicopter**, spent days moving among villages, then radioed Long Tieng for pickup.

> *“Meo officers with three or four stripes [captain or major) came from Long Tieng to buy our opium.*
>
> ***They came in American helicopters,** perhaps two or three men at one time.*
>
> *The helicopter leaves them here for a few days and they walk to villages over there (swinging his arm in a semicircle in the direction of Gier Goot, Long Makkhay and Nam Pac], then come back here and radioed Long Tieng to send another helicopter for them.*
>
> ***They take the opium back to Long Tieng**.”*
>
> *—* Ger Su Yang Interview

The use of helicopters (specifically American ones) was further corrobated by **[General Ouane Rattikone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ouane_Rattikone)** and former Royal Lao Air Force commander **[Thao Ma](https://vi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thao_Ma).**

**Independently,** it was discovered that Ouane had also developed a close relationship with Ted Shackley (the CIA station chief in Vientiane).

### **Mae Salong: Roads Into an Opium Economy**

By the early 1960s, **Mae Salong** had become the headquarters of **General Tuan Hsi-wen’s 5th Chinese Irregular Force**, descended from the KMT armies driven out of Yunnan after Mao’s victory.

The American relationship with those forces went back at least to **1951**, when a [top-secret State Department memorandum](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1951v06p1/d162?utm_source=chatgpt.com) recorded Thailand’s prime minister saying that a representative of the **“American Intelligence Agency”** had asked him to provide facilities supporting **General Li Mi’s KMT forces** in Burma. He agreed.

The KMT remnants eventually reorganized into the **Chinese Irregular Forces (CIF)**, with Tuan’s 5th Army based at **Mae Salong**.

[A later CIA assessment](https://www.cia.gov/readingroom/docs/CIA-RDP86T01017R000808060002-7.pdf?utm_source=chatgpt.com) described the 3rd and 5th Armies as the region’s dominant opium traffickers—and noted that **intelligence networks they had already built in Burma were well suited to distributing opium throughout eastern Burma and northern Thailand.**

*At least they’re being resourceful.*

So the **intelligence network** and the **narcotics network** were using **the same infrastructure.**

Washington was still dealing with those forces twenty years later. In **September 1971**, [CIA’s Far East Division wrote](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76v20/d136?utm_source=chatgpt.com) that the CIF had survived for years **“primarily on the revenue obtained from opium traffic.”**

Yet [the same memorandum](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76v20/d136?utm_source=chatgpt.com) discussed secretly using the CIF ***against*** heroin refineries near Tachilek, with officials insisting that **the American role remain hidden**.

## **THE PIPELINE REACHES THE WORLD**

By the early 1970s, the question was no longer whether Golden Triangle heroin could reach Americans.

It was **how it was getting there**.

The answer was: almost any way it could.

[State Department testimony from 1972](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76ve01/d220) described heroin moving from **Vientiane to Tan Son Nhut Airport** in Saigon, where it reached the enormous market created by the American war.

The same testimony documented **17 pounds of high-purity No. 4 heroin** routed from Thailand through **U-Tapao Air Base** toward the United States using forged military paperwork.

Military mail offered another lane.

On April 5, 1971, Customs officers at **Fort Monmouth, New Jersey**, seized roughly **7.7 kilograms** of Double U-O Globe heroin sent from Bangkok through the military postal system. McCoy also reported a **15.5-kilogram Vientiane-to-New York shipment** later that year involving a Filipino diplomat and a Chinese merchant from Bangkok (*that sounds like the beginning of a corny joke*). [McCoy’s account](https://archive.org/details/politicsofheroin0000mcco) followed the couriers to **their arrests in New York.**

The covert-war system did not need to run street sales in New York or build a new syndicate in Marseille. Once the product reached an international transit point, **professional traffickers** already had **brokers**, **chemists**, **ships**, **couriers**, **corrupt** **contacts**…

And, most importantly, **customers**.

Former CIA officer **Lucien Conein** told McCoy that Corsican contacts in Vientiane supplied buyers in Saigon, who used **French merchant vessels** and family connections to move morphine base toward Marseille.

## **RETURN OF THE POLICE ACADEMY**

*Remember all those foreign police officers from Part 2?*

**They’re back.**

By FY1971, the [Justice Department](https://ojp.gov/pdffiles1/Digitization/9474NCJRS.pdf) reported that the International Police Academy had trained **3,932 officers from 73 countries**. Graduates commanded **13 national police agencies**. And DOJ openly described IPA graduates and overseas OPS advisers as **valuable contacts** for Justice Department representatives.

Three years later, the academy’s own *[Police Academy Review](https://ojp.gov/pdffiles1/Digitization/19349NCJRS.pdf)* claimed **5,204 graduates from 77 countries**.

Thousands of officers had passed through the school in Washington and returned to police institutions **around the world.**

And, **as it turns out,** narcotics instruction was already part of that machinery.

Treasury reports for [1966](https://fraser.stlouisfed.org/files/docs/publications/treasar/AR_TREASURY_1966.pdf), [1967](https://fraser.stlouisfed.org/files/docs/publications/treasar/AR_TREASURY_1967.pdf) and [1968](https://fraser.stlouisfed.org/files/docs/publications/treasar/AR_TREASURY_1968.pdf) show the Federal Bureau of Narcotics repeatedly teaching at **the AID academy**. Police training and drug enforcement were not two separate stories that happened to collide in Laos. Washington had been joining them **for years**.

That was the overt system.

**But the CIA maintained its own door into the same world.**

Part 2 documented the **Agency’s Police Group**, its liaison with OPS and IPA, the exchange of participant information, biographies and assessments, and the **sponsorship of selected foreign officers**.

*So, the CIA could literally **cherry-pick** officers from other countries, send them to the US for “police training” under USAID, **and then send them back** without any CIA affiliation on paper.*

Selected officers could also receive additional **CIA instruction** or be assessed for possible **operational use**.

This is why the police story was never a tangent.

**It was setup.**

## **LAOS BUILDS A NARCOTICS POLICE SYSTEM**

Heroin arriving from Vientiane helped force Washington to pay attention to Laos.

With encouragement from the U.S. Mission, the Royal Lao Government created a narcotics-control program.

**USAID/OPS, BNDD and Customs** helped build it.

The August 1974 [Vientiane program record](https://foia.state.gov/DOCUMENTS/FOIA_Micro_Oct2024_10/F-1989-03959/DOC_0C09000035/C09000035.pdf) counted **711 Lao personnel** trained across the National Police, Military Police, Customs, the Groupe Spécial d’Investigation and the judiciary.

The program had reached roughly **$2 million in 1973**.

**USAID** supplied more than classroom instruction too: the plan included advisers, participant training, vehicles, motorcycles, communications equipment and records work. So now Washington had a **material relationship** with the agencies expected to control narcotics routes.

Inside the Royal Lao Army, the Provost Marshal General created a narcotics-control section. **One hundred sixty-three military police** received initial narcotics training. **Seven** received narcotics training **in the United States**, with possible destinations including the U.S. Army Military Police School, the International Police Academy and DEA’s training school.

*And what were these military police supposed to investigate?*

Oh!

Trafficking by **Royal Lao Army officials** and the use of **military transportation** to move narcotics.

***Just sit with that for a moment.***

The same U.S.-supported security establishment surrounding the war (and most of the trafficking, as we’ve seen) was now being trained by the United States to police the drug trade inside itself.

## **WHO GUARDS THE GATES?**

**A global heroin pipeline** needs more than farmers, pilots and gangsters.

It needs **gatekeepers**.

- Customs officers decide which suitcase gets opened.
- Airport police decide which passenger gets stopped.
- Military police decide which vehicle gets searched.
- Narcotics investigators decide which allegation becomes a case.
- Intelligence officers decide which information reaches law enforcement—and which information doesn’t.

**That is the power of the chokepoint.** A trafficking organization does not need every officer on the payroll. It needs warning at the right airport, a friendly commander at the right checkpoint, or an allegation to land on the desk of someone who considers *another mission* more important.

And when you have drug-empire levels of money, **paying off the right people is easy.**

- **[Tan Son Nhut customs inspectors — 1972](https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/1972/07/29/vietnam-v-survivors)** — Vietnamese customs officers reportedly made **many times their official salaries** by simply not inspecting *certain* baggage and cargo.
- **[Pan Am employee — 1971](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/F2/469/973/79829/)** — A Pan Am employee in Nassau testified he was bribed to **pre-clear two footlockers containing 133 pounds of marijuana**, bypassing normal Customs inspection.
- **[Tan Son Nhut cargo corruption](https://abuse-drug.com/lib/library/books/McCoy/book/45.htm)** — The chief collections officer at Tan Son Nhut cargo warehouse was getting roughly **$22,000 a month** (equivalent to about **$190,000** in 2026). You can only imagine what his superiors made.
- **[San Ysidro customs inspector José Barron — 1988](https://www.latimes.com/archives/la-xpm-1988-09-20-mn-2312-story.html)** — Prosecutors accused Barron of taking about **$1 million in bribes** to wave dozens of marijuana-loaded vehicles through his inspection lane.
- **[Operation Clean Sweep — 1987](https://www.latimes.com/archives/la-xpm-1987-06-16-mn-7467-story.html)** — A nationwide Customs corruption probe found that **front-line inspectors were especially valuable to traffickers** because corrupting one officer could effectively open a border lane.
- **[JFK baggage handlers — 1987](https://time.com/archive/6708644/drugs-a-1-5-billion-inside-job/)** — Airport workers allegedly diverted cocaine-filled suitcases **before they reached Customs**, helping move an estimated **$1.5 billion in cocaine** over several years.

If it wasn’t clear, Washington had spent **years** building relationships with the institutions **standing at those gates.**

And all the systems in place worked like clockwork:

- **USAID** funded advisers, training, vehicles, communications equipment and records systems.
- IPA trained thousands of officers.
- DOJ viewed graduates as useful overseas contacts.
- CIA maintained separate liaison, assessment and sponsorship channels.
- BNDD, Customs and later DEA connected those institutions to the international drug war.

None of those relationships alone produced protection.

But together they shaped who exchanged intelligence, who trained whom, and which Americans could pick up a telephone overseas and **reach a familiar police official**.

That is influence in its most practical form. With access and leverage like that, you didn’t need ownership.

**Then the CIA files add one more detail.**

Under [Project TWO-FOLD](https://www.cia.gov/readingroom/docs/DOC_0001451843.pdf), CIA secretly helped **spot**, **assess** and **recruit personnel** who became BNDD employees. Some were intended for **internal-security work**; others operated under **nonofficial cover** against international traffickers.

And **the recruits didn’t know** CIA was handling part of the process.

So CIA did not merely have doors into foreign police systems.

It had its own undercover personnel (**agents or assets**) touching America’s own narcotics bureau.

That is how a pipeline *can* become insulated: not by controlling every cop, but by having access at **the chokepoints** that matter.

## **THE PROGRAM ENDED. THE CONTACTS DIDN’T.**

By 1974, broad **USAID police assistance** was already winding down.

Congress then enacted [Section 660](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/STATUTE-88/pdf/STATUTE-88-Pg1795.pdf), formally **restricting Foreign Assistance Act support** for foreign police, prisons, law-enforcement forces and internal-intelligence programs beginning July 1, 1975.

But there was an exception:

**It did not close the international narcotics channel.**

Section 660 preserved narcotics-control assistance and specified DEA/FBI activities from the beginning.

Laos shows the handoff in advance. The 1974 mission plan called for OPS narcotics assistance to end at the close of FY1975 while **DEA assumed advisory responsibility** for Lao narcotics institutions.

The office could close.

The academy could close.

**The relationships didn’t disappear when the program did.  
They just handed over the reins to the DEA.**

## **FAQ**

### **Did CIA create the Golden Triangle drug trade?**

No. The regional opium economy and criminal networks existed independently. What the documents show is that U.S. covert-war infrastructure strengthened or intersected with actors, routes and institutions already involved in that economy.

### **How did Golden Triangle heroin reach international markets?**

Through multiple overlapping channels: commercial aircraft, military bases, military mail, diplomatic baggage, merchant shipping, fishing vessels, servicemen and civilian couriers. Saigon, Bangkok, Hong Kong, Paris, Marseille and New York all appear in the documented routes.

### **What role did organized crime play?**

Corsican, Chinese and American criminal networks already knew how to move, refine and wholesale narcotics. They supplied the international distribution system once the product left the regional pipeline.

### **Why does the International Police Academy matter?**

IPA trained thousands of foreign officers and sent them back into police institutions worldwide. DOJ treated graduates as useful contacts, while CIA maintained separate channels for information, assessment, sponsorship and additional training involving selected officers.

### **Did closing OPS and IPA eliminate the network?**

No. The programs genuinely wound down, but their graduates, advisers, equipment and relationships remained. International narcotics authorities also stayed open, and DEA was positioned to inherit specific advisory responsibilities.
