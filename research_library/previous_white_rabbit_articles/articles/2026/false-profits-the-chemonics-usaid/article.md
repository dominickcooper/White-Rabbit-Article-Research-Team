For decades, [Chemonics International](https://en.m.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemonics) has stood as a towering force in the world of international development, securing billions of dollars in contracts from the U.S. Agency for International Development (USAID). On the surface, it presents itself as a key player in humanitarian aid, providing expertise in governance, agriculture, healthcare, and economic development across more than 100 countries. However, beneath its polished branding lies a different reality—one of lucrative deals, political entanglements, and a questionable track record.

Unlike traditional nonprofit organizations working in the global development space, Chemonics is a for-profit corporation. Its primary goal, like any business, is not charity but revenue. Between 2008 and 2024 alone, [it secured](https://www.usaspending.gov/award/CONT_AWD_AIDOAATO1500007_7200_AIDOAAI1500004_7200) over **$17 billion** in USAID contracts, making it the single largest recipient of U.S. foreign aid funding in history. This raises a critical question: is Chemonics truly an agent of humanitarian progress, or is it an opportunistic middleman, profiting from crises while masquerading as a force for good?

## The Origins of Chemonics

The deeper one digs into Chemonics’ history, the more troubling the connections become. The firm was founded in 1975 by **Thurston F. (Tony) Teele**, a former USAID political officer, with backing from **Gerald D. Murphy**, the CEO of Erly Industries.

Murphy once [candidly stated](https://web.archive.org/web/20220923074836/https://www.nytimes.com/1993/10/11/business/cozy-links-to-a-us-agency-prove-useful-to-a-rice-trader.html) that he started Chemonics because he had always wanted **“a way to do two things: one, have my own CIA, and two, be helpful to people.”**

This offhand remark, buried in an old interview with the New York Times, now rings with eerie significance.

Was Chemonics conceived as a simple consulting firm, or was it designed as an intelligence-adjacent entity—one that conveniently operates in some of the most geopolitically sensitive regions of the world?

Through a network of government ties, revolving-door politics, and highly lucrative contracts, Chemonics has entrenched itself as a dominant force in the aid-industrial complex. It operates not just as a contractor, but as a power broker, influencing policies and securing funding streams that fuel its ever-growing empire.

Yet, for all the billions funneled into its initiatives, there remains a **glaring lack of accountability**. Projects are mismanaged, crucial aid deliveries are delayed, and in some cases, entire initiatives fail—while Chemonics continues to profit.

This investigation pulls back the curtain on Chemonics’ vast influence, its financial entanglements with USAID, and the systemic failures that allow it to thrive despite its checkered history.

The question isn’t just whether Chemonics is effective—it’s whether its business model is built on **aid, or exploitation.**

His experience at USAID gave him **intimate knowledge of how government contracts were awarded**, and his expertise in economic development made him an ideal figure to navigate the murky world of international finance and policy.

When Teele founded Chemonics, he was **not building a humanitarian organization**—he was designing a private-sector entity that would secure government money under the pretense of aid. For **27 years**, he remained at the helm, directing Chemonics as it **transformed into a multi-billion-dollar powerhouse**.

However, Murphy was a businessman first, a strategist second, and—perhaps most importantly—a **man who understood how to manipulate the system**. His corporate empire, **Erly Industries**, was a major player in global food trade, and he used Chemonics as a vehicle to expand his influence.

One of his [most notorious maneuvers](https://merip.org/1994/01/chemonics-revisited/) came in the **late 1980s**, when he orchestrated a USAID-backed loan for a **rice processing plant in Jordan**. The **$4 million loan** was granted under the assumption that it would help local Jordanian firms process U.S. rice imports. Instead, Murphy used the deal to **lock out competitors** and funnel rice directly into the Iraqi market under Saddam Hussein’s regime.

By **1990**, these deals accounted for nearly **one-third of Erly’s total revenue**. When the **Gulf War** and subsequent U.S. embargo on Iraq destroyed this revenue stream, Erly Industries spiraled into financial ruin. But one division of Murphy’s empire remained profitable: **Chemonics**.

### **The Pivot: Chemonics Goes Independent**

By the **mid-1990s**, Erly Industries was in decline, weighed down by debts and scandals. But Chemonics had been **one of its few consistently profitable divisions**, thanks to **its unique ability to secure USAID contracts**. Seeing the writing on the wall, Murphy and Teele orchestrated a **strategic spinoff**:

- **1995:** Chemonics officially separates from Erly Industries, becoming an independent firm
- **Maintains its government connections**, continuing to dominate USAID contracts
- By 1995, it had completed projects in **92 countries**, working across sectors like agriculture, economic development, and infrastructure

This **independence was largely symbolic**. Though Chemonics was no longer legally tied to Murphy’s collapsing empire, it **retained the same playbook**: securing **massive government contracts**, keeping overhead high, and ensuring **a steady flow of taxpayer money into its operations**.

## A System Built on Connections, Not Merit

From the very beginning, Chemonics’ success was **not built on innovation or humanitarian expertise**—it was built on:

1. **Government Ties** – Teele’s deep connections within USAID gave Chemonics an inside track on contract awards
2. **Political Favoritism** – The company’s proximity to Washington D.C. made it a go-to contractor for foreign aid, regardless of performance
3. **Corporate Influence** – Murphy’s ability to manipulate USAID programs to benefit his personal business interests set a precedent for how Chemonics would operate

One of co-founder Gerald Murphy’s **[most audacious schemes](https://merip.org/1994/01/chemonics-revisited/)** played out in the late 1980s, when he orchestrated a USAID-backed deal that **funneled millions in U.S. taxpayer dollars into his own company’s pockets.**

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!Tq__!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Ffedcdfee-57b3-42eb-b449-4d8d89a331fa_2048x2728.webp)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!Tq__!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Ffedcdfee-57b3-42eb-b449-4d8d89a331fa_2048x2728.webp)

Comet Rice, a subsidiary of Erly.

Here’s how it worked:

1. Murphy arranged for a group of well-connected **Jordanians** (under the name **Amman Resources**) to **secure a $4 million USAID loan** to build a rice processing plant in Aqaba, Jordan.
2. USAID, believing it was **supporting a local Jordanian business**, approved the funding—**without realizing the entire deal was orchestrated by Murphy.**
3. Once built, the **plant refused to process rice from any other U.S. suppliers**—instead, it exclusively handled rice from **Comet**, one of Murphy’s subsidiaries under **Erly Industries.**
4. This maneuver **allowed Comet to corner 80% of the Iraqi rice import market**, with **U.S. taxpayers unknowingly subsidizing Murphy’s corporate dominance.**

By **1990**, these shady deals had made Murphy’s rice empire **one of the largest U.S. exporters to Iraq**, supplying **millions of dollars in food commodities directly to Saddam Hussein’s regime.** At this time, Comet’s sales to Iraq accounted for **almost one third** of Erly’s total revenues.

But then, disaster struck.

When the **Gulf War erupted in 1991**, the **U.S. imposed trade sanctions on Iraq**. Overnight, Murphy lost a **$130 million market**, and **Erly Industries began hemorrhaging money.**

Between **1992 and 1993**, the company reported losses of **$21.1 million**, with sales plummeting from **$218 million to $169 million.** Murphy had gambled on U.S. foreign policy remaining stable—and he had lost.

**But Chemonics did not lose.**

Unlike Erly’s rice business, Chemonics **continued receiving USAID contracts, shielding it from financial collapse.**

By the mid-1990s, as Erly Industries struggled, **Chemonics was thriving**—a clear sign that Murphy’s real cash cow wasn’t rice, but **government contracts.**

Even after Murphy’s **death in 2020**, Chemonics remains **one of the most powerful Beltway contractors**, still receiving billions in taxpayer-funded aid projects.

But as history shows, **these connections come at a cost**—one that has left a **trail of mismanaged projects, failed aid programs, and unanswered questions about where all the money is really going**.

- **What makes this company so politically untouchable?**

### **The Mechanics of a Beltway Bandit**

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!V9WP!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Ff96bab50-38ea-4345-aa28-8015a10d45d2_610x227.webp)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!V9WP!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Ff96bab50-38ea-4345-aa28-8015a10d45d2_610x227.webp)

A **[Beltway Bandit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beltway_bandit)** operates in a predictable cycle:

1. **Secure lucrative government contracts** through lobbying and insider influence
2. **Perform just well enough** to maintain relationships and avoid scrutiny
3. **Expand operations globally**, not necessarily to improve effectiveness, but to justify more funding
4. **Exploit government inefficiencies** and bureaucratic loopholes to maintain dominance

Chemonics fits this mold perfectly. It **does not generate revenue through sales or private investment**—its entire business model depends on **winning USAID contracts, no matter the cost**.

And part of the way to win that is simple - *keep it vague*.

Chemonics’ list of services reads more like a corporate buzzword generator than a clear mission statement. [According to](https://www.devex.com/news/top-usaid-contractors-for-2015-88181) **[Devex](https://www.devex.com/news/top-usaid-contractors-for-2015-88181)**, the company specializes in:

- **“Capacity Building”**
- **“Communications”**
- **“Corporate Social Responsibility”**
- **“Knowledge management”**
- **“Performance management and appraisal”**
- **“Program design services”**

But what do these vague phrases actually mean in practice?

The answer: **whatever Chemonics needs them to mean** to justify continued government funding.

By keeping its services **intentionally broad**, Chemonics can **pivot to whatever projects are most profitable at the time**—whether it’s economic development, disaster relief, healthcare, or governance reform. The company’s **ability to adapt its rhetoric, rather than its actual performance, is a key reason why it remains USAID’s top contractor.**

## Case Studies in Corruption

### **Securing Contracts in Conflict Zones & Disaster Areas**

Chemonics has mastered the art of **profiting from instability**. The company has received massive funding for projects in countries recovering from war, natural disasters, and political upheaval, including:

- **Haiti (post-earthquake reconstruction)**
- **Syria (funding White Helmets during the fall of Assad)**
- **Afghanistan (economic development initiatives pre- and post-Taliban takeover)**

While these projects are often framed as **“humanitarian aid,”** they serve another purpose: **keeping the USAID money flowing into Chemonics’ accounts.**

This pattern repeats itself in crisis after crisis: **USAID grants Chemonics massive contracts, the projects underdeliver, and yet the funding never stops.**

Most businesses suffer when they fail to deliver results. **Chemonics, on the other hand, profits from its own inefficiency.**

- **Failed projects lead to more contracts** (to “fix” the very problems Chemonics helped create, in some cases)
- **Overspending means higher budgets in future funding cycles**
- **Accountability is virtually nonexistent**, as USAID continues awarding contracts regardless of past performance

Even when **[whistleblowers](https://www.scworld.com/news/chemonics-discloses-months-long-breach-affecting-263k-people) and [watchdog groups](https://www.thebureauinvestigates.com/stories/2023-11-09/a-disaster-waiting-to-happen-how-usaids-10bn-health-project-unravelled) expose mismanagement**, the worst consequence Chemonics faces is **a minor financial settlement**—like the **$3.1 million** it paid in **2024** for **fraudulent billing practices.**

Compare that to the **billions in contracts it has received**, and it becomes clear: **penalties are merely the cost of doing business.**

By **2017**, it was clear the program was failing. Yet, despite this, **Chemonics continued receiving funding**, further proving that **performance is irrelevant when a company is this deeply entrenched in Washington’s foreign aid network.**

### **The Haiti Connection: A Case Study in Profiteering**

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!Zojr!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F4d01649d-c5c7-4bac-95a7-5d7f2b1e3cf1_768x516.jpeg)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!Zojr!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F4d01649d-c5c7-4bac-95a7-5d7f2b1e3cf1_768x516.jpeg)

THONY BELIZAIRE/AFP/Getty Images /Social Sharing

After the **2010 Haiti earthquake**, the U.S. government pledged **billions in aid**, much of which flowed through USAID. **Chemonics was one of the biggest recipients**, receiving [hundreds of millions](https://thegroundtruthproject.org/usaid-contractor-chemonics-cited-for-numerous-mistakes-in-haiti/) for reconstruction projects.

**What Went Wrong?**

- **Predominance of U.S. Firms**: [Investigations revealed](https://cepr.net/publications/usaid-otis-politicized-problematic-cash-for-work-programs/) that a [considerable share](https://cepr.net/publications/haiti-by-the-numbers-ten-years-later/) of USAID's reconstruction funds was [channeled to U.S. companies](https://www.thenation.com/article/archive/usaid-helping-haiti-recover-or-us-contractors-make-millions/). For instance, a report by the **Center for Economic and Policy Research (CEPR)** noted that out of every $100 of U.S. contracts paid out for Haiti's rebuilding, Haitian firms received only $1.60.
