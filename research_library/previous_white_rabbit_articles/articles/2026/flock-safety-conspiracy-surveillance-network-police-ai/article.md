# Flock Safety: America Outsourced the Surveillance State

[![](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/7c5d329e-5a73-4ba0-81a9-961a4a3e3979_1672x941.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!mea4!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F7c5d329e-5a73-4ba0-81a9-961a4a3e3979_1672x941.png)

After 9/11, Congress passed the **[USA PATRIOT Act](https://www.justice.gov/archive/ag/speeches/2003/090903nycremarks.htm)**.

Whatever you think of that law, at least the expansion of federal surveillance power happened in public: there was legislation, a vote, hearings, lawsuits, amendments, and years of argument over where national security ended and privacy began.

The PATRIOT Act expanded access to records and electronic surveillance, including roving wiretaps. **[Section 215 later became the legal basis for the NSA’s bulk telephony metadata program](https://www.justice.gov/archives/opa/pr/joint-statement-department-justice-and-office-director-national-intelligence-declassification)**, collecting records showing who called whom, when, and for how long.

**That fight never really ended.**

But while Americans were arguing about who could see their phone records, something else happened outside the Capitol.

**The surveillance state went outside.**

[![](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/2899875b-af41-4dc4-b246-5facedfa6fd6_1672x941.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!bGq6!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F2899875b-af41-4dc4-b246-5facedfa6fd6_1672x941.png)

## THE PATRIOT ACT HAD A VOTE. THIS DIDN’T.

There is no **Flock Act**.

Congress never held a national vote asking whether police departments, neighborhoods, businesses, private donors, and technology companies should collectively build a searchable map of where American vehicles travel.

Instead, the network arrived **one contract at a time.**

- A homeowners association buys a few cameras.
- A police department installs fifty more.
- A shopping center joins.
- Another town signs up.
- Cameras appear at entrances, intersections, highways, schools, apartment complexes, churches, and businesses.

Then those observations become searchable.

That distinction is the premise of this investigation:

**America didn’t vote for a national surveillance network.**

**It outsourced one.**

The government does not necessarily need to own the camera, manufacture the software, or maintain one giant federal database. If private companies build the sensors, local agencies supply the coverage, commercial databases supply identity, and federal agencies can access or combine the information when needed, **the end result can begin to resemble a national system anyway.**

*And the best way to understand why that matters is not through Peter Thiel, venture capital, or some giant relationship chart.*

It’s through a woman named **[Chrisanna Elser](https://denverite.com/2025/10/27/bow-mar-flock-cameras-accusation/)**.

## THE DAY A CAMERA MADE AN INNOCENT WOMAN A SUSPECT

In September 2025, a police officer showed up at Elser’s home in Colorado holding Flock images of her truck.

A package had been stolen from a porch in the tiny town of Bow Mar. Elser’s truck had passed through town around the time of the theft.

That was enough to put her in the frame.

The officer told her:

“*[Nothing gets in or out of the town without us knowing about it.](https://www.404media.co/cop-used-flock-to-wrongfully-accuse-a-woman-then-refused-to-look-at-evidence-that-exonerated-her-body-camera-shows/)*”

**He was not exaggerating about the system’s reach.** Flock cameras had logged her truck entering and leaving town and showed that she had driven through the neighborhood before.

*The problem?*

**She didn’t steal the package.**

**And Elser had her own video.**

Her truck had dash-camera footage. She had GPS records. A neighbor’s doorbell camera helped establish where she actually was. She spent weeks trying to get police to look at evidence that contradicted the story produced by the surveillance system.

**Eventually, the allegation was dropped.**

Julian Dorey highlighted the case [on his podcast](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DLEekW0roNE) and landed on the part that matters most: **the technology had effectively flipped the burden from “prove she did it” to “prove the cameras misunderstood what she was doing.”**

That’s a very different relationship between citizen and state.

And it exposes the real danger of mass surveillance better than any hypothetical ever could.

**Surveillance doesn’t just record facts.   
It can create a story out of facts.**

The truck was there.

The package disappeared.

The software could place the truck inside the town.

The story sounded airtight.

**It was also wrong.**

## FLOCK IS NOT REALLY A CAMERA COMPANY ANYMORE

If Flock were merely selling cameras that photographed license plates, this article would be much shorter.

Today the company operates more than **[120,000 cameras nationwide, capturing roughly 20 billion plate scans every month](https://www.wsj.com/tech/flock-cameras-are-spreading-across-america-the-backlash-is-growing-just-as-fast-fff74ed2)**. Those scans can include the plate number, location, date, time, and vehicle characteristics.

One scan is almost meaningless.

Thousands are not.

### From a plate to a pattern of life

A camera sees your vehicle outside a hospital.

Another catches it near your office.

Another sees it at a church.

Another records it near a protest.

Another sees the same second car parked near yours three nights in a row.

No camera has to know your name to **start building a pattern.**

**Then the databases arrive.**

A plate can be connected to registration information. Registration can become a person. A person can become an address. An address can become relatives, associates, police records, commercial identity data, and other information.

*Sounds kind of like [the Phoenix Program](https://thewhiterabbitreport.substack.com/p/phoenix-program-template-for-endless-wars-operation-gladio?utm_source=flock-camera-article)…*

This is why the comparison to Google is useful.

**Google made the internet searchable.   
Flock is helping make physical movement searchable.**

### The search no longer needs a license plate

Police have already used **[Flock FreeForm](https://www.404media.co/how-cops-use-flock-to-track-people-not-cars/)** to search for people using physical descriptions rather than known plates. Clothing, tattoos, body type, and other attributes can become the starting point.

Then there is **OS Investigate**.

By August 2026, [Flock’s developing OS Investigate](https://www.wired.com/story/flock-safety-os-investigate/) system could reach **plate scans**, **police case files**, **911 dispatch logs**, **ballistics information**, and **commercial identity data**. It also included searches built around patterns: vehicles repeatedly visiting a place, vehicles appearing together, or possible associates inferred from movement.

Old surveillance asks:

**Where did this known suspect go?**

Behavioral surveillance asks:

**Who moved like the person we’re looking for?**

That is a much bigger leap than better cameras.

It is the beginning of a **search engine for real life**.

## EVEN FLOCK’S CEO DESCRIBES THE NEXT STEP

One of the most revealing descriptions came from Flock CEO **[Garrett Langley](https://www.flocksafety.com/blog/flock-safety-ceo-garrett-langley-ted2026)** himself.

[![Garrett Langley](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/44303175-6dca-4714-8ac6-2bd7e1f811e9_7443x4964.jpeg "Garrett Langley")](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!Lxd0!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F44303175-6dca-4714-8ac6-2bd7e1f811e9_7443x4964.jpeg)

38 year old founder, Garrett Langley

[In a long interview about Flock’s expansion](https://cheekypint.transistor.fm/26/transcript) into corporate security, Langley explained that businesses can connect HR systems to Flock so that **when an employee is terminated, the employee can be added automatically to a localized hot list**. If the former employee’s vehicle later returns to the property, security can be alerted.

You can understand the safety argument **immediately**.

A fired employee makes threats.

The company wants warning if he returns.

Fine.

But then ask the next question.

*What happens when a corporate security tool that knows your vehicle is connected to a wider network that knows where that vehicle appears?*

The important word in the previous sentence isn’t “camera.”

It’s **network**.

Julian Dorey made the same point when discussing Langley’s example: the problem is not hard to imagine at the company’s front gate. The question is where that power stops once tracking becomes **cheap**, **automated**, and **normalized**.

**That is the recurring pattern in this story and has been the pattern in many other stories.**

A use case starts narrow.

The infrastructure **does not stay narrow**.

## THE RIVALRY IS REAL. THE CHOICE MAY NOT BE.

Now we can finally talk about **[Axon Enterprise](https://www.axon.com/)**.

This is where the story gets more interesting than “Flock has some rich investors.”

Axon is the company behind **Tasers** and one of the **largest police body-camera** **and** **digital-evidence ecosystems** in the country. Increasingly, it also sells real-time operations software, drones, AI tools, vehicle intelligence, records systems, 911 technology, and fixed license-plate readers.

In other words, Axon is building toward the same basic destination from a different starting point.

### Axon used to own a piece of Flock

Before the rivalry, Axon invested in Flock.

In March 2020, Axon made a **[$4.7 million minority investment in Flock](https://investor.axon.com/image/AXON-2020-Annual-Report.pdf)**, followed by another **$2.1 million investment** later that year. The companies also entered a commercial partnership.

Then the relationship deteriorated.

Axon moved into fixed ALPR cameras with **[Outpost and Lightpost](https://www.axon.com/newsroom/press-releases/axon-announces-new-fixed-ALPR-camera-solutions-and-next-gen-AI-advancements-to-expand-real-time-public-safety-ecosystem)**. Its own [SEC filings now identify Flock as a competitor in fixed license-plate recognition and FlockOS as a competitor to Axon Fusus](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1069183/000162828026011360/axon-20251231.htm).

So yes:

**Flock and Axon genuinely compete.**

But that’s not the interesting part.

**Why should everyone’s movements be persistently searchable in the first place?**

The competition is real.

**The choice offered to citizens may be much smaller than it looks.**

## AXON IS CONNECTING THE CAMERAS YOU ALREADY OWN

Flock mostly makes people think about cameras beside roads.

Axon expands the picture.

In 2025, Axon told shareholders that its partnerships were laying the foundation for a **“[connected public-private safety network.](https://investor.axon.com/2025-05-07-Axon-reports-Q1-2025-revenue-of-604-million%2C-up-31-year-over-year)”**

That phrase deserves to sit on the page by itself.

Because “public-private” means the sensor network doesn’t have to stop with government cameras.

Through **[Axon Community Request](https://www.axon.com/products/community-request)**, police can send area-based requests to nearby **[Ring](https://www.axon.com/products/community-request/ring)** users.

**YES.**

Residents can **voluntarily choose** to share relevant video, and the footage goes **directly into Axon Evidence** with chain-of-custody information.

*Zoom out.*

**The doorbell camera** you bought to see who stole an Amazon package can now become an **evidence node inside a police platform**.

Road camera.

Body camera.

Private doorbell.

911 call.

Evidence database.

Drone.

The boundaries between those systems are getting thinner.

**Big Brother doesn’t need to own every camera   
if enough cameras know how to talk to the same software.**

## NOW FOLLOW THE MONEY

This is where the investor section finally matters.

Not because rich people investing in technology is suspicious.

Because now we know what is being financed.

### **Andreessen Horowitz** understood the network effect

**Andreessen Horowitz**, or **[a16z](https://a16z.com/)**, has been one of Flock’s major backers.

The firm’s own explanation of the investment contains the key line:

“*[The power of Flock Safety is in its network.](https://a16z.com/announcement/investing-in-flock-safety/)*”

**Exactly.**

One camera is a camera.

A hundred thousand cameras connected across thousands of jurisdictions become infrastructure.

A16z’s broader **American Dynamism** thesis is equally revealing. The firm openly champions private technology companies tackling functions once associated with government, pointing to Palantir in **intelligence**, Anduril in **defense**, and Flock in **public safety**.

Its conclusion?

“*[Washington and Silicon Valley are going to have to become friends.](https://a16z.com/building-american-dynamism/)*”

**He literally said that.**

### **Founders Fund** owns pieces of multiple layers

### **Founders Fund** is not a defense-tech fund. That’s what makes this interesting.

Then there is **[Founders Fund](https://foundersfund.com/portfolio/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)**.

If its portfolio were just Flock, Palantir, and Anduril, you could shrug and say: *Okay, **Peter Thiel’s people** like government-tech companies.*

But that’s not what Founders Fund is.

Its investments have stretched across some of the most important companies in modern life: **SpaceX, Facebook, Airbnb, Stripe, Neuralink, OpenAI, Spotify, Affirm, Polymarket, Oculus, Credit Karma, Lyft, Postmates**—and plenty more.

Think about the categories represented there.

**Social identity. Payments. Credit. Transportation. Housing. Artificial intelligence. Space infrastructure. Brain-computer interfaces. Political prediction markets. Consumer behavior.**

And then, sitting in the same investment universe:

**Flock. Palantir. Anduril.**

*Now it gets interesting.*

Don’t think about those three as company names. Think about what they actually do.

**Flock** watches movement through the physical world.

**Anduril** builds autonomous sensing and command systems for borders, battlefields, and national security.

**Palantir** takes huge piles of disconnected information and turns them into something an analyst can actually search, map, and act on.

Put simply:

**sense → connect → analyze → act.**

That does **not** mean Founders Fund owns one giant surveillance machine. It doesn’t mean Facebook data is secretly being piped into Flock, or that Stripe and Palantir share some hidden back-end database.

*It doesn’t mean they **DON’T** do that either…*

The point is much more interesting.

**The capital financing America’s surveillance infrastructure is not sitting in some isolated defense-sector corner. It sits inside the same investment ecosystem that helped finance the platforms Americans use to communicate, travel, pay, borrow, work, socialize, and increasingly interact with AI.**

And then look at the people moving through that ecosystem.

**[Trae Stephens](https://foundersfund.com/team/trae-stephens/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)** worked inside the **U.S. Intelligence Community**, moved to **Palantir**, became a partner at **Founders Fund**, and co-founded **Anduril**.

That’s almost the entire story in one résumé:

**intelligence → data fusion → venture capital → autonomous surveillance and defense.**

Now add Flock to the portfolio.

*Still think we’re just talking about some roadside cameras?*

The more revealing pattern is that **Silicon Valley, consumer technology, finance, intelligence, defense, and public safety are increasingly being funded—and populated—by overlapping networks of people and capital.**

The lines between those worlds are getting harder to see.

And that matters enormously when the product being built is a system designed to make **physical life searchable**.

## THE CAMERAS CAN ARRIVE WITHOUT THE TAXPAYER BUYING THEM

Las Vegas gives us a near-perfect example of the outsourced-surveillance premise.

The **[Horowitz Family Foundation](https://projects.propublica.org/nonprofits/organizations/357050910)**, connected to a16z cofounder **[Ben Horowitz](https://a16z.com/author/ben-horowitz/)** and his wife Felicia, donated **[almost $1.9 million for Flock license plate readers and another $2.47 million for supporting software](https://thenevadaindependent.com/article/vegas-police-are-big-users-of-license-plate-readers-public-has-little-input-because-its-a-gift)** used by Las Vegas Metropolitan Police.

The money flowed through the private **[LVMPD Foundation](https://lvmpdfoundation.org/about-us/)**.

That matters because the cameras were gifts.

**[The Nevada Independent](https://thenevadaindependent.com/article/vegas-police-are-big-users-of-license-plate-readers-public-has-little-input-because-its-a-gift)** found that the arrangement reduced the normal opportunities for residents to weigh in through the public-meeting process that accompanies ordinary taxpayer purchases.

Read the chain:

- **venture-capital fortune ↓**
- **family foundation ↓**
- **private police foundation ↓**
- **Flock surveillance equipment ↓**
- **local police**

No federal law.

No national referendum.

No “National Vehicle Surveillance Act.”

Just infrastructure.

**This is what outsourcing looks like.**

## THE FEDERAL GOVERNMENT DOESN’T HAVE TO OWN THE CAMERA

Now return to the premise.

If private companies build the network,   
what happens when federal agencies want access?

Flock has acknowledged limited pilots with **[Customs and Border Protection](https://www.flocksafety.com/blog/ensuring-local-compliance)** and **Homeland Security Investigations** focused on **human trafficking and fentanyl distribution**. Its [historical federal pilot list also includes the FBI, ATF, NCIS, and National Park Service](https://www.flocksafety.com/blog/does-flock-share-data-with-ice).

A distributed network **creates pathways.**

- Local departments can establish **sharing relationships** (*like with Ring cameras*).
- Federal officers can work **through local agencies**.
- So the data can move between systems without Washington owning every camera.

A January 2026 Senate letter described **DHS surveillance procurement** as a **[“muddled patchwork”](https://www.warner.senate.gov/public/_cache/files/9/9/99fdb231-e6eb-4a85-8700-c16c9cea69a6/B2B57708CB0278A83937247C8498A48B2ABBD1E4D6FED6B95973D47541321C0D.2026.01.29-letter-to-dhs-oig-re-data.pdf)** involving tools from companies including Palantir, Clearview AI, PenLink, Paragon, and Flock-related access.

That phrase may be more important than “master database.”

A patchwork can still cover the whole quilt.

### Palantir is already inside HSI

Flock says it does not work with Palantir directly.

It doesn’t need to for the broader point to matter.

## THE REAL POWER IS DECIDING WHO GETS SEARCHED

This is where surveillance arguments often go wrong.

The biggest danger is not necessarily that every person gets arrested.

It’s that everyone can become **searchable** while only some people become **targets**.

We have already seen what that looks like.

A Texas deputy ran a nationwide Flock search connected to [a woman who had self-managed an abortion](https://www.404media.co/a-texas-cop-searched-license-plate-cameras-nationwide-for-a-woman-who-got-an-abortion/). [Subsequent court records showed the investigation went beyond a simple welfare check](https://www.404media.co/police-said-they-surveilled-woman-who-had-an-abortion-for-her-safety-court-records-show-they-considered-charging-her-with-a-crime/).

Police officers have been accused of using license-plate readers to monitor wives, girlfriends, ex-partners, and other people for personal reasons.

By August 2026, **[at least 69 police officials had been accused, charged, or convicted of misusing Flock or other license-plate reader systems](https://www.washingtonpost.com/technology/2026/08/19/we-found-cops-who-misused-flock-their-police-departments-didnt-know/)**.

Readers familiar with **[The International Syndicate](https://thewhiterabbitreport.substack.com/p/international-syndicate-cia-mi6-nato-global-control-gladio)** will recognize why that question matters.

If a durable **intelligence-finance-security network** exists above changing administrations, a system that makes physical movement searchable would be an incredibly valuable tool.

## FAQ

### Is Flock Safety a government surveillance program?

Flock is a private venture-backed company, not a proven federal front. But police departments use its cameras at large scale, federal agencies have conducted pilots, and local-to-federal sharing relationships can give government investigators access to privately built infrastructure. The more useful distinction is not private versus government—it is **who can access the data and under what rules**.

### Are Flock and Axon secretly working together?

They are currently competitors. Historically, Axon invested in Flock and partnered with the company before moving into the same fixed-ALPR market. The “kayfabe” argument is not that their rivalry is fake; it is that switching from one vendor to the other can preserve the same surveillance capability.

### Does Flock work with Palantir?

Flock says it does not work with Palantir and does not share customer data with Palantir. The connection discussed here is structural: Flock and Palantir sit in overlapping investment and government ecosystems, while DHS can use multiple contractors for different parts of an investigation.

### What is the Israeli intelligence connection?

There is no documented evidence that Israeli intelligence controls Flock. The surrounding ecosystem includes Israeli-founded Carbyne, former Israeli prime minister Ehud Barak, Barak-cofounded Toka, American venture capital in Israeli security companies, and Palantir’s strategic partnership with Israel’s Ministry of Defense. Those are documented connections; a centralized Israeli command structure is not.

### Could this technology be used to control trafficking networks?

It can unquestionably help map them. Vehicle movement, identities, locations, associates, police records, and other data can reveal how an illicit network operates. What remains speculative is the claim that a hidden organization deliberately keeps trafficking networks alive in order to manage them. **The capability is documented; the intent is the unresolved question.**
