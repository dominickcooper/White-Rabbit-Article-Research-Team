# THE PHOENIX PROGRAM: THE TEMPLATE FOR ENDLESS WARS

[![](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/52dcb9d7-3439-4064-b45f-214ad7b27e29_1536x1024.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!5uit!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F52dcb9d7-3439-4064-b45f-214ad7b27e29_1536x1024.png)

The official history of **[The Phoenix Program](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phoenix_Program)** *usually* begins in 1967.

That was when American officials created the **[Intelligence Coordination and Exploitation Program](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1964-68v06/d296)**, better known as **ICEX**. Its purpose was to gather the scattered intelligence being collected by the [CIA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_Intelligence_Agency), the U.S. military, South Vietnamese police, local militias, and other government agencies—and feed it into one coordinated attack against the [Viet Cong’s](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Viet_Cong) underground organization.

In December 1967, the South Vietnamese government established its own companion program, **[Phụng Hoàng](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phoenix_Program)**, named after the mythical phoenix. President [Nguyễn Văn Thiệu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nguy%E1%BB%85n_V%C4%83n_Thi%E1%BB%87u) formally [expanded the program by decree in 1968](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1964-68v06/d296). From there, ICEX and Phung Hoang became part of what Americans commonly called the **Phoenix Program**.

*At least, that is **the familiar timeline.***

It is also incomplete.

**Phoenix** did not spring fully formed from the jungles of Vietnam. The men who built it did not invent political blacklists, secret police files, covert action teams, paramilitary proxies, interrogation centers, or intelligence-driven raids in 1967.

Those tools were already waiting.

They had been tested in Europe. Refined in Asia. Hidden inside military missions, police programs, universities, aid organizations, and allied governments.

Vietnam was where *someone* finally tied them together.

**Phoenix was new as an organizational chart.   
But it was not new as an idea.**

### **THE GLADIO PROTOCOL**

[![Operation Gladio Timeline](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/c54f8941-b983-451b-923a-7de6381b51ff_2172x724.png "Operation Gladio Timeline")](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!uEuj!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fc54f8941-b983-451b-923a-7de6381b51ff_2172x724.png)

To understand where that idea came from, we have to go back to Europe after World War II.

Western intelligence agencies feared that Soviet armies might invade Western Europe or that communist parties might gain power from within. In response, the CIA, British intelligence, NATO governments, and local security services helped establish secret **[stay-behind networks](https://phpisn.ethz.ch/lory1.ethz.ch/collections/coll_gladio/chronology76c1.html?navinfo=15301)** across the continent.

You can find out more on that here:

**Their stated mission** was simple.

Should Soviet troops occupy a country, **hidden cells** would remain behind enemy lines. They would gather intelligence, sabotage supply routes, maintain communications, guide resistance groups, and prepare for guerrilla warfare.

These **hidden cells** were referred to as **stay-behind units**.

In Italy, this network carried [the codename](https://thewhiterabbitreport.substack.com/p/operation-gladio-natos-covert-war) **[Gladio](https://thewhiterabbitreport.substack.com/p/operation-gladio-natos-covert-war)**—Latin for “sword.” The name later became shorthand for the wider family of European stay-behind organizations, though each country’s network had its own structure, history, and degree of American involvement.

While there is no released document showing that **the Phoenix Program** answered to a Gladio headquarters or formed part of the same formal chain of command, the **underlying method** and pattern **were identical**.

- Build secret local networks.
- Recruit politically dependable allies.
- Train them to operate when normal government authority breaks down.
- Use local forces so the American hand remains one step removed.
- Hide covert work inside military commands, police departments, political organizations, businesses, aid programs, or other respectable institutions.
- Treat communism not merely as a foreign army, but as an infection that might spread through elections, labor unions, churches, universities, newspapers, villages, and ordinary civic life.

*And above all?*

**Divide responsibility.**

- One office **supplies** the money.
- Another **gathers** the names.
- A local service **conducts** the arrest.
- A paramilitary team **carries** **out** the raid.
- An allied government **records** the death.

**Everyone plays a role.**

Yet when the bodies appear, **no one** seems to bear the responsibility.

That was the deeper **Gladio principle**. Not simply a hidden army waiting for Soviet tanks, but a **model of** **covert warfare** built upon networks, proxies, compartmentalization, and deniability.

And one man provides a direct bridge between that world and Vietnam.

### **WILLIAM COLBY: FROM STAY-BEHIND NETWORKS TO PHOENIX**

[![William Coby | Biography & Facts | Britannica](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/24cec6a4-43de-4854-8cef-2449a4b37c8b_655x468.webp "William Coby | Biography & Facts | Britannica")](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!t-Ue!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F24cec6a4-43de-4854-8cef-2449a4b37c8b_655x468.webp)

William Colby

Before **[William Colby](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Colby)** became director of the CIA, he built his career in the underground.

**During World War II**, Colby [parachuted behind enemy lines](https://www.cia.gov/resources/csi/studies-in-intelligence/archives/vol-43-no-3/skis-and-daggers/) in France and Norway as an officer in the **[Office of Strategic Services](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Office_of_Strategic_Services)**. [His teams](https://www.cia.gov/resources/csi/studies-in-intelligence/archives/vol-43-no-3/skis-and-daggers/) worked with local resistance forces, conducted sabotage, gathered intelligence, and disrupted German movements.

When Colby [joined the CIA in 1950](https://time.com/archive/6716527/europe-natos-secret-armies/), one of his early assignments involved helping develop **stay-behind capabilities in Scandinavia**. Colby later acknowledged his role in organizing networks that could survive a Soviet invasion and operate from behind occupied territory.

**After Scandinavia came Italy.**

There, Colby participated in American covert political efforts aimed at preventing the Italian Communist Party from gaining power. The battlefield was not a jungle or a conventional front line. It was Italian political life itself—**elections**, **parties**, **unions**, **newspapers**, **civic groups**, and **the** **institutions** that shaped public opinion.

**Then came Vietnam.**

Colby became **chief of the CIA station in Saigon** and later headed the American pacification program known as **[CORDS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civil_Operations_and_Rural_Development_Support)**, placing him near the center of the system that oversaw Phoenix.

**His career** connected **European stay-behind warfare** to the political and intelligence war in Vietnam. But his story did not end with Phoenix. Years later, [his name would surface](https://archive.org/details/operationgladiou0000will/mode/2up?q=phoenix) beside an **Australian bank** surrounded by former intelligence officers, missing records, money-laundering allegations, and **suspicions of covert finance**.

The geography changed.

The basic question did not:

**How do you find and destroy an enemy that hides inside ordinary society?**

### **WHEN CIVILIANS BECAME “INFRASTRUCTURE”**

[![Who Were the Viet Cong?](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/4dbc4778-ae84-4983-b144-bb27f8fef64a_1500x895.jpeg "Who Were the Viet Cong?")](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!9cFr!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F4dbc4778-ae84-4983-b144-bb27f8fef64a_1500x895.jpeg)

Credit: Three Lions/Stringer/Getty Images

Now, the **[Viet Cong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Viet_Cong)** was not merely an army.

Its armed units relied upon **a political and logistical network** spread through South Vietnamese society. This network recruited new members, collected taxes, moved weapons, carried messages, gathered intelligence, arranged food and shelter, enforced party decisions, and warned guerrillas when government troops approached.

American officials called it the **Viet Cong Infrastructure**, or **VCI**.

That label changed the boundaries of the war.

**A soldier carrying a rifle was an obvious military target.**

*But what about **the farmer** who supplied him with rice?*   
***The teenage** messenger who carried a note?   
**The teacher** accused of spreading communist ideas?   
**The** **village official** suspected of collecting taxes for the Viet Cong?*

What about **the wife** who knew where her husband slept?

**The cousin** who gave him shelter?

**The neighbor** who said nothing when police came asking questions?

Once an **insurgency** was **defined as a network**, almost **anyone** connected to that network could become part of the battlefield.

And once civilians became **infrastructure**, the normal line between **combatant and noncombatant** began to collapse.

Interrogation became a way to generate new names.

A name became a dossier.  
**A dossier became a blacklist.**

And a **blacklist** could become an **arrest**, a **prison sentence**, a **midnight raid**—or a death reported under the deliberately bloodless word **“neutralized.”**

**This was the real beginning of Phoenix.**

Phoenix began when **governments learned to map political loyalty** as though it were **an enemy** order of battle—and to treat the civilian population as the hidden structure holding that enemy together.

Vietnam, in turn, would turn that principle **into a national system.**

*But…*

This system had **already** appeared elsewhere.

Years before American officials spoke openly of “neutralizing” the VCI, another **U.S.-backed government** had compiled political blacklists, empowered anti-communist police and paramilitaries, and targeted whole communities accused of supporting an underground enemy.

**That story began in Korea.**

Years earlier, Korea had shown how an **anti-communist government** could treat whole communities as part of an enemy network—and how a list of suspected political loyalties could become something close to a death sentence.

### **JEJU AND THE WAR AGAINST THE POPULATION**

[![](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/b5af0e2c-d9db-4f27-ae58-9101a42cc9e3_3336x1321.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!wUDj!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fb5af0e2c-d9db-4f27-ae58-9101a42cc9e3_3336x1321.png)

The tiny Jeju Island is roughly 45 miles across (east to west) and 25 miles from north to south

When Japan surrendered in 1945, **Korea** did not immediately regain control of its own future. [The peninsula was divided](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1954_Geneva_Conference) into **Soviet** and **American** occupation zones, with the United States governing the South through the **[United States Army Military Government in Korea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Army_Military_Government_in_Korea)**, or **USAMGIK**.

**[Jeju Island](https://jeju43peace.org/historytruth/fact-truth/)** soon became one of the new order’s **most violent fault lines.**

Tensions exploded after **police** **fired** into a **March 1, 1947** **demonstration**, killing several spectators. Public anger spread into a general strike involving government offices, schools, businesses, transportation services, and even dozens of Jeju police officers.

Rather than treating the unrest as a broad revolt against police violence, **occupation authorities** increasingly viewed the island through an ideological lens. A senior police official claimed that **90 percent of Jeju residents** leaned left, and the island began to acquire a new label:

**“Red Island.”**

*There it was.*  
**The first crucial step.**

A place filled with fishermen, farmers, shopkeepers, teachers, civil servants, and children [had been reclassified](https://www.unesco.org/en/memory-world/revealing-truth-jeju-43-archives) as **a political threat.**

Once that happened, nearly **any action could be read as proof.**

*Refuse to cooperate with the police?*   
**Communist!**

*Join a strike?*   
**Communist!**

*Hide in the mountains to avoid arrest?*   
**Communist!**

*Complain about torture?*   
**Probably a communist.**

The military government sent hundreds of additional police officers to Jeju. Officials who had supported the strike were removed, while members of the far-right **Northwest Youth League** poured onto the island. According to the **Jeju 4·3 Peace Foundation**, the group occupied roles in police, government, and education **while terrorizing residents** under the banner of hunting communists.

These were not **ordinary** police reinforcements either.

They were hardened anti-communist activists, many of them **refugees from the Soviet-controlled North**, who had personal reasons to hate anyone associated with the political left.

Their zeal made them useful.  
**And dangerous.**

Arrests mounted. Detention cells overflowed. **Reports of torture spread**, including several deaths in police custody. By April 1948, members of the Jeju branch of the **Workers’ Party of South Korea** **launched an armed uprising**, attacking police stations and right-wing organizations while opposing elections that would establish a separate government in the South.

The response quickly expanded **beyond the gunmen.**

**More police arrived.**   
More **Northwest Youth League members** followed.   
**Korean military units** followed them.

Of course, the **[Korean military units](https://jeju43peace.org/jeju-4-3-u-s-intervention/)** [were under American oversight](https://jeju43peace.org/jeju-4-3-u-s-intervention/), supported by reconnaissance aircraft and other U.S.-controlled resources. American military officials were embedded within the advisory and command structure surrounding the operations.

Then the island itself **became a blacklist.**

[![Revisited - Nearly 80 years ago, a brutal crackdown on Jeju Island in  southern Korea claimed tens of thousands of lives, as some residents  resisted the country's division after World War II.](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/12fd7b8d-9d6e-481b-9a9d-13cf4aa6d2f8_447x447.jpeg "Revisited - Nearly 80 years ago, a brutal crackdown on Jeju Island in  southern Korea claimed tens of thousands of lives, as some residents  resisted the country's division after World War II.")](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!Pmwk!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F12fd7b8d-9d6e-481b-9a9d-13cf4aa6d2f8_447x447.jpeg)

Authorities declared broad sections of Jeju’s interior off-limits. Anyone found more than **five kilometers from the coastline** could be treated as **an insurgent** and **shot**, regardless of why that person was there. The order effectively classified scores of mountain villages (*and the people living within them*) as part of the **guerrilla support zone.**

Think about what that meant.

**The** **state** **no longer had to prove** that a farmer had carried supplies to a guerrilla unit.

His home was in the wrong place.

**That was enough.**

Villages were emptied. Houses were burned. Livestock was slaughtered so it could not feed insurgents. Residents were forced toward coastal settlements, while **those who remained** in the interior risked being treated as enemy supporters. It was assumed by the U.S. military that residents of remote villages were supplying partisan units.

*Because…you know. They gotta be.*

**That assumption** sounds *remarkably* close to the logic Phoenix would later use in Vietnam.

The guerrilla does not stand alone.

He depends upon food, shelter, warnings, recruits, messengers, relatives, and silence.

Therefore, **the people around him** become part of the target.

Jeju was effectively a **testing ground for Vietnam.**

**Destroy the underground by controlling, relocating, interrogating, and terrorizing the population believed to sustain it.**

### **THE BODO LEAGUE: WHEN THE LIST BECAME THE SENTENCE**

Jeju showed how **geography** could turn a population into a target.

The **National Guidance Alliance**, better known as the **[Bodo League](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bodo_League_massacre)**, showed how **paperwork** could do the same thing.

[South Korean authorities used the organization](https://www.jinsil.go.kr/upload/TRCK%20NewsLetter%20Vol.%2011_1697538546330.pdf) to register people considered **former communists**, **leftists**, **sympathizers**, or **politically unreliable citizens**. In theory, it was a program of guidance and ideological conversion.

***Brainwashing candidates**, if you will.*

But government registration rarely remains neutral during a civil war.

Once your name was in the system, **the file remained.**

Some members may have held genuine communist ties. Others were low-level activists, people who had attended meetings, or [villagers swept into local registration drives](https://www.jinsil.go.kr/upload/TRCK%20NewsLetter%20Vol.%2011_1697538546330.pdf). Later investigations found cases involving ordinary farmers and unarmed civilians whose **recorded membership** became the central evidence used against them.

Then **North Korea** [invaded in](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Korean_War) **[June 1950](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Korean_War).**

South Korean authorities feared that registered leftists might aid the advancing forces, organize behind the lines, or join the enemy.

*So, what do you think they did?*

**Yup.**

The police and military units began **combing through the lists** and rounding up **Bodo League members**, along with any others suspected of possible collaboration.

[![H.MAP](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/f1b15050-addc-454e-96ee-2f4c6954dc66_740x450.jpeg "H.MAP")](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!MRNM!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Ff1b15050-addc-454e-96ee-2f4c6954dc66_740x450.jpeg)

What had been presented as a **register for political re-education** became a ready-made list of **undesirables.**

- **First, compile the names.**
- **Then classify the threat.**
- **Then act before guilt has to be proved.**

**[South Korea’s Truth and Reconciliation Commission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Truth_and_Reconciliation_Commission_(South_Korea))** has since confirmed multiple cases in which unarmed civilians were detained by police, military police, **the Counter Intelligence Corps**, and other security agencies before being **taken away and killed** without trial.

The commission stated plainly that Bodo League members (or people merely *suspected* of aligning with North Korean forces) were [killed by the military and police](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bodo_League_massacre).

[![](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/146ac5e3-782e-4b63-9f2c-fa1fbc18b0bd_500x361.jpeg)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!abn8!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F146ac5e3-782e-4b63-9f2c-fa1fbc18b0bd_500x361.jpeg)

Prisoners lie on the ground before execution by South Korean troops near Daejon, South Korea, July 1950. Photo by U.S. Army Maj. Abbott.

The victims did not have to be caught carrying weapons.

Some were just ordered to report to police stations. *You know, **for questioning.***

**Others were taken** from local detention centers, prisons, warehouses, or temporary holding sites. From there, groups were transported to valleys, mines, coastlines, or remote fields **where they were killed.**

No courts.   
No defense attorneys.   
No judges.

**The file had already answered the question of guilt.**

This is the clearest Korean precursor to **Phoenix’s blacklist system.**

**Both operations:**

- Treated suspected political affiliation as **operational intelligence**.
- Created **categories of civilians** whose names could trigger detention.
- Allowed the **emergency of war** to replace the slower burden of proving individual guilt.

Phoenix, of course, would later make the process **more organized.**

### **THE CIA’S SECRET WAR BEHIND THE LINES**

Jeju and the Bodo League belonged mainly to **the domestic side** of South Korea’s anti-communist war.

At the same time, **American intelligence** was building **a different machine** on the other side of the country.

**The** **CIA and U.S. military intelligence** [recruited local agents](https://www.cia.gov/readingroom/document/01410803), inserted teams into **North Korea**, supported guerrilla operations, collected battlefield intelligence, and developed networks meant to function **behind enemy lines**.

[Declassified CIA material](https://www.cia.gov/readingroom/document/06146212) describes personnel being landed covertly on the North Korean coast, while [an official history of Army human intelligence](https://www.cia.gov/readingroom/docs/DOC_0000872714.pdf) states that **the Far East Command** had built a **large clandestine collection apparatus** by the end of the war.

These missions involved a different target.

**Jeju’s police and paramilitary forces** sought to **control a population** inside territory held by the government.

**The northern networks** sought to penetrate enemy territory.

But they relied upon many of the same tools:

- Trusted local recruits
- Secret communications
- Covert movement
- Compartmentalized command
- And of course, sabotage, reconnaissance, informants, and (*most importantly*) **deniability**.

The North Korean attempts, however, were **a bit of an embarrassment.**

> *“In 1951 and 1952, the Far East Air Force had dropped some **200 sabotage agents** of its own on **19 separate missions** directed against North Korean facilities.*
>
> ***The agents accomplished practically nothing**, and only **one party** ever returned safely to UN lines.*
>
> *Despite these unpromising precedents, the Liaison Detachment’s Guerrilla Division had laid plans in the spring of 1953 to use the 1st PAIR’s “Southwind” element in yet another attempt at mounting sabotage operations. It proposed to parachute in 48 two-man teams to blow up North Korean railroads.*
>
> ***Perhaps fortunately for all concerned**, the mission was never implemented.”*
>
> — [US Army HUMINT in Korean War](https://web.archive.org/web/20220115022205/https://www.cia.gov/static/5f627af58cdf8f8f490cd87d1b0cd4e4/Evolution-US-Army-HUMINT.pdf)

Regardless of that FUBAR, Korea produced **two separate precedents**.

The first was the **domestic model**:

Blacklists. Police records. Political classifications. Paramilitaries. Preventive detention. Collective punishment. Civilian repression.

The second was the **covert model**:

Clandestine cells. Local agents. Guerrilla warfare. Behind-the-lines intelligence. Sabotage. Deniable operations.

Phoenix would later **join those worlds.**

It would take the **covert network-building methods** used against an external enemy and **combine them** with **the internal-security machinery** used to identify, classify, interrogate, and remove suspected civilians.

That was the leap.

The target was no longer merely **an army** across a border.

It was an **invisible political structure** hiding inside the population.

Korea showed how **secret networks** could operate behind enemy lines and how **political lists** could be turned **against** a nation’s own civilians.

In South Vietnam, the United States would help construct **the institutions needed to combine both methods.**

And they started building these institutions **years before the Vietnam War.**

## **BUILDING SOUTH VIETNAM’S POLICE STATE**

[![](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/fdf45cd9-97a3-46f0-8032-b74ca3734f20_960x747.jpeg)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!lLNH!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Ffdf45cd9-97a3-46f0-8032-b74ca3734f20_960x747.jpeg)

A Vietnamese National Policeman questions a suspected Viet Cong in the village of Troung Lan, Binh Dinh Province, June 9, 1967.

Korea had shown what could happen when political suspicion became a government file, but South Vietnam would **take that process further.**

**First things first:**

You need an **internal-security system** capable of watching its own population.

**It needed:**

- Police
- Records
- Fingerprints
- Radios
- Informants
- Counterintelligence officers
- Armed civil guards

And most importantly **a government** **willing to treat political opposition as a threat to national security.**

**The United States** helped build all of it.

### **WASHINGTON’S CHOSEN GOVERNMENT**

After the **[French defeat at Điện Biên Phủ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Dien_Bien_Phu)** in 1954, Vietnam was temporarily divided between the communist-led government of **[Hồ Chí Minh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ho_Chi_Minh)** in the North and the **Western-backed** State of Vietnam in the South.

Washington needed someone who could hold the South together.

That man was **[Ngô Đình Diệm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ng%C3%B4_%C4%90%C3%ACnh_Di%E1%BB%87m)**.

[![Head shot of Diem](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/e5fe14cb-4c2f-449b-ba96-55950efcc3f9_960x1285.jpeg "Head shot of Diem")](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!WcGW!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fe5fe14cb-4c2f-449b-ba96-55950efcc3f9_960x1285.jpeg)

Ngô Đình Diệm

**[Diệm was a Catholic nationalist](https://vietnamproject.archives.msu.edu/terms/subjects/Ng%C3%B4%2C%20%C4%90%C3%ACnh%20Di%E1%BB%87m%2C%201901-1963/)** from a powerful political family. He had served in earlier Vietnamese governments but spent **years outside the country**, cultivating relationships with American officials, academics, journalists, Catholic leaders, and anti-communist activists.

One of his most important American allies was **[Wesley Fishel](https://vietnamproject.archives.msu.edu/terms/subjects/Fishel%2C%20Wesley%20R/)**, a political scientist at Michigan State University.

[![](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/1ceceb62-d3a9-4a64-a282-1652d308d4e2_282x168.jpeg)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!-CJY!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F1ceceb62-d3a9-4a64-a282-1652d308d4e2_282x168.jpeg)

Wesley R. Fishel (left) meets with Ngô Đình Diệm

**[Fishel met Diệm](https://vietnamproject.archives.msu.edu/collections/Wesley%20R.%20Fishel%20Papers/)** in Japan in the early 1950s. The two became close friends and maintained regular correspondence. ### **FROM SCATTERED PROGRAMS TO ONE MACHINE**

[As early as 1964,](https://www.armyupress.army.mil/Journals/Military-Review/English-Edition-Archives/COIN-Reader-1/Andrade-Willbanks-MA-2006/) the CIA was using small **counterterror teams** to seek out and destroy Viet Cong cadre hiding in villages. These teams were among the **forerunners** of the **[Provincial Reconnaissance Units](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76v06/d74)** (PRUs) that would later become **Phoenix’s most feared direct-action force.**

The CIA also helped establish **Provincial Interrogation Centers**, where captured suspects could be questioned about the structure of the communist underground.

**The centers served a practical purpose.** A **captured guerrilla** might know where weapons were stored. A **courier** might identify a district organizer. A **village cadre member** might name the person above him in the chain.

But interrogation did **more** than recover information.

**It produced new targets.**

Police intelligence units added another layer. The South Vietnamese **National Police Special Branch** investigated political activity, gathered reports from informants, monitored suspected communist organizers, and maintained dossiers.

Meanwhile, **Census Grievance teams** entered villages under the banner of listening to residents and recording complaints.

*That sounds nice enough. **Like an HR department.***

Except they gathered **household information**, mapped local relationships, identified power structures, and reported signs of Viet Cong taxation, recruiting, propaganda, or intimidation.

Not all census work was sinister. Governments need to know who lives where and what local people need.

**But power corrupts.**   
And **absolute power** corrupts absolutely.

Information collected for administration quickly became **information used for control.**

- **A family registry** could identify a suspect’s relatives.
- **A grievance interview** could expose village rivalries.
- **A census report** could reveal who had recently disappeared into the mountains.
- **A list of residents** could show who belonged in the village. **And who did not.**

The problem was not a lack of intelligence programs.

It was that **there were too many.**

By early 1965, the CIA, the [U.S. Agency for International Development](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Agency_for_International_Development), the State Department, the U.S. Information Service, the American military, and multiple South Vietnamese agencies were **all running separate pieces** of the pacification campaign. Their programs overlapped, competed for money, guarded their sources, and followed different chains of command.

One office might possess a useful name **but refuse to share it.**

Another might arrest a suspect **without telling the agency** that had been watching him.

The military focused on enemy forces. The police focused on internal security. Civilian aid officials focused on village administration and development.

Everyone was fighting the same war, but they were not fighting it together.

And who would be better at **uniting intelligence** than **The United States**?

### **HOW THE INTELLIGENCE LOOP WORKED**

[![How Phoenix Program Intelligence Loop Worked Infographic](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/94d149ea-923f-4c33-9e76-782c50c10c88_1122x1402.png "How Phoenix Program Intelligence Loop Worked Infographic")](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!sWhp!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F94d149ea-923f-4c33-9e76-782c50c10c88_1122x1402.png)

[The basic loop](https://www.armyupress.army.mil/Journals/Military-Review/English-Edition-Archives/COIN-Reader-1/Andrade-Willbanks-MA-2006/) worked like this:

1. **Someone supplied a name.**  
   The source might be a paid informant, captured document, prisoner, defector, police officer, Census Grievance worker, village official, military patrol, or frightened neighbor.
2. **The name entered a dossier.**  
   The file could include the suspect’s address, family, occupation, political history, known associates, alleged position, and reports from earlier interrogations.
3. **The suspect was classified.**  
   Intelligence officers attempted to decide whether the person was truly part of the Viet Cong Infrastructure and, if so, what role he or she played.
4. **The target was assigned.**  
   Police, militia units, regular military forces, or Provincial Reconnaissance Units might receive the intelligence and act upon it.
5. **The suspect was neutralized.**  
   The person might defect, be captured, imprisoned, killed during an operation, or otherwise removed from the underground network.
6. **The result produced more intelligence.**  
   Interrogation reports, seized papers, address books, photographs, letters, and statements from relatives could generate more names.
7. **The cycle began again.**

This is what made Phoenix **more than a collection of raids.**

Each operation fed the next.

**Target by target,** Phoenix attempted to climb the network.

The same structure also **created** **a deeper danger.**

A false name did not die inside the file.

It moved to another, **even worse**, file.

**CIA province officers directed and supervised their targeting** (because of course, CIA), while **American military personnel** commonly accompanied PRU elements.

**Kill or capture.**That was the wording.

By December 1969, [another classified review](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76v06/d157) described **roughly 4,200 PRU personnel** operating in **forty-four provinces.**

**Vietnam**, by the way, is [just](https://www.census.gov/geographies/reference-files/2010/geo/state-area.html) **[a bit smaller](https://www.census.gov/geographies/reference-files/2010/geo/state-area.html) than the state of Montana.**

**The CIA funded the program** and retained overall administrative control for the United States, while [more than](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76v06/d157) **[one hundred American military advisers](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76v06/d157)** and a smaller number of **CIA personnel** supported it.

[![](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/36232bc3-3398-461e-b729-5b1617774a86_712x156.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!01wd!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F36232bc3-3398-461e-b729-5b1617774a86_712x156.png)

Excerpt from [Memo for the 303 Committee](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1969-76v06/d157)

**Officials praised the PRUs** because they struck directly at individually chosen targets.

They also understood the danger.

The same review warned of the **political embarrassment** attached to an **American-backed police paramilitary force** attacking people who appeared to be civilians. It acknowledged that many PRU targets were killed and that the units had employed **methods considered extreme** by American standards.

That was the contradiction at Phoenix’s core.

Its targets often looked like civilians because many of them were civilians.

Others were just accused of being “the enemy”.

**And sometimes that was enough.**

### **WHAT “NEUTRALIZED” CONCEALED**

[![Opinion | Behind the Phoenix Program - The New York Times](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/af7efcc9-c451-4dc0-ac31-9afde41412cd_2048x1666.jpeg "Opinion | Behind the Phoenix Program - The New York Times")](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!6nfc!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Faf7efcc9-c451-4dc0-ac31-9afde41412cd_2048x1666.jpeg)

Phoenix measured success through a bloodless word:

**[Neutralized](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1964-68v06/d296).**

In the formal statistics, **suspected** members of the **Viet Cong Infrastructure** were generally counted as **neutralized** if they were killed, captured, or persuaded to rally to the South Vietnamese government.

Those outcomes were not remotely alike.

- A defector walked away **alive**.
- A prisoner might face **years of confinement**.
- A person **killed** in a firefight never reached an interrogation room or courtroom.

Yet each could appear as **one successful neutralization** in a monthly report.

The CIA initially listed the preferred order as defection in place, inducement to rally, capture, and finally the destruction of infrastructure elements.

That order made practical sense.

- A living suspect could talk.
- A defector could become an informant.
- A captured cadre member could identify the structure around him.

**But the broader language** used inside the program went beyond those formal categories. [The CIA’s own assessment](https://www.cia.gov/readingroom/document/0005347532) discussed **eliminating communist influence** through **death**, **imprisonment**, **political discredit**, or even **public identification**.

Again, **unlike outcomes** were folded into the same mission.

The purpose was not necessarily to prove that a suspect had committed a specific crime. **The purpose** was to **remove his usefulness** to the underground.

A suspect did not have to be convicted.

He just had to be **processed**.  
And the rest **worked itself out.**

## **HOW THE MACHINE TURNED SUSPICION INTO DEATH**

[![Sifting the Ashes of Counterinsurgency: The Role of America's Phoenix  Program in the Vietnam War | Readex](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/7e927a18-0aa3-4619-97a6-9d295fb7de03_355x267.png "Sifting the Ashes of Counterinsurgency: The Role of America's Phoenix  Program in the Vietnam War | Readex")](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!njlQ!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F7e927a18-0aa3-4619-97a6-9d295fb7de03_355x267.png)

**The blacklist** was supposed to give Phoenix its precision.

Instead of sweeping through a village and arresting everyone, the government could identify a **specific** communist organizer and send forces **directly** to him.

A rifle shot instead of a shotgun blast.

*At least, **that was the theory.***

The CIA’s own assessment admitted the central problem: **some blacklists** were **unreliable**. Important members of the underground could be missing, while innocent people might be included by mistake.

But a “mistake” was only one source of error.

*There were **also grudges.***

- A local official might **label a rival** as Viet Cong.
- A neighbor might **report someone** over a land dispute.
- A rejected suitor might **accuse a woman’s family.**
- A paid informant might **invent information** to keep the money coming.
- A corrupt officer might **accept a bribe** to remove one name and add another.
- A prisoner under torture might **identify anyone** he thought would make the pain stop.

Then came the barriers no chart could solve.

Different dialects.

Poor translation.

Even misspelled names.

Or several people in the same village **sharing similar names.**

[The U.S. Army’s later review](https://www.armyupress.army.mil/Journals/Military-Review/English-Edition-Archives/COIN-Reader-1/Andrade-Willbanks-MA-2006/) of Phoenix acknowledged that **unrealistic quotas** encouraged false reporting and the capture of innocent people against whom local officials held grudges.

And the fate of these individuals — both true enemies and “mistakes” — was not for the faint of heart.

### **THE INTERROGATION-TO-RAID CYCLE**

[![](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/4d5305b8-4731-4c5f-9475-2293da68929d_1375x1144.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!YUL6!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F4d5305b8-4731-4c5f-9475-2293da68929d_1375x1144.png)

“Viet Cong prisoner” awaits interrogation at the A-109 Special Forces Detachment in Thuong

Interrogation sat at the center of this system because interrogators could turn **one captive into many targets.**

Used lawfully, questioning could produce valuable information. A genuine cadre member might reveal a meeting place, weapons cache, courier route, or chain of command.

But **coercion poisoned the process.**

**A tortured prisoner has one goal:**

Make the pain stop.

*Truth becomes secondary.*

The prisoner names **the person the interrogator already suspects.**

Or the neighbor he **dislikes**.

Or someone he **barely knows**.

Or a **name pulled from memory** because silence brings another shock.

The new name enters a dossier.

The dossier produces a raid.

The raid produces another prisoner.

The new prisoner produces more names.

**And so it goes on.**

And their methods of extracting names was **very effective.**

>
> *The use of the insertion of the 6-inch dowel into the canal of one of my detainee’s ears, and the tapping through the brain until dead.*
>
> *The starvation to death (in a cage) of a Vietnamese woman who was suspected of being part of the local political education cadre in one of the local villages....*
>

>

>
> *The first gook wouldn’t talk. Intelligence gives you a signal, thumb towards the door, and you push the guy out.... If the second guy didn’t look like he wants to say something, or he’s lying, the intelligence officer says,*
>
> *“This guy’s out the door.”*
>
> *You kick him out....*
>
> *The last prisoner is crying and he’s...talking Vietnamese like crazy.... Before we get back to the base camp, after this guy does all the talking, and the intelligence officer documents everything, they kick him out the door anyway.*

Former military intelligence officer **[K. Barton Osborn](https://www.cia.gov/readingroom/document/01434866)** told lawmakers that he had witnessed brutal treatment and described Phoenix as a **depersonalized system of killing**. His testimony did not prove that every center used the same methods, but it shattered the claim that **abuse could be dismissed** as a few vague rumors.

[Newspaper article](https://www.proquest.com/openview/bd38fa688e05188518c4c1d110525d13/1?pq-origsite=gscholar&cbl=18750) about the Phoenix Program that references Barton. *The Washington Post*, August 3, 1971.

[William Colby responded](https://www.cia.gov/readingroom/document/cia-rdp99-00418r000100090001-2) that torture and unlawful killing **violated official policy**.

*Yeah, that’ll teach them.*

He also admitted that reprehensible acts had occurred during the wider anti-infrastructure campaign.

**That distinction became the government’s main defense:**

The abuse happened.

*But the program did not order it.*

**So not** ***our*** **fault.**

Obviously, this was before the movie “A Few Good Men” came out.

### **QUOTAS: WHEN THE NUMBERS BECAME THE MISSION**

Just like a good machine, the **Phoenix Program had metrics of success**.

Provincial officials received **[monthly neutralization goals](https://www.cia.gov/readingroom/document/0005347532)** based on estimates of how many VCI members operated in their area. The quotas were meant to force reluctant officials to take the underground seriously.

Well, turns out that this motivational tool created a new set of problems (and you can imagine what those were).

A district that produced few neutralizations looked **ineffective**.

A province that exceeded its goal looked **aggressive**.

A local chief needed to show that his area was improving, and how does he do that?

Well, he needed bodies, prisoners, or defectors. But…

**What happens when a province runs out of genuine targets…*****but hasn’t met its quota?***

You take **any intelligence scraps** you can get to meet the quota.

- Lower the standard.
- Count marginal suspects.
- Accept weaker intelligence.
- Inflate a prisoner’s importance.
- Report a conventional battlefield death as a VCI neutralization after the fact.

Or find someone against whom the local chief already holds a grudge. (*always a favorite)*

A special **Phụng Hoàng campaign** launched in late 1968 assigned quotas to each province and directed agencies to concentrate upon eliminating the VCI. In the same assessment, the CIA warned that Phoenix’s improved intelligence-coordination system could be used as a tool of **political elimination**.

The agency understood the danger.  
And of course let it be.   
**It was all part of the plan.**

*Remember Korea?*

**Political elimination** was not some unforeseen malfunction of Phoenix.

It followed the same logic already seen in Korea, where police files, ideological registries, informants, and right-wing paramilitaries turned suspicion into detention.

And detention into death.

On Jeju, entire villages were treated as guerrilla support zones. Phoenix did not invent that method. It mechanized it. The blacklist was never merely a tool for finding the enemy.

In the wrong hands—or perhaps the intended ones—**it was a way to decide who the enemy would be.**

And it was that approach that led the **Phoenix Program model** to be used by the CIA around the world **for decades**.

## **EXPORTING THE PHOENIX MODEL**

[![](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/0b57e9d7-c54f-4701-8c21-96ef7bf50114_1672x941.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!NBc5!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F0b57e9d7-c54f-4701-8c21-96ef7bf50114_1672x941.png)

**The Phoenix Program** did not need to survive under the name **Phoenix**.

After all, names attract attention.

Or **congressional hearings.**

Methods travel much more easily.

By the time American involvement in Vietnam began winding down, the **machinery behind Phoenix** had already been reduced to templates that could be used elsewhere:

How to map a political underground, identify its leaders, recruit informants, centralize intelligence, interrogate prisoners, and turn the information gathered in one operation into the targets for the next.

### **PROJECT X: MAKING VIETNAM PORTABLE**

The clearest documentary bridge out of Vietnam was a **U.S. Army program** known as **Project X**.

**Project X** began in the mid-1960s as part of the **[Army’s Foreign Intelligence Assistance Program](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Army_Foreign_Intelligence_Assistance_Program)**. Its purpose was to collect counterintelligence and counterinsurgency lessons, organize them into **training material**, and make them available to **American advisers** working with foreign military and security services.

Years later, **Major Victor Tise**, a former instructor at the [U.S. Army School of the Americas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Hemisphere_Institute_for_Security_Cooperation), [described Project X](https://nsarchive2.gwu.edu/NSAEBB/NSAEBB122/910801%20USSOUTHCOM%20CI%20Training%20(U).pdf) with unusual bluntness.

**“[A] training package to provide counterinsurgency techniques learned in Vietnam to Latin American countries.”**

*Pretty cut and dry.*

**Project X materials** addressed the same basic problem Phoenix had confronted: how does a government locate an enemy organization hidden inside the civilian population?

The answer involved much more than teaching troops **how to fight guerrillas**.

It meant:

- Identifying political networks.
- Recruiting and managing informants.
- Infiltrating organizations.
- Building personality files.
- Maintaining blacklists.
- Monitoring meetings and communications.
- Interrogating suspects.
- Identifying movement leaders.
- Exploiting captured papers and equipment.
- Using one arrest to generate the next operation.

That was the part of Phoenix worth exporting.

The **cycle**.

The National Security Archive later obtained a **1992 Pentagon investigation** tracing instructions involving **coercion**, **false imprisonment**, **execution**, and other “**prohibited” practices** back to Project X materials. The report warned that the manuals contained “**offensive and objectionable**” material that could damage American credibility.

Of course, the discovery did not occur when the manuals were first written.

The “discovery” came after **years of use.**

Some of the material was suspended during the Carter administration over fears that it could contribute to human-rights violations. Then, **under the Reagan administration**, instructors returned to the **Project X archives** and **revived** parts of the program.

The resulting Spanish-language manuals were reviewed at Defense Department headquarters and returned **unchanged**, according to **Tise**. More than a thousand copies were eventually distributed for use in countries including **El Salvador**, **Guatemala**, **Ecuador**, and **Peru**, as well as at the **School of the Americas** between 1987 and 1991.

*Remember that this would have been during the **[Iran-Contra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iran%E2%80%93Contra_affair)****[Scandal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iran%E2%80%93Contra_affair).***

The program had come **full circle.**

Techniques developed during Vietnam had been stored, translated, repackaged, approved, and handed to a **new generation of foreign officers**.

### **THE OFFICE OF PUBLIC SAFETY**

[![](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/0913a53e-294a-4f64-a3bf-ffecde382a39_250x250.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!BGlz!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F0913a53e-294a-4f64-a3bf-ffecde382a39_250x250.png)

Project X provided the material.

[The](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Office_of_Public_Safety) **[Office of Public Safety](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Office_of_Public_Safety)**, or OPS, helped provide the delivery system.

Created within the **U.S. foreign-aid structure**, the OPS [sent](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1961-63v12/d90) **[American police advisers abroad](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1961-63v12/d90)**, supplied equipment, trained foreign officers, developed police academies, and helped friendly governments modernize their internal-security forces.

Publicly, the mission was **professionalization**.

Turns out though, you can do **two things at the same time.**

- A program can **improve ordinary policing** while also strengthening the **government’s ability to monitor** political opponents.
- It can **train detectives** to solve crimes while **teaching intelligence units** to identify subversives.
- It can provide **radios for emergency response** while creating a **national network** through which political information travels.

American policy documents described these police-assistance programs as a means of building foreign capacity to counter communist subversion and insurgency. Officials called for training in records, investigations, intelligence, and internal security, often through the **Agency for International Development** (*Yes, **USAID***) but, when needed, through the **military** or **CIA**.

The language resembled the mission Michigan State had carried into South Vietnam.

**Public safety on the surface.**

**Political security beneath it.**

By 1962, the United States had **[expanded police programs](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1961-63v07-09mSupp/d415)** across **Latin America** and opened an Inter-American Police Academy in Panama. The number of American police technicians and foreign officers receiving U.S.-funded training grew rapidly.

One such figure was **[Dan Mitrione](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dan_Mitrione)**.  
Not surprisingly, he became one of the program’s **darkest symbols.**

[![Dan Mitrione Portrait](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/cc169eea-76f6-4bef-b499-c03be2aa2a09_1619x971.png "Dan Mitrione Portrait")](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!cPoo!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fcc169eea-76f6-4bef-b499-c03be2aa2a09_1619x971.png)

The **National Security Archive** describes the OPS mission there as assisting Uruguayan police in improving **counterinsurgency techniques** amid the **torture of prisoners**.

Mitrione, by the way, **was also connected to Jim Jones** (*Jonestown, “Drink the Kool-Aid”, etc*).

### **THE APOSTLES OF THE PHOENIX PROGRAM**

[![](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/4145394b-5ced-4d38-befe-61155ddd2cd2_1402x1122.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!UdDu!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F4145394b-5ced-4d38-befe-61155ddd2cd2_1402x1122.png)

Theodore Shackley

The Phoenix model did not spread only through manuals.

The people who built it moved too.

[CIA officer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theodore_Shackley) **[Theodore Shackley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theodore_Shackley)** had served in senior assignments in Laos and Vietnam, including as **station chief in Saigon** during the later Phoenix years. ### **OPERATION CONDOR: THE TRANSNATIONAL BLACKLIST**

[![Historical Context | Plan Cóndor](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/d7e16546-eb55-4af7-913d-b10c72d237db_1000x1251.jpeg "Historical Context | Plan Cóndor")](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!DbeG!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fd7e16546-eb55-4af7-913d-b10c72d237db_1000x1251.jpeg)

If Phoenix was an intelligence-and-targeting system contained mainly within South Vietnam, **[Operation Condor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operation_Condor)** expanded the same logic across national borders.

During the 1970s, [military governments](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1977-80v24/d12) in **Chile**, **Argentina**, **Uruguay**, **Paraguay**, **Bolivia**, and **Brazil** developed a cooperative system for tracking leftists, exiles, guerrillas, and political opponents.

At first, Condor centered on **exchanging intelligence**.

- Names
- Photographs
- Addresses
- Aliases
- Travel records
- Political ties
- Known associates

The kind of information **Phoenix centers** had gathered on the **Viet Cong Infrastructure**.

But the network did not remain a passive database.  
*Almost like it was never meant to be that…*

The potential targets included **alleged** terrorists, opposition politicians, and even figures connected to international human-rights organizations. American officials also received reports that **Condor countries** continued **directing assassination operations** outside their own borders.

But the Phoenix Program template can be seen again and again.

Iran.   
Honduras.   
The Philippines.

## **FROM PHOENIX TO ABU GHRAIB**

[![Exposure | The New Yorker](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/df2e8076-b0f0-4606-8f8c-fa8aaf2acd79_1119x852.png "Exposure | The New Yorker")](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!KefM!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fdf2e8076-b0f0-4606-8f8c-fa8aaf2acd79_1119x852.png)

After September 11, 2001, the language changed again.

Communist infrastructure became “**terrorist networks”**.

Cadre became “**high-value targets”**.

Neutralization became “**capture or kill”**.

Blacklists became “**targeting databases”**.

- Identify its nodes.

### **THE MANUALS SURVIVED THE PROGRAM**

The documentary chain begins before Phoenix itself.

In 1963, the CIA produced the **[KUBARK Counterintelligence Interrogation Manual](https://www2.gwu.edu/~nsarchiv/NSAEBB/NSAEBB122/CIA%20Kubark%201-60.pdf)**, a guide for extracting intelligence from resistant prisoners.

[![](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/bfb2b764-1d37-4ea1-863a-a4006537cc7c_635x361.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!OXzx!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fbfb2b764-1d37-4ea1-863a-a4006537cc7c_635x361.png)

Intro from [CIA KUBARK Manual](https://nsarchive2.gwu.edu/NSAEBB/NSAEBB122/CIA%20Kubark%201-60.pdf)

**[KUBARK](https://www2.gwu.edu/~nsarchiv/NSAEBB/NSAEBB122/CIA%20Kubark%2061-112.pdf)** [discussed](https://www2.gwu.edu/~nsarchiv/NSAEBB/NSAEBB122/CIA%20Kubark%2061-112.pdf) both ordinary and coercive interrogation. Its coercive sections addressed fear, threats, pain, prolonged standing, isolation, debility, sensory deprivation, and the manipulation of a prisoner’s environment and perception of time.

The manual did not merely describe brutality.

It tried to make coercion **systematic**.

- How should the room be arranged?
- How should the prisoner be isolated?
- When does a threat weaken resistance?
- How can pain be made to feel self-inflicted?
- How can the interrogator create dependence?

The [National Security Archive notes](https://nsarchive2.gwu.edu/NSAEBB/NSAEBB122/?utm_source=chatgpt.com) that **KUBARK** recommended techniques disturbingly similar to methods that later appeared at **[Abu Ghraib](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abu_Ghraib_torture_and_prisoner_abuse)**, including prolonged standing, sensory deprivation, fear, and the controlled manipulation of a prisoner’s surroundings.

The [1983](https://nsarchive2.gwu.edu/NSAEBB/NSAEBB122/CIA%20Human%20Res%20Exploit%20A1-G11.pdf) *[Human Resource Exploitation Training Manual](https://nsarchive2.gwu.edu/NSAEBB/NSAEBB122/CIA%20Human%20Res%20Exploit%20A1-G11.pdf)* borrowed heavily from KUBARK.

**It also drew from Project X.**

*Are you seeing the pattern here?*

**KUBARK interrogation doctrine**

↓

**Vietnam-era Project X manuals**

↓

**Latin American training material**

↓

**Human Resource Exploitation Manual**

The manual itself **contained a warning** that now reads like a prophecy:

> *“The routine use of torture lowers the moral caliber of the organization that uses it and corrupts those that rely on it.”*

The government knew.

It wrote the warning into the curriculum.

Then they **kept using the curriculum.**

### **THE PATRIOT ACT AND THE DOMESTIC INFORMATION MACHINE**

[![Let's Dismantle the Surveillance State - Progressive.org](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/ae340132-6051-44fb-93b5-393323a38e52_980x680.jpeg "Let's Dismantle the Surveillance State - Progressive.org")](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!5Cbm!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fae340132-6051-44fb-93b5-393323a38e52_980x680.jpeg)

And the Phoenix Program template does not stop with foreign interrogation rooms.

It also appears in the information architecture built inside the United States after 9/11.

**[The USA PATRIOT Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patriot_Act)**

Now, to be clear, this was not a **domestic Phoenix Program**.  
*Not entirely.*

It did not create **paramilitary teams** to assassinate American political opponents (*that we know of*), and no released evidence shows that its authors copied Phoenix directly.

*But again*…**we see the template.**

Phoenix was created because American officials believed agencies possessed pieces of information but were not sharing them quickly enough.

The police had one file.

The military held another.

The CIA knew something else.

The interrogation center had new names.

**The answer was coordination.**

After 9/11, officials described a **remarkably similar problem.**

The FBI, CIA, federal prosecutors, immigration authorities, local police, and other agencies [had gathered fragments of information](https://www.justice.gov/archive/olp/pdf/patriot_report_from_the_field0704.pdf) about suspected terrorists—but legal, bureaucratic, and cultural barriers prevented those fragments from becoming one usable picture.

The USA PATRIOT Act **[removed those barriers](https://www.justice.gov/sites/default/files/ag/legacy/2005/04/19/sabintestimony4_19.pdf)**.

**A hidden enemy** can be found by mapping relationships.

- *Who called whom?*
- *Who sent money?*
- *Who traveled together?*
- *Who rented the room?*
- *Who shared the address?*
- *Who attended the meeting?*
- *Who appeared in the contact list?*

The individual fact may mean little.

**The network** gives it meaning.

That is the Phoenix Program translated into **the age of metadata.**

### **FROM ICEX TO F3EAD**

[![Harnessing Military Precision for Business: F3EAD](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/862f5f00-ba59-4f98-8147-294a0f2cc421_1920x1080.png "Harnessing Military Precision for Business: F3EAD")](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!GlTY!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F862f5f00-ba59-4f98-8147-294a0f2cc421_1920x1080.png)

While Phoenix’s intelligence cycle was slow by modern standards, **modern special-operations forces** compressed that cycle **into hours**.

[The method](https://www.armyupress.army.mil/Journals/Military-Review/English-Edition-Archives/July-August-2018/Lushenko/) became known as **[F3EAD](https://www.armyupress.army.mil/Journals/Military-Review/English-Edition-Archives/May-June-2017/The-State-of-Afghanistans-Intelligence-Enterprise/)**:

- **Find.** Find the target.
- **Fix.** Confirm the location.
- **Finish.** Capture or kill.
- **Exploit.** Exploit the site, body, prisoner, computer, telephone, papers, and weapons.
- **Analyze.** Analyze the recovered information.
- **Disseminate.** Send the results to the people preparing the next operation.

**Then repeat.**

U.S. Army publications describe F3EAD as an **intelligence-driven targeting method** in which [material recovered](https://www.armyupress.army.mil/Journals/Military-Review/English-Edition-Archives/May-June-2017/The-State-of-Afghanistans-Intelligence-Enterprise/) during one operation enriches databases and reveals links among **devices**, **people**, and **social networks**.

F3EAD is not inherently criminal. Structurally, F3EAD solves the same problem Phoenix tried to solve.

The cycle is faster. **The logic is nearly identical.**

Technology reduced some forms of error.

- It made targets **easier to identify**.
- It gave commanders **better tools** for avoiding civilians.
- It allowed intelligence from one operation to reach another unit **almost instantly**.

But speed does not make an assumption true.

A false accusation entered into a Phoenix dossier might poison one district’s files.

A **false assumption** entered into a **modern network** can cross agencies, borders, and databases before anyone questions the original source.

The machinery has become more precise.   
**It has also become more powerful.**

*Let’s look at the timeline again:*

- **Project X** turned that lesson into a training package.
- **The Office of Public Safety** gave it institutional cover.
- **Operation Condor** carried the list across borders.
- **KUBARK** preserved the interrogation doctrine.
- **The Patriot Act** [strengthened the information-sharing](https://www.justice.gov/archive/ll/subs/congress/hjcpatriotactcombinedresponses3.pdf) architecture at home.
- **F3EAD** accelerated the raid-and-exploitation cycle abroad.

The names changed.

The filing systems changed.

The enemy changed.

But the central principle survived:

**Find the network. Build the file. Remove the target. Exploit the result.**

**Begin again.**

## **THE PROGRAM ENDED. THE SYSTEM DID NOT.**

The Phoenix Program *officially* faded with America’s withdrawal from Vietnam.

But the **Phoenix Program template** did not truly end.

It has been repackaged into new agencies, new manuals, new databases, and new wars. The officers retire, but **the trainees remain**.

*Trace the pattern.*

**Operation Gladio** built secret networks throughout postwar Europe. Intelligence agencies recruited reliable local allies, hid covert activity inside legitimate institutions, divided responsibility among proxies, and treated political movements as potential enemy infrastructure.

**In Korea**, that logic turned inward.

**Jeju villages** became suspected support zones. Bodo League registries placed alleged leftists and unreliable citizens into government files. Once war began, those files could determine who was detained, interrogated, or killed.

At the same time, **the CIA built clandestine networks** for reconnaissance, sabotage, and guerrilla operations behind enemy lines.

Two systems emerged.

One hunted hidden enemies abroad.

The other hunted suspected enemies within.

**Vietnam brought them together.**

**Before Phoenix received its name**, American advisers had already helped South Vietnam build national police forces, fingerprint files, communications networks, interrogation centers, intelligence units, civil guards, and centralized records.

**Michigan State University** provided the respectable face of technical assistance. CIA programs operated within the same administrative structure. Professors taught public administration while intelligence personnel trained counterespionage officers.

**Then came Phoenix.**

Police intelligence, census records, informants, interrogation reports, military forces, psychological operations, and **paramilitary teams** were connected through one coordinated system.

When Vietnam ended, **Project X** turned those lessons into [training material](https://nsarchive2.gwu.edu/NSAEBB/NSAEBB122/) that could travel. **The Office of Public Safety** delivered police equipment, records systems, intelligence training, and counterinsurgency expertise **to allied governments** around the world.

**Phoenix had become portable.**

**Operation Condor** then carried the blacklist across national borders.

South American security services exchanged names, photographs, travel records, and political affiliations. A dissident could flee one dictatorship only to be identified, kidnapped, interrogated, or killed by another.

**The network** no longer stopped at the border.

**Neither did the target list.**

The Cold War eventually ended.

**The method found a new enemy.**

**After September 11**, communism was replaced with “terrorism.”

**The Patriot Act** expanded information sharing among intelligence agencies and law enforcement.

[Then came](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CHRG-110shrg47298/html/CHRG-110shrg47298.htm) **Guantánamo**, **Afghanistan**, **Iraq**, and **Abu Ghraib**.

- Find the network.
- Break one node.
- Exploit what remains.

**Begin again.**

Modern special-operations forces eventually refined that cycle into **F3EAD**.

But the underlying structure remains familiar.

**Identify → locate → capture or kill → exploit → generate new targets.**

And this template, fashioned with **Operation Gladio** and refined since then, should concern everyone.

Because today, each of us **leaves behind a trail** that Phoenix officials could only have dreamed of collecting.

- Your emails reveal who you speak with.
- Your phone records show when and how often you speak.
- Your location history shows where you sleep, work, worship, shop, and meet others.
- Your website visits expose what you read, fear, question, and believe.
- Your purchases reveal your habits.
- Your social media activity maps your friends, family, politics, and interests.
- Your face can be matched by cameras.
- Your fingerprints unlock devices.
- Your voice can identify you.

Information does not simply disappear, especially when it is valuable.

**It simply waits to be used.**

**MORE ON THAT HERE:**

And the same data that helps find a missing child can help **track a political opponent.**

The same **location history** that proves a suspect visited a terrorist safe house can show that **a citizen attended a protest.**

**Or went into the Capitol on [January 6th](https://thewhiterabbitreport.substack.com/p/was-january-6-a-set-up-16-clues-of?utm_source=publication-search).**

The same social graph that identifies a criminal network can turn friends, coworkers, relatives, and acquaintances into suspicious “associations.”

The same biometric system that stops identity fraud can make anonymity nearly impossible.

So what prevents a corrupt or frightened government from exploiting those nodes?

In the end, **it’s We The People.**

- Whistleblowers
- Investigative reporters
- Citizen oversight
- Independent judges
- Citizens willing to demand answers

And those safeguards are not automatic.

**They work only when people defend them.**

History shows what happens when **fear** weakens due process. When **secret evidence or rumors** replaces proof. When officials divide responsibility until no one feels accountable. When **citizens accept surveillance** because they believe only the guilty will be watched.

The targets are always described as **dangerous**.

The tools are always presented as **temporary**.

The lists are always said to contain **the right names**.

***Until they do not.***

Phoenix was not one isolated scandal from a distant war.

**It was one stage in a much longer evolution.**

That is why remembering the pattern matters.

Not to fear every police officer.

Not to assume every intelligence analyst has evil intent.

Not to pretend genuine threats do not exist.

But to understand what powerful systems can become **when secrecy grows**, oversight weakens, and suspicion becomes enough.

**Stay vigilant.**

**Stay aware.**

And most importantly, **do not forget our history.**

Not the polished history taught in schools and written to protect institutions.

**The true history.**

Because, as we have seen, these patterns **do not disappear.**

And the way we destroy the power behind these secret programs and buried truths is by **exposing their patterns**. **Again and again.**

## **FREQUENTLY ASKED QUESTIONS**

### **Did Operation Phoenix really begin before the Vietnam War?**

Not as a formally named program.

ICEX was created in 1967, while South Vietnam’s Phụng Hoàng program expanded in 1968. But the essential pieces—political blacklists, police intelligence, population files, counterinsurgency teams, interrogation centers, paramilitary forces, and intelligence-driven raids—had already been developed in Korea, postwar Europe, and earlier American programs inside South Vietnam.

Phoenix did not invent those methods.

It centralized them.

### **How many people were killed by the Phoenix Program?**

The [official American count](https://www.cia.gov/readingroom/docs/CIA-RDP80R01720R001100090041-2.pdf) recorded more than 20,000 suspected members of the Viet Cong Infrastructure as killed between 1968 and 1971. Some writers and investigators place the number [closer to 40,000](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phoenix_Program).

But the totals require context.

Most officially recorded deaths were attributed to conventional military operations rather than individual CIA-directed assassinations. At the same time, Provincial Reconnaissance Units were explicitly authorized to kill or capture selected targets, and officials admitted that unreliable blacklists, political grudges, quotas, and abusive interrogations could place innocent people inside the system.

The most important question is therefore not merely how many died.

It is how the government decided who counted as the enemy.

### **Was Operation Phoenix directly connected to Operation Gladio?**

No released document proves that Phoenix was formally controlled by Gladio or belonged to the same command structure.

The connection is found in the operating model.

Both relied upon secret local networks, politically dependable allies, proxy forces, compartmentalized intelligence, institutional cover, and enough divided responsibility to preserve deniability.

William Colby also provides a personnel bridge. Before overseeing American pacification efforts in Vietnam, he had worked with resistance forces during World War II, postwar stay-behind networks in Scandinavia, and covert political operations in Italy.

Gladio and Phoenix were different programs.

But they emerged from the same Cold War belief that communism had to be fought not only on battlefields, but inside political parties, villages, unions, universities, churches, and civilian society.

### **How were Phoenix techniques exported to other countries?**

The clearest bridge was **[Project X](https://nsarchive2.gwu.edu/NSAEBB/NSAEBB122/)**[,](https://nsarchive2.gwu.edu/NSAEBB/NSAEBB122/) a U.S. Army program that gathered counterintelligence and counterinsurgency lessons and converted them into training material for foreign security forces.

These materials taught officers how to identify political networks, recruit informants, infiltrate organizations, maintain blacklists, interrogate prisoners, exploit captured information, and target movement leaders.

The Office of Public Safety and other American programs then helped train and equip police and intelligence services across Latin America and elsewhere.

Operation Condor later expanded the basic principle across national borders. South American dictatorships exchanged information about dissidents, located targets in neighboring countries, and cooperated in surveillance, abduction, interrogation, disappearance, and assassination.

Phoenix coordinated a blacklist within one country.

Condor made the blacklist international.

### **Is the Patriot Act a modern version of the Phoenix Program?**

Not literally.

The Patriot Act did not create death squads, interrogation centers, or a program for assassinating political opponents. It operated through legislation, courts, surveillance orders, intelligence agencies, and law enforcement.

But it reflected a similar theory of information.

Phoenix was created because government agencies possessed scattered pieces of intelligence that were not being coordinated. The answer was to centralize information, map relationships, share files, and identify hidden networks.

After September 11, the Patriot Act expanded intelligence sharing and surveillance authorities for much the same stated reason.

The danger appears when association begins to substitute for guilt.

Who called whom?

Who visited which website?

Who attended which meeting?

Who traveled to the same location?

Who appears in another person’s contacts?

In a digital society, those connections can reveal a genuine terrorist network.

They can also turn innocent relationships into suspicious “nodes” if the underlying information is false, misunderstood, or politically manipulated.

That does not make every modern intelligence system Phoenix.

It means the same warning still applies:

**A database is only as trustworthy as the information placed inside it—and the people allowed to decide what that information means.**
