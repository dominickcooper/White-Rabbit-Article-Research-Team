## What Are SSRIs?

**[SSRIs](https://www.mayoclinic.org/diseases-conditions/depression/in-depth/ssris/art-20044825)**—*selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors*—sound clean. Clinical. Safe.

They’re sold as tools. Fixes. A way to steady the mind.

At **their core**, they work like this: they block the brain from pulling serotonin back in. That leaves more of it drifting in the gap between cells. **More signal**. **More “balance.”**

At least, **that’s the claim.**

**[Serotonin](https://www.simplypsychology.org/what-is-serotonin.html)**ties to **mood**, **sleep**, **hunger**, **thought**. Mess with it, and you don’t just mess with sadness—you mess with **the whole system**.

**That’s the trade.**

These drugs came in as a “better way.” Before them, older meds—[tricyclics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tricyclic_antidepressant) and [MAOIs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monoamine_oxidase_inhibitor)—hit hard and came with real risk. Overdose could kill. Side effects could wreck a body.

Then came [Prozac](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fluoxetine).

[![](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/1560cdf4-3b74-4115-8ea0-8829b155e7a4_320x320.jpeg)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!C86A!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F1560cdf4-3b74-4115-8ea0-8829b155e7a4_320x320.jpeg)

Eli Lilly biochemist David Wong (left) played an instrumental role in the discovery of Prozac.

[When](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/from-freud-to-fluoxetine/202003/the-story-of-prozac-a-landmark-drug-in-psychiatry) **[Prozac](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/from-freud-to-fluoxetine/202003/the-story-of-prozac-a-landmark-drug-in-psychiatry)** [hit the market in 1987](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/from-freud-to-fluoxetine/202003/the-story-of-prozac-a-landmark-drug-in-psychiatry), it was hailed as a breakthrough. Safer. Gentler. A clean fix for a messy problem.

That story stuck.

But stories aren’t always the truth.

### Common SSRI Medications

They go by brand names now—familiar, almost friendly. But under the surface, they all move in the same way.

- **[Fluoxetine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fluoxetine) (Prozac)** — often given to kids for depression and OCD
- **[Sertraline](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sertraline) (Zoloft)** — widely prescribed for for anxiety, mood swings
- **[Escitalopram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Escitalopram) (Lexapro)** — approved for adolescents aged 12 and up
- **[Paroxetine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paroxetine) (Paxil)** — tied to some of the worst trial data
- **[Citalopram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Citalopram) (Celexa)** — commonly prescribed, though [not officially FDA-approved for children](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/drugsatfda_docs/label/2023/215428s002lbl.pdf)
- **[Fluvoxamine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fluvoxamine) (Luvox)** — primarily used for OCD in children

These medications are not chemically identical, but they share the same basic mechanism and, as the evidence will show, **many of the same risks.**

## The Surge in SSRI Prescriptions

### Trends Since the 1970s

In the 1970s, antidepressant use was relatively rare. Depression itself was considered a serious but uncommon condition, typically reserved for severe, debilitating presentations.

Today, the picture is almost unrecognizable.

Antidepressents are everywhere.

The United States is, by virtually every metric, the most medicated nation on earth. Antidepressant prescriptions have increased by more than 400% since the 1990s.

SSRIs now account for **the vast majority of those prescriptions.**

RFK Jr., [speaking at a press conference with Governor Abbott](https://www.newsnationnow.com/politics/ssri-rfk-jr-psychiatric-drugs/) and HHS Secretary Kennedy about Texas healthcare, connected this pharmaceutical trend to broader behavioral shifts:

> *“Something changed about human behavior in the 70’s, and it only happened in America, where we’ve got 20% of the population now taking these drugs.”*

[![](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/ea69ce67-1a6a-4520-b7eb-69eb104e4af4_1085x814.jpeg)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!So15!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fea69ce67-1a6a-4520-b7eb-69eb104e4af4_1085x814.jpeg)

His comments, whether one agrees with their political framing or not, point to a trend that is statistically undeniable.

Today, nearly **[one in five Americans](https://www.rasmussenreports.com/public_content/politics/partner_surveys/mental_health_20_are_currently_on_medication)** takes a psychiatric drug.

That’s not a small shift.  
That’s a whole redesign of what “*normal*” is.

### Growth in Pediatric Psychiatry

Then came the children.

**The rise of pediatric psychiatry** as a specialty, combined with an expanding definition of childhood mental illness, created fertile ground for pharmaceutical companies to market their products to an **entirely new demographic**.

And, in the early 90s, the floodgates opened.

New labels. New diagnoses. New markets.

- Kids who couldn’t sit still → **ADHD**
- Kids who felt low → **depression**
- Kids who worried → **anxiety**

[![The Rise of All-Purpose Antidepressants | Scientific American](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/a50cf90a-79de-4ada-802c-9405bf706c59_2318x1508.jpeg "The Rise of All-Purpose Antidepressants | Scientific American")](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!e6vo!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fa50cf90a-79de-4ada-802c-9405bf706c59_2318x1508.jpeg)

And each label came with a **pill**.  
Each new diagnosis came a corresponding prescription.

**Ritalin** gave way to **Adderall**; children presenting with low mood were placed on **Prozac** or **Zoloft**. By the time the 2000s arrived, it was not unusual for elementary-school children to be taking multiple psychiatric medications simultaneously.

## SSRIs and Behavioral Changes

### Emotional Blunting and Personality Shifts

One of the most commonly reported — and least discussed — side effects of SSRIs is emotional blunting: a flattening of the full emotional spectrum that extends well beyond the relief of depressive symptoms. Patients describe feeling “emotionally numb,” as though the drug has not just lifted their lows but erased their highs as well.

Not worse.

Just… **less**.

[A survey of 1,829 SSRI users in New Zealand](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24534123/) found that 60% reported feeling emotionally numb and 52% felt that they “didn’t feel like themselves.”

**Here’s the breakdown:**

- Sexual Difficulties - 62%
- Feeling Emotionally Numb - 60%
- Feeling Not Like Myself - 52%
- Reduction In Positive Feelings - 42%
- Caring Less About Others - 39%
- Suicidality - 39%
- Withdrawal Effects - 55%.

**Plus, eight of the 20 adverse effects** studied were reported **by over half the participants.**

**OVER HALF.**

These are not minor inconveniences. For a child, that’s no small thing. That’s the years where you learn who you are. What you feel.

**What matters.**

Now imagine that range cut in half.

*Or worse.*

[Pediatric nurse and parent advocate @TheHoleTweet](https://x.com/TheHoleTweet), who spent over 20 years working with children in clinical settings, described what she observed:

> *“Every one of these school shooters have had the same blank stare on their faces when we see a mugshot or pictures of them. They literally look like they’ve had every single human emotion sucked out of them.”*

Call it anecdote if you want.

But it is consistent with what the **clinical literature documents** as “[affective blunting](https://animosanopsychiatry.com/blog/emotional-numbness-symptoms-underlying-causes-and-treatment-approaches/)” — a recognized side effect that many physicians still fail to adequately communicate to patients and families before prescribing.

### Reports of Agitation and Mania

Beyond blunting, SSRIs carry a documented risk of inducing the opposite effect: agitation, hostility, impulsivity, and mania.

The FDA’s own reviewers found that [SSRIs could trigger these states](https://accpjournals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/phar.2767) — a finding that the agency described as resembling the stimulant effects of cocaine or methamphetamine.

- Restlessness that won’t stop
- Irritation that spikes without warning
- Sudden bursts of energy
- Impulse without brakes

[In the New Zealand survey](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0165178117305371), **47% of respondents** reported agitation as a side effect. In clinical trials, these states have been documented even in patients with no prior history of such symptoms.

The paradox is striking: a drug **prescribed to reduce distress** can instead **produce** exactly the kind of **escalating agitation** and **impulsivity** that leads to dangerous behavior.

And once that behavior starts, **it doesn’t always simmer down quietly.**

## Clinical Trials and Controversies

When Prozac entered the market, it was followed by more than **[39,000 adverse event reports](https://countercurrents.org/pringle270107.htm#:~:text=But%20with%20the%20massive%20off,to%20doctors%20in%20the%20US.)** by 1997 — including suicides and psychotic episodes.

Rather than triggering a regulatory re-examination, the FDA and pharmaceutical companies actively worked to **contain and contextualize** this data in ways that protected **the product** rather than the **public**.

The **[structural design of the trials](https://kffhealthnews.org/morning-breakout/dr00027432/#:~:text=FDA%2C%20Congress%20Reviewing%20Documents,Sun%20%2C%201/1).)** themselves has been criticized too.

The use of **short trial periods**, the **exclusion of high-risk patients**, the **inclusion of washout periods** that artificially reduce side effect reporting, and the selection of endpoint measures **favorable to the drugs** have all been documented in peer-reviewed literature.

### Paxil Study Findings

The Paxil (paroxetine) clinical trial data is perhaps **the most extensively analyzed** — **and most damning** — in the SSRI literature.

## SSRIs and Suicidal Ideation

### FDA Black Box Warnings

In 2004, [following pressure from advocacy groups and independent researchers](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7031767/), the FDA mandated that all antidepressants carry a **black box warning** — the agency’s most serious caution — about the increased risk of suicidal thoughts and behavior in children and adolescents.

This was based on actual data from clinical trials showing a **statistically significant increase in suicidal ideation** among minors taking these drugs.

**The [39% rate of suicidal ideation](https://www.psychology.org.nz/journal-archive/NZJP-Vol371-2008-5-Liebert.pdf)** among SSRI users in [the New Zealand survey](https://www.psychology.org.nz/journal-archive/NZJP-Vol371-2008-5-Liebert.pdf) is consistent with what the clinical trial data showed.

Perhaps the most disturbing evidence comes from studies in which SSRIs were administered to people **with no history of mental illness**.

One came **within moments of throwing herself in front of a train** before a phone call interrupted her.

This is a [documented clinical finding](https://davidhealy.org/wp-content/uploads/2012/05/2000-Healy-Healthy-Volunteer-Suicide.pdf).

The drug did not unmask a “*pre-existing vulnerability”* in these individuals.

**It created the ideation** de novo, in **people who had been healthy and stable** before the trial began.

## Alternative Approaches to Mental Health

The treatment of **depression**, **anxiety**, and **related conditions in children** does not begin and end with SSRIs.

A robust evidence base supports a range of **non-pharmaceutical interventions** that have shown **significant efficacy** — often comparable to medication, and without the associated risks.

- **[Cognitive behavioral therapy](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28859190/)** (CBT) is arguably the gold standard for the treatment of childhood depression and anxiety, with decades of research supporting its effectiveness.
- **[Dialectical behavior therapy](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/psychological-medicine/article/efficacy-of-dialectical-behavior-therapy-for-adolescent-selfharm-and-suicidal-ideation-a-systematic-review-and-metaanalysis/800E5FFC241040B62414A8EA25BA2B72)** (DBT) has shown particular efficacy with adolescents.
- **[Play therapy](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/232438673_The_Efficacy_of_Play_Therapy_With_Children_A_Meta-Analytic_Review_of_Treatment_Outcomes)** and **[family therapy](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/1467-6427.12476)** provide age-appropriate frameworks for younger children.
- In cases of trauma-based presentations, **[EMDR](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0272735809000890)**has a growing evidence base.

And most importantly, these interventions **do not come with black box warnings**.

There’s also lifestyle changes:

- **[Regular physical activity](https://www.npr.org/2026/01/12/nx-s1-5667599/exercise-is-as-effective-as-medication-in-treating-depression-study-finds)** has been shown in multiple studies to have antidepressant effects comparable to medication.
- **[Sleep hygiene](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12349785/)** is profoundly connected to mood regulation, particularly in adolescents.
- **[Nutrition](https://www.psychiatry.org/News-room/APA-Blogs/Mental-Health-Through-Better-Nutrition)**— including omega-3 fatty acids, vitamin D, and gut microbiome health — has increasingly been recognized as a significant contributor to mental wellbeing.

Also, social connection, time in nature, reduced screen time, and stable family environments **all represent modifiable factors** with meaningful effects on childhood mental health.

## FAQs

**1. Are SSRIs ever appropriate for children?** In certain cases of severe, treatment-resistant depression or OCD, SSRIs may be considered — but only after other interventions have been tried, with fully informed consent, and with close monitoring for adverse effects, particularly suicidal ideation. The FDA has approved specific SSRIs for limited pediatric uses. However, the evidence base supporting their use is far weaker, and the risk profile far higher, than most prescribing physicians communicate.

**2. What are the signs that an SSRI may be harming a child?** Parents and caregivers should watch for: sudden increase in agitation, hostility, or irritability; emotional blunting or unusual detachment; new or worsening suicidal thoughts; significant sleep disruption; and any sudden behavioral change shortly after starting or changing a dose. These symptoms warrant immediate contact with a healthcare provider.

**3. Can a child safely stop taking SSRIs?** Abrupt discontinuation of SSRIs is not safe and can trigger severe withdrawal symptoms. Any reduction in dose must be done gradually, typically by no more than 10% per month, under medical supervision. Unfortunately, many physicians are not well-versed in safe tapering protocols — the peer support community at SurvivingAntidepressants.org has become a major resource for patients in this situation.

**4. Is there a proven link between SSRIs and mass violence?** There is no proven causal relationship established in controlled research. What does exist is: documented pharmacological mechanisms by which SSRIs can induce agitation, hostility, impulsivity, and mania; a significant number of high-profile violent incidents in which perpetrators were on or recently withdrawn from SSRIs or related medications; and an FDA black box warning about suicidal and homicidal ideation. The research is difficult to conduct, and the data often sealed. But the pattern is sufficiently consistent to warrant serious, independent investigation — not dismissal.

**5. What alternatives to SSRIs should parents explore first?** Before turning to medication, parents and clinicians should explore: cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT), which has strong evidence for childhood depression and anxiety; physical exercise, which has demonstrated antidepressant effects comparable to medication in multiple studies; nutritional assessment and intervention; sleep hygiene improvement; reduction of social media and screen exposure; and family or systemic therapy where appropriate. These approaches carry no black box warnings and no withdrawal risk.
