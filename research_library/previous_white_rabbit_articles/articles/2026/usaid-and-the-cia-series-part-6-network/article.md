## **CONGRESS ACTUALLY KILLED THE OFFICE**

Let’s get one thing out of the way first: **Congress really did kill OPS.**

**[Section 660 of the Foreign Assistance Act](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?edition=prelim&num=0&req=granuleid%3AUSC-prelim-title22-section2420)** cut off Foreign Assistance Act money for training or supporting foreign police, prisons, internal intelligence and surveillance.

The International Police Academy shut down.

The **giant** **USAID police apparatus** that had put American advisers next to foreign security services from Latin America to Southeast Asia lost its legal home.

**The office was gone.**

But here’s the catch.

Section 660 told **AID** what it could no longer ***fund***.

- It did not erase the Justice Department.
- It did not erase State.
- It did not erase Defense.
- It did not erase the FBI or **[DEA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drug_Enforcement_Administration)**.

And the law itself left **important doors open** for **narcotics work** and ***certain*** federal law-enforcement activity.

Then Congress started adding more exceptions.

- Maritime enforcement
- Antiterrorism
- Judicially controlled investigations
- ***Specific*** countries (*that’s weird)*
- **Post-conflict** police rebuilding (*ah. the police are back*)

In other words, Washington didn’t bring OPS back. It started splitting up the old bundle into **other departments**.

*And that is where this story gets interesting.*

## **THEN ICITAP GREW TEETH**

Kriskovich helped build ICITAP in 1986.

At first, it still looked like the narrow thing Congress had let back through the Section 660 exception: investigations, forensics, police management and technical training.

**But then came Panama.**

After [Operation Just Cause](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_invasion_of_Panama), ICITAP developed the plan to turn Panama’s former military security force into civilian-led police, and [that mission changed ICITAP](https://www.justice.gov/criminal/criminal-icitap/icitap-historical-milestones) from a training organization into a **“full-service international law enforcement development organization.”**

***That is a much bigger job** than teaching detectives how to work a case.*

ICITAP was now helping **rebuild the institutions** that controlled policing inside another country. **Then [the map started filling in](https://www.justice.gov/criminal/criminal-icitap/file/831286/dl?inline=):**

- **Colombia** in 1991
- **El Salvador** in 1992, where ICITAP helped build the National Civilian Police and National Public Security Academy
- **Somalia** in 1994
- **Haiti** two days after U.S. troops arrived, where ICITAP began **a five-year plan** to build **a new civilian police force**.

**Then El Salvador. Again.**

**Kriskovich** had already trained the Salvadoran Special Investigative Unit in 1985. The next year he became ICITAP’s founding director; a few years after that, [the same program was helping](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/GOVPUB-D5_400-PURL-LPS1656/pdf/GOVPUB-D5_400-PURL-LPS1656.pdf) build El Salvador’s new national police structure.

**By the time Kriskovich retired in 1994**, the capability had grown **far beyond** the four-week Caribbean Police School he started with.

**The United States** (***once again***) had a program that could train foreign police, reorganize them, build academies, create investigative systems and help stand up whole civilian forces.

OPS had been broken apart, but a large piece of what it once did had returned through **a new legal and bureaucratic route**.

## **THE CUBAN SOCIAL NETWORK**

Now we get to **[ZunZuneo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ZunZuneo)**.

Beginning in 2009, USAID’s Office of Transition Initiatives [quietly funded a Cuban social-media project called](https://oig.usaid.gov/sites/default/files/2018-06/9-000-16-001-s.pdf) **[ZunZuneo](https://oig.usaid.gov/sites/default/files/2018-06/9-000-16-001-s.pdf?utm_source=chatgpt.com)**—slang for the sound of a hummingbird. Creative Associates International built it under a broader $15.5 million Cuban civil-society program designed [to expand independent civic activity on the island](https://oig.usaid.gov/sites/default/files/2018-06/9-000-16-001-s.pdf).

To Cuban users, it was supposed to look **homegrown**.

**Behind the scenes,** [it was anything but.](https://oig.usaid.gov/sites/default/files/2018-06/9-000-16-001-s.pdf)

The project was routed through **contractors**, **grants** and **offshore corporate structures** so the U.S. government’s role would not be obvious.

It gathered **a huge database** of Cuban cellphone numbers, built an audience with ordinary content, and then explored **how that audience could be segmented** by political tendencies and nudged toward greater civic participation.

- USAID supplied the money.
- A private contractor built the platform.
- Offshore structures created distance from the sponsor.
- Cuban citizens supplied the audience.

And the whole thing worked better **if those citizens did not know Washington was behind it.**

This was not traditional foreign aid in the sense most taxpayers would picture when they hear the term. It was a U.S.-funded political-influence project aimed at building a communications network inside an authoritarian state while obscuring the American hand behind it.

The Inspector General stopped short of labeling ZunZuneo a covert action, and the project never became the mass-mobilization machine some planners imagined. But the architecture is sitting there in the documents: **policy in one place, money in another, implementation through contractors, ownership pushed offshore, and attribution kept at arm’s length.**

So by the Obama years, Washington did not need to put a politically sensitive influence operation **inside the CIA** at all.

**You just put it in a “tweet.”   
You just put it in 5,000 tweets.**

**Repeat the same message loud enough and frequent enough,   
and you can stir up any large group of people  
to do anything you want.**

- A civilian aid agency could fund it.
- A contractor could build it.
- Offshore entities could hide the fingerprints.

**And the target population could use it.**

That is a much more modern version of the same basic logic we have been following throughout this series:

**Split the mission across enough institutions, and the capability (or culpability) becomes harder to see.**

## **AFGHANISTAN**

Afghanistan fits **[The Pipeline from Part 3](https://thewhiterabbitreport.substack.com/p/usaid-and-the-cia-series-part-3-drug-pipeline?utm_source=publication-search)**almost too well.

U.S. intelligence built alliances with **local forces** for strategic reasons, those allies were deeply tied to the opium trade, and Washington tolerated the narcotics economy because breaking those relationships would threaten the larger mission.

[In McCoy’s words](https://archive.org/details/pdfy-MiSTDFMs_BBF8W9B/page/n1/mode/2up), the heroin trade became an “**inadvertent but inevitable consequence**” of Cold War strategy.

**Now [look at Afghanistan](https://www.unodc.org/documents/data-and-analysis/AOTP/Report_2014.pdf).**

- In 2001, after the Taliban’s poppy ban, Afghan opium production collapsed to about **185 tons**.
- In 2002, after the U.S.-led invasion, it jumped back to about **3,400 tons**.
- By 2007 it had reached **8,200 tons**.

And right in the middle of that transition was **ICITAP**.

- [In 2002](https://www.justice.gov/criminal/criminal-icitap/icitap-historical-milestones), ICITAP **led an international advance team into Afghanistan and helped reestablish the Afghan National Police**.
- In 2009, it returned with experts specifically supporting U.S. military operations in **counternarcotics and detention/corrections**.
- Then in 2017, SIGAR recommended that ICITAP be considered and funded as the lead agency for future U.S. police-assistance operations in Afghanistan.

Now compare that mission **to the outcome.**

By 2017, Afghanistan was producing an estimated **9,000 tons of opium**, roughly **86 percent of global production**, [the highest level UNODC had recorded](https://www.unodc.org/documents/crop-monitoring/Afghanistan/Afghanistan_opium_survey_2017_peace_security_web.pdf) for the country. Only about **750 hectares** were eradicated that year out of roughly **328,000 hectares** under cultivation.

*So what exactly was being “countered”?*

[In Southeast Asia](https://archive.org/details/pdfy-MiSTDFMs_BBF8W9B/page/n1/mode/2up), he found that U.S.-backed security forces and intelligence clients became entangled with the opium economy because they were useful to the larger war.

**Plus building the pipeline that we talked about.**

Afghanistan shows the same basic pressure.

- Build the police.
- Support the military.
- Train counternarcotics units.
- Work with local power brokers.
- Fight the competition, er, **I mean Taliban**.
- Keep the government standing.

And somehow “dismantle” the opium economy woven through the same country at the same time.

*Funny how that “dismantling” never really happens.*

From **185 tons in 2001** to **9,000 tons in 2017**.

And by the time Afghan opium production reached that record, [Washington’s own watchdog](https://www.justice.gov/criminal/criminal-icitap/file/831286/dl?inline) was recommending that **ICITAP take an even larger role in U.S. police assistance there.**

And, of course, keep the drugs moving.

## **ICITAP WENT GLOBAL.**

[![](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/ade26d57-d9eb-467e-97e8-32c7654d4b60_947x709.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!ADXw!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fade26d57-d9eb-467e-97e8-32c7654d4b60_947x709.png)

Today, ICITAP barely resembles the narrow investigative-training program Congress authorized in 1986.

[DOJ now describes it](https://www.justice.gov/criminal/criminal-icitap/about-icitap) as a **“full-service criminal justice development agency”** whose mission is to strengthen U.S. national security by **“advancing U.S. law enforcement operations abroad.”** It is funded primarily by the State Department and Department of Defense and works across police, corrections, border security, counternarcotics, counterterrorism, cybercrime, digital evidence, intelligence-led policing, forensics and even strategic communications.

[In 2024 alone](https://www.justice.gov/criminal/criminal-icitap/icitap-frequently-asked-questions-faqs), ICITAP operated programs in about **80 countries**, held **871 training and mentoring events**, trained almost **25,000 foreign participants**, and conducted **278 activities with 40 other U.S. government partners**.

***THAT’S HUGE.***

ICITAP is no longer merely **teaching cops** how to dust for fingerprints. It is helping shape the **police**, **border**, **corrections**, **investigative and security institutions** of foreign governments across the world.

***And Iraq shows just how far that model can go.***

[![Exposure | The New Yorker](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/f539e69c-7de1-4eb6-8bea-ad77c860119f_1119x852.png "Exposure | The New Yorker")](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!I3Jm!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Ff539e69c-7de1-4eb6-8bea-ad77c860119f_1119x852.png)

[Beginning in 2003](https://www.justice.gov/archive/iraq/icitap.htm), ICITAP helped rebuild the Iraqi police after the U.S. invasion. It helped establish the Baghdad Police College and regional training centers, trained the Iraqi instructor corps and, through the larger coalition program, helped train **more than 260,000 Iraqi police officers**. It also helped build Iraq’s corrections system and trained anti-corruption investigators handling **thousands of cases.**

And the scale of the larger U.S. system is even bigger.

[Amnesty International](https://www.amnesty.org/es/wp-content/uploads/2021/06/ior300032003en.pdf) was already warning more than twenty years ago that the United States was training **more than 100,000 foreign police and soldiers every year across more than 150 countries**, through a sprawling network of military schools, civilian programs, contractors and overseas missions.

The document trail increasingly makes OPS look ***less*** like an extinct program and more like an ***early centralized version*** of something that later became **much MUCH larger.**

**OPS put the capability inside one office.   
The modern system distributes it.   
A network.**

## **THE GOVERNMENT PRIVATIZED IT**

ICITAP has repeatedly relied on **private contractors** to recruit, employ and deploy personnel into foreign police-development missions. Today, DOJ openly directs prospective ICITAP contractors to [Amentum](https://www.justice.gov/criminal/criminal-icitap/icitap-frequently-asked-questions-faqs), its **current prime contractor**, for overseas law-enforcement, corrections and forensic positions.

Earlier, the same role was filled by **companies like SAIC and PAE**.

And these companies hire former **military**, **intelligence** and **law-enforcement** personnel. They hold defense and intelligence contracts. They maintain security clearances and classified capabilities.

And then, under separate contracts, **they provide people for programs like ICITAP**.

The result is **a workforce** where the badge on the paycheck can tell you very little about ***whose*** mission the person is actually carrying out.

That became obvious in the [old SAIC arrangement](https://oig.justice.gov/sites/default/files/archive/special/0502/index.htm): ICITAP could recruit the individual, choose the assignment and send the name to SAIC, which would formally employ and pay the person.

The contractor layer did not necessarily replace government control. But it did give government programs **distance, flexibility and deniability of structure**.

The same problem shows up in the broader U.S. training system.

[Amnesty described](https://www.amnesty.org/es/wp-content/uploads/2021/06/ior300032003en.pdf) a **“vast and complex network”** of foreign security training and warned that **private training** could fall through gaps in vetting, transparency and oversight. The system spans **hundreds of facilities**, military programs, DOJ police programs and private contractors.

***That is a very different architecture from OPS.***

*But maybe that was the point.*

- A single office can be subpoenaed.
- A single director can be hauled before Congress.
- A single academy can be shut down.

**A network spread across State, Justice, Defense, contractors, embassies and foreign ministries is much harder to kill.**

## **WHAT DOES IT ALL MEAN**

**After six parts,** the most interesting conclusion is no longer that OPS *somehow* survived intact.

*It didn’t.*

**Something more adaptable survived.**

Congress dismantled the Office of Public Safety because one organization had become **too visible**, **too controversial** and **too entangled** with intelligence, internal security and foreign repression.

**Then the functions began reappearing somewhere else.**

- FBI training.
- DEA relationships.
- AID funding.
- State Department narcotics programs.
- Defense support.
- ICITAP.
- Private contractors.
- Embassy-based advisers.
- Regional police academies.

And **intelligence agencies** still interested in the relationships those programs created.

The names changed. The authorities changed. The money moved through different channels.

**But the underlying tools and capabilities kept rebuilding themselves.**

American strategic policy did not always ***require*** the CIA to ***directly*** run the dirty part. It needed relationships with people who could accomplish the larger objective. Once those relationships became valuable enough, drug trafficking, corruption, abusive security forces, etc could become ***secondary concerns***.

We have now seen that same logic repeat across police assistance.

- In Haiti, ICITAP helped create the national police and its specialized units, while its own director later tried to wall the program off from intelligence recruitment.
- In Afghanistan, ICITAP helped rebuild the police, then returned specifically for counternarcotics work while opium production climbed to historic levels.
- In Iraq, it helped train a police apparatus numbering in the hundreds of thousands.

And today it sits inside **a global system** operating across scores of countries, backed by State and Defense money and supported by private national-security contractors.

So perhaps the wrong question has been:

**Did OPS survive?**

The better one is:

**What happens when the functions of OPS   
are no longer contained inside anything called OPS?**

**Because once the capability is distributed**, every **individual piece** can look ordinary: a training grant, a Justice Department adviser, a Pentagon program, a State Department contract, a USAID project, a private security company, an embassy attache, a local police academy.

The network works because the pieces do not all have to know what the other pieces are doing.

And that may be **the biggest lesson** from Parts 1 through 6:

**Congress killed the organization.   
Washington preserved the network.**

**Then it learned how to distribute it.**

## **PART 7: THE NETWORK LEARNS NEW TRICKS**

Police were only one doorway.

By the twenty-first century, the same basic architecture had moved into **civil society, media, technology, social networks, NGOs, anti-corruption programs, election assistance and information operations**.

The foreign police officer was no longer the only useful relationship.

Now it could be a journalist. An activist. A prosecutor. A tech founder. An election monitor. A social-media user. A nonprofit. A “civil society partner.”

And just like OPS, the public mission could be **perfectly real** while the access created by that mission served **a much larger strategic purpose.**

Part 7, the final part of this series, follows that evolution.

**Because once Washington learned that it did not need to keep the capability inside one office, the possibilities became almost endless.**

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Did Congress actually shut down USAID’s Office of Public Safety?

**Yes.** Section 660 cut off Foreign Assistance Act funding for foreign police training, prisons, internal intelligence, and surveillance, and the International Police Academy closed. But Congress did **not** erase the FBI, DEA, Justice Department, State Department, Defense Department, or the exceptions it began carving back into the law. The result was not OPS coming back under the same name. It was the old bundle of functions being split across new legal authorities, agencies, programs, and contractors. Pasted text

### Is ICITAP basically OPS under a different name?

Not exactly—and that is what makes it more interesting. **ICITAP was legally and institutionally different from OPS**, but it rebuilt many of the same capabilities: training foreign police, creating academies, developing investigative systems, advising ministries, rebuilding national police forces, and eventually operating across scores of countries. Its structure was also deliberately divided: **AID supplied money, State supplied policy guidance, Justice operated the program, FBI supplied expertise, and contractors later supplied personnel and scale.** Pasted text

That is not a one-for-one institutional successor. It is **functional migration**.

### Did the CIA actually use these foreign-police relationships for intelligence?

The historical record shows that it did. Earlier parts of this series documented CIA liaison with OPS and the International Police Academy, participant information moving through intelligence channels, International Police Services operating as a CIA proprietary, and Project TWO-FOLD using covert recruitment inside the CIA–BNDD relationship. Pasted text

The ICITAP era raises the same pressure point again. Director **Janice Stromsem** tried to establish a rule preventing ICITAP personnel and contractors from being used by CIA, DIA, or other intelligence agencies to recruit foreign police contacts. Her concern was simple: once you have spent years finding, vetting, training, and building trust with foreign police officials, you have also created an extremely valuable intelligence-access network. Pasted text

### Did the United States really lose track of how much foreign-police assistance it was running?

Pretty much.

When GAO tried to count U.S. foreign-police assistance for 1990, it found **at least $117 million spread across 125 countries**, with at least **46 countries receiving assistance from more than one U.S. program**. GAO also warned that the real number was higher because agencies did not consistently report training funded from their own budgets, police and military trainees were not always separated, and different agencies could count the same trainees twice. Pasted text

So by the early 1990s, the United States had rebuilt a massive foreign-police system—but there was no single office, no single ledger, and no single official who could tell you exactly how big it was.
